# Sweep 006: Contra Costa County Public Libraries

## Scope

This sweep uses the Contra Costa County Library (CCCL) system as the first real facility-category test of the Registry methodology.

The sweep is intentionally system-level. It does not assign a Registry status to the County Library system or any individual branch. It asks which rules apply across the system, which spaces require branch-specific research, and what controlling or persuasive law governs peaceful First Amendment activity in those spaces.

The standing factual baseline is maintained in docs/california/standing-scenario.md. The facility workflow is maintained in docs/california/facility-investigation-methodology.md.

### Core research question

For an ordinary member of the public peacefully recording, observing, receiving information, or engaging in expression at a Contra Costa County public library, how do the governing rules change with the exact area, activity, access condition, and policy invoked?

## Source hierarchy used

Priority was given to:

1. U.S. Supreme Court and published Ninth Circuit authority;
2. current Contra Costa County Library policies and Board of Supervisors records;
3. California statutes and official California library-law compilations;
4. persuasive published federal appellate decisions involving public libraries;
5. California federal district-court decisions as persuasive/contextual authority.

No incident video, viral clip, or unofficial characterization is treated as legal authority.

# II. The library is not one constitutional forum

## Controlling Ninth Circuit anchor: Faith Center Church Evangelistic Ministries v. Glover

Authority: Faith Center Church Evangelistic Ministries v. Glover, 480 F.3d 891 (9th Cir. 2007), amended published opinion.

Official Ninth Circuit opinion:

https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2007/03/09/0516132.pdf

### Narrow retained proposition

The Ninth Circuit classified the Antioch Library meeting room, under the policy and practices then before it, as a limited public forum.

The court examined the specific access sought, the meeting-room policy, permitted speakers and subjects, application/approval requirements, fee conditions, excluded uses, and governmental practice. It expressly rejected reliance on other County meeting rooms because the relevant forum was defined by the access sought and, in that case, was the Antioch Library meeting room.

This is controlling support for the project's area-by-area method.

### What Faith Center does not establish

It does not hold that:

- every Contra Costa library interior is a limited public forum;
- every room in every branch has the same classification;
- a public library as a whole has one forum classification;
- photography or videography receives the same analysis as meeting-room speech;
- a policy violation automatically authorizes removal, detention, citation, or arrest.

The forum must still be defined by the particular access and activity at issue.

## Meeting-room litigation history

The 2007 Ninth Circuit decision addressed the County's then-current exclusion of religious worship services from the Antioch meeting room at the preliminary-injunction stage.

On remand, the district court later held the religious-service restriction unconstitutional under then-prevailing Establishment Clause doctrine in Faith Center Church Evangelistic Ministries v. Glover, No. C 04-3111 JSW, 2009 WL 1765974 (N.D. Cal. June 19, 2009).

The current CCCL meeting-room policy PDF itself records the policy change: the RELIGIOUS USE heading and the sentence barring religious services are struck through and marked effective July 6, 2009.

Current policy/rules PDF:

https://ccclib.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/72/2026/03/LP-RMS-MeetingRoomRules_2025E_ADA.pdf

Important current-law caveat: the 2009 district court used the then-prevailing Lemon/entanglement framework. The Supreme Court later stated in Kennedy v. Bremerton School District, 597 U.S. 507 (2022), that it had abandoned Lemon and its endorsement-test offshoot.

Official Supreme Court opinion:

https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/21pdf/21-418diff_k4l8.pdf

Accordingly:

- the 2007 Ninth Circuit forum classification remains useful controlling authority for the historical Antioch meeting-room forum and for forum methodology;
- the 2009 district-court Establishment Clause reasoning is historical and is not carried forward as a current general Establishment Clause test;
- current County policy, not the superseded religious-use language, controls present facility research.

# III. California library-exterior anchor: Prigmore v. City of Redding

**Authority:** *Prigmore v. City of Redding*, 211 Cal.App.4th 1322, 150 Cal.Rptr.3d 647 (2012), published Third District Court of Appeal.

Source:

https://law.justia.com/cases/california/court-of-appeal/2012/c068866.html

The case involved leafleting on outdoor grounds of the Redding Municipal Library.

### Narrow retained propositions

- The forum was defined according to the **access sought** rather than the entire library campus.
- On the record before the court, the relevant forum consisted of the public open space on the entry side, the entry/exit-door area, and the adjacent parking lot.
- Those outdoor areas were treated as a **traditional public forum** based on their use, unrestricted public access, physical characteristics, congregation features, relationship to nearby public spaces, and the library's function.
- The court distinguished outdoor areas from the interior.
- The court declined to adopt the older California "basic incompatibility" formulation as the classification rule.
- Most challenged restrictions in Redding's outdoor policy were likely invalid at the preliminary-injunction stage, while the vehicle-leafleting ban in the parking lot was upheld on the safety rationale before the court.

### Scope caution

*Prigmore* does not establish that every California library exterior or parking lot is automatically a traditional public forum. Property ownership/control, actual use, access, boundaries, physical characteristics, history, and the expressive activity still matter.

Footnote 11 stated broadly that federal courts had treated library interiors as limited public forums, citing *Faith Center* and *Kreimer*. This project does not use that footnote as a categorical interior holding because *Faith Center* itself defined the forum as the Antioch meeting room. The ordinary interior remains separately analyzed.

### Sweep consequence

Contra Costa branch research should separately classify:

- ordinary municipal/public sidewalks;
- library-controlled entry plazas or forecourts;
- doorway/approach areas;
- parking lots;
- interior public-service/reading spaces; and
- intentionally opened rooms/channels.

**Status:** DOCTRINE-REVIEWED for the narrow Redding exterior proposition.

# IV. Main library floors and ordinary patron access

No published Ninth Circuit decision located in this sweep categorically classifies the ordinary reading, stacks, service, or circulation areas of a California public library for every expressive activity.

That gap matters.

## Persuasive authority: Kreimer

Authority: Kreimer v. Bureau of Police for Town of Morristown, 958 F.2d 1242 (3d Cir. 1992).

Source:

https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F2/958/1242/371694/

The Third Circuit treated the public library before it as a limited public forum for First Amendment activity compatible with its purpose, emphasizing acquisition of knowledge through reading, writing, and quiet contemplation.

Of special relevance, the patron rule upheld in Kreimer required patrons to be engaged in activities associated with public-library use while in the building.

## Contra Costa's current activity requirement

CCCL's 2026 Patron Conduct Policy states that patrons must be engaged in activities associated with the use of a public library. Its examples include reading, studying, quiet conversation, working, using library materials/equipment, and participating in a library program/activity. The policy states that patrons not engaged in such activities may be required to leave.

That language closely resembles the rule examined in Kreimer, but Kreimer is Third Circuit authority and therefore persuasive, not controlling, in California.

### Recording/observation question

The current Patron Conduct Policy does not expressly say whether peaceful observation, photography, videography, documentation of public officials performing public duties, or gathering information for later speech qualifies as an activity associated with the use of a public library.

The public-source sweep did not locate a dedicated current public-facing CCCL photography/videography policy on the policy portal or in the current Patron Conduct Policy.

Research status: AUTHORITY-UNRESOLVED.

This is not a conclusion that recording is permitted or forbidden. It means the public-facing policy materials reviewed in this sweep do not answer the question directly.

## Persuasive circuit disagreement cautions against a blanket label

Other circuits have used differing forum terminology for public libraries.

- Kreimer, Third Circuit, described the library before it as a limited public forum for compatible activity.
- Doe v. City of Albuquerque, 667 F.3d 1111 (10th Cir. 2012), in a challenge to a citywide ban on registered sex offenders entering public libraries, treated the libraries there as designated public forums and recognized the First Amendment interest in receiving information.

Source for Doe:

https://www.leagle.com/decision/infco20120120059

These decisions are persuasive only here. Their differing terminology reinforces the project rule: do not import another court's building-wide label without analyzing the California facility, specific area, governmental intent, policy, practice, and activity.

# V. Patron-conduct rules relevant to peaceful audits

The current Patron Conduct Policy contains several provisions likely to appear in a peaceful recording or observation encounter.

## Interference and disruption

The policy states that conduct interfering with another person's ability or right to use and benefit from library facilities, disrupting another person's use, or disrupting staff duties can lead to removal or suspension.

It separately addresses fighting or seriously disruptive behavior, threats/intimidation, harassment/stalking, blocking aisles/exits/entrances, loud talking/yelling/profanity, disturbing other patrons or staff, and misuse of designated spaces.

For standing-scenario analysis, those predicates are not silently assumed merely because someone objects to a camera or becomes uncomfortable.

A facility entry should document the actual conduct and actual effect.

## Distress, alarm, and harassment language

The policy contains reasonable-person language concerning actions or words that cause fear, distress, alarm, or harassment, and it also addresses conduct such as persistent staring or gestures.

That language must be applied to actual facts. It does not independently answer whether peaceful filming from a lawful position is prohibited.

The project's disorderly-conduct, trespass, and standing-scenario chapters remain controlling for California statutory analysis.

## Policy-compliance clause

The policy also addresses failure to comply with a staff request to stop disruptive behavior, the Patron Conduct Policy, or another library policy.

A staff request and a criminal-law command are not interchangeable.

If a later enforcement event occurs, the facility file must separately identify:

1. the policy rule;
2. who has authority to issue an exclusion or removal order;
3. what property or area the order covers;
4. the California statute, if any, relied on for detention, citation, or arrest; and
5. the conduct alleged to satisfy that statute.

# VI. Children and teen areas

## Project field limitation

Independent of the legal analysis below, the project will not intentionally enter or remain in a specifically designated children's area for an audit and will not intentionally position or aim a camera to record into such an area. Children and their activities are not audit targets.

This is a methodological choice, not a statement that public presence or recording in every such area would be unlawful. Branch research may still document the location, signage, boundaries, and governing policy from ordinary public circulation areas.


Current CCCL policy:

https://ccclib.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/72/2026/03/LP-Children-Teen-Area-Policy_ADA.pdf

The Board-approved policy reserves children's areas primarily for children age 11 and under, parents/guardians, and patrons using the children's collection at staff discretion. Teen areas are reserved primarily for patrons age 12-18, persons accompanying them, and patrons using the teen collection at staff discretion.

Unaccompanied adults may be directed to adult areas.

## Field significance

This is a concrete area-specific access condition.

An ordinary public library can therefore contain general public reading/service areas, age/purpose-designated areas, reservable study rooms, reservable meeting rooms, staff/restricted areas, and exterior areas under the same roof or at the same address.

The presence of minors may support legitimate privacy and safety interests, but this policy does not itself create a general photography/videography prohibition.

# VII. Meeting and study rooms

CCCL's current rooms directory:

https://ccclib.org/rooms/

Current meeting-room policy/rules:

https://ccclib.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/72/2026/03/LP-RMS-MeetingRoomRules_2025E_ADA.pdf

## System-level rule, branch-level implementation

The system encourages meeting-room use for educational, cultural, and community-related meetings, programs, and activities.

The current rooms directory also shows important branch-specific differences:

- some rooms are reserved directly through CCCL;
- some community rooms are booked or controlled by a city or town;
- room capacities, eligibility, fees, hours, and reservation rules vary;
- some rooms have specialized local rules.

Examples include rooms whose booking is handled by the City of Brentwood, City of Clayton, Town of Danville, City of San Pablo, and other local entities.

## Registry consequence

A branch entry must not assume that the County controls every room merely because the room is inside or associated with a County Library branch.

For each room, determine:

- owner/controller;
- booking authority;
- governing policy;
- permitted purposes/speakers;
- whether the room is open during or outside ordinary branch hours;
- whether County patron rules remain applicable; and
- exact forum classification.

Faith Center provides controlling precedent for the historical Antioch meeting room on its record, not automatic classification of every room currently listed in the system.

# VIII. Exhibits, displays, and non-library materials

Current policy portal:

https://ccclib.org/policies/

Current rules for display/distribution of non-library materials:

https://ccclib.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/72/2024/10/LP-rulesforexhibitsdisplay-October2024E.pdf

The rules permit non-library informational materials subject to authorization, available space, and subject/use restrictions. They prohibit material used for religious services and instructions on how to vote for a candidate or political party, while allowing announcements of political meetings. The rules also permit branch-specific rules tied to local needs and conditions.

## Research posture

These display/distribution channels appear intentionally opened for specified speakers and subjects and therefore require a channel-specific forum analysis.

This sweep does not decide whether the political or religious restrictions are constitutional.

Reasons for further review include:

- the Library affirmatively opens a distribution/display channel to non-library materials;
- the restrictions are expressly subject-matter based;
- the religious language must be reconciled with current limited-forum doctrine and with the separate meeting-room litigation history;
- political-party/candidate restrictions require their own limited-forum reasonableness and viewpoint-neutrality analysis.

Research status: AUTHORITY-UNRESOLVED.

Because these restrictions involve political expression, later legal analysis should evaluate the rule neutrally rather than favoring or opposing any party, candidate, or viewpoint.

# IX. Patron-record privacy is not a camera statute

CCCL privacy page:

https://ccclib.org/privacy/

The Library identifies California Government Code section 7927.105 as protecting registration and patron-use records, including personally identifiable borrowing or resource-use information.

The California State Library's library-law compilation is also a source for library-specific public-record provisions:

https://www.library.ca.gov/uploads/2025/01/California-Library-Laws-2025.pdf

## Important distinction

Library-record confidentiality and visitor recording are different legal questions.

Government Code section 7927.105 concerns disclosure and confidentiality of qualifying library-maintained patron-use records. It should not be cited as though its text creates a categorical prohibition on a member of the public incidentally photographing or recording another person in a library.

Separate doctrines may still matter, including California constitutional privacy, confidential-communications law, harassment/stalking law, facility-specific policy, restrictions tailored to children or sensitive areas, disruption/interference, and tort/privacy law.

Those issues require their own elements.

# X. Suspension, exclusion, and due process

Current Suspension Policy:

https://ccclib.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/72/2026/03/Suspension-Policy_ADA.pdf

The policy creates escalating suspension categories ranging from one day through suspensions longer than one year.

For severe or repeat misconduct, the policy provides for written notice identifying the reason and duration and can suspend access to all Contra Costa County Library facilities.

## Appeals

The current policy provides:

- written appeal to the County Librarian for suspensions longer than one week and up to one year, with a written response within seven days;
- a review-panel process for suspensions longer than one year;
- no stated formal appeal process in the published policy for suspensions of one week or less.

## Persuasive due-process authority

### Neinast v. Board of Trustees of Columbus Metropolitan Library

Authority: 346 F.3d 585 (6th Cir. 2003), persuasive only.

Source:

https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/346/585/510700/

The Sixth Circuit upheld the process used for a one-day library eviction on its facts where the patron received notice of the charge and an opportunity to present his side. The court did not prescribe one universal procedure for all library exclusions.

### Van Den Heuvel v. Dorothy

An Eastern District of California screening decision stated that a weeks-to-month countywide library suspension could plausibly implicate a liberty interest while leaving the ultimate issue for later proceedings.

Source:

https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/district-courts/california/caedce/2%3A2021cv02176/403475/24/

This is nonbinding district-court authority.

## Current conclusion

The exact constitutional process required for immediate ejection, one-day suspension, one-week suspension, multiweek or multimonth suspension, and systemwide long-term exclusion has not been resolved by a published controlling Ninth Circuit or California appellate library case located in this sweep.

Research status: AUTHORITY-UNRESOLVED.

A later chapter should apply ordinary procedural-due-process doctrine and any additional California law to the duration, scope, factual basis, notice, urgency, opportunity to respond, and appeal mechanism.

# XI. Suspension policy and California trespass

The Suspension Policy states that a suspended person who returns before the listed return date will be asked to leave; if the person refuses, law enforcement will be contacted. It further states that the person is subject to arrest for trespass under California Penal Code section 602.

That policy statement does not identify a subsection of section 602 or walk through statutory elements.

Sweep 002 already established that California trespass provisions are element-specific and area/access-specific.

## Research consequence

For an actual enforcement event:

- identify the exact Penal Code subsection;
- determine whether its property/access predicates apply;
- distinguish a valid administrative suspension or removal order from an independent criminal offense;
- do not treat the policy's generic citation to section 602 as completing the criminal-law analysis.

Research flag: POLICY-TO-CRIMINAL-AUTHORITY-GAP.

The flag does not mean arrest would necessarily be unlawful. It means the public-facing policy does not itself supply the complete statutory bridge.

# XII. Photography and videography: current system finding

As of September 21, 2026, this sweep reviewed:

- CCCL's public policy index;
- the current Patron Conduct Policy;
- the current Suspension Policy;
- children/teen-area policy;
- meeting-room policy/rules;
- exhibits/display rules; and
- targeted searches of the CCCL public website.

No dedicated current public-facing CCCL policy specifically governing ordinary patron photography or videography inside library facilities was located.

## What follows

Nothing categorical yet.

A future branch investigation should ask:

1. Is there an unpublished/internal or branch-specific photography policy?
2. Are there posted camera/recording signs?
3. What exact area is involved?
4. What activity is being recorded?
5. Is the claimed restriction based on privacy, disruption, program licensing, minors, staff safety, facility control, or another rationale?
6. Who adopted the rule?
7. What is the exclusion/removal authority?
8. If police enforcement follows, what independent offense is claimed?

The constitutional analysis must then combine the project's recording, forum, trespass/removal, privacy, and police-encounter chapters.

Do not use: "There is no photography policy."

Use: "No dedicated public-facing photography/videography policy was located in the sources reviewed as of September 21, 2026."

# XIV. Sweep 006 field rules

## Field rule: define the library area

Do not say: "A public library is a public forum."

Use: Identify the exact area and activity. A meeting room intentionally opened for community meetings can have a different forum analysis from stacks, a children's area, a study room, a staff office, or an exterior sidewalk.

## Field rule: library purpose matters, but does not answer every activity

Libraries may adopt reasonable rules preserving reading, study, information access, safety, and other intended uses. Whether a particular recording or expressive restriction is valid still requires the actual rule, area, activity, purpose, and governing doctrine.

## Field rule: "library activity" is a real policy predicate

CCCL can point to an adopted rule requiring patrons to engage in activities associated with public-library use. Whether a peaceful auditor's particular information-gathering activity satisfies, or can constitutionally be excluded under, that rule remains a separate question.

## Field rule: protect the policy/removal/crime distinction

A patron-conduct violation, staff instruction, suspension notice, removal authority, and California criminal trespass are separate steps. Identify the authority for each.

## Field rule: patron-record privacy is not camera law

Patron-record confidentiality protects qualifying library-maintained records. Do not transform it into a categorical visitor-recording prohibition without separate authority.

# XVIII. Sweep conclusion

Contra Costa is a strong first library system because it demonstrates why the Registry must be system → branch → area/activity rather than one facility label.

The current record establishes:

- Board-approved systemwide patron and suspension rules;
- age/purpose-designated spaces;
- intentionally opened meeting and display channels;
- branch-specific room-control differences;
- a controlling Ninth Circuit case involving one CCCL meeting room;
- a current "library activity" requirement with a close persuasive analogue in Kreimer;
- no dedicated public-facing recording policy located in the sources reviewed;
- an administrative suspension scheme with duration-dependent appeals; and
- a generic Penal Code section 602 enforcement statement that still requires a subsection/element bridge before criminal analysis.

The next phase should apply this legal/system spine to one branch at a time without assuming that the answer for one room or branch determines another.
