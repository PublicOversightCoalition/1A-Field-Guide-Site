# Out-of-State Case Study: Reyes / Fahey (Connecticut)

> **ILLUSTRATIVE ONLY: NOT CALIFORNIA AUTHORITY**

**Status:** Open research note; primary sentencing/protective-order documents pending  
**Last reviewed:** September 24, 2026

## Why this case is retained

This case is useful to the California Field Guide only as an institutional-process illustration.

It should **not** be cited as authority for California forum classification, California trespass law, California protective-order law, or California police powers.

The useful question is narrower:

> How can one continuous event generate several different official narratives, charges, later corrections, and collateral consequences even where objective video exists?

That makes the matter a useful example for the Field Guide's work on:

- subjective complaint conversion;
- allegation-to-elements analysis;
- narrative cascade;
- objective video and evidence preservation;
- separating parallel misconduct from predicate-destroying evidence;
- historical enforcement records; and
- the difference between an initial accusation, a charging decision, an adjudicated offense, and later collateral orders.

## Analytical lesson 1: one event can contain separate legal acts

The video may strongly prove misconduct by one participant without necessarily negating a separately completed offense by another participant.

For this reason, distinguish:

### Parallel misconduct

Evidence proves another actor committed separate wrongful conduct, but does not itself negate every element of the charge against the auditor.

Example structure:

> disputed initial entry → order to leave → auditor begins leaving → homeowner later advances armed / spits

Evidence of the later conduct can be highly important while the court still separately decides whether the earlier entry already satisfied a trespass statute.

### Predicate-destroying evidence

Evidence directly contradicts the factual proposition being used to justify the seizure, removal, charge, or conviction itself.

Example structure:

> complainant says auditor followed and threatened them → continuous video shows complainant initiated contact and followed the auditor while no threat occurred

That is a different evidentiary problem because the recording goes directly to the asserted predicate.

The California Field Guide should preserve this distinction.

## Analytical lesson 2: narrative cascade

One possible institutional failure mode is:

> complainant characterization → dispatch summary → responding-officer framing → report language → charging language → later official repetition

The existence of a cascade does not prove corruption or intentional falsification.

The research question is whether later actors independently verified the underlying facts or simply inherited conclusions such as "harassing," "disruptive," "trespassing," or "threatening."

For a California facility incident, preserve the chain separately:

1. original complainant words;
2. CAD/dispatch characterization;
3. officer observations;
4. body-camera statements;
5. incident/arrest report;
6. charging document;
7. prosecutor characterization;
8. judicial findings.

Do not silently treat those eight items as independent corroboration if they all derive from one original assertion.

## Analytical lesson 3: objective video does not answer every legal question

Video can establish facts with exceptional clarity but legal questions remain issue-specific.

In this case, video evidence could establish whether Reyes was leaving when Fahey approached, whether Fahey had a firearm visible, whether Fahey advanced, and whether the spit occurred.

It does not, by itself, resolve the separate legal question whether Reyes knowingly lacked license or privilege **when he first entered** the driveway.

The Field Guide should therefore ask:

> What proposition does this piece of evidence actually prove or disprove?

rather than:

> Who does the video make look worse overall?

## Analytical lesson 4: third-party audience conduct requires attribution discipline

At sentencing, reporting says Fahey family members described threats received from some of Reyes's followers and the effect of repeated videos concerning the family.

Those facts can be relevant to a sentencing/protective-order record, but the Field Guide should preserve an attribution distinction:

- conduct by the auditor;
- speech by the auditor;
- speech or threats by third parties;
- conduct the auditor directed, solicited, encouraged, or knowingly facilitated;
- conduct by third parties with no established attribution to the auditor.

Do not collapse "followers threatened us" into "the speaker threatened us" without evidence satisfying the relevant legal standard.

## Analytical lesson 5: collateral consequences can dwarf the triggering offense

The reported sentence for the Class C misdemeanor was a suspended three-month term plus probation, while the four standing criminal protective orders reportedly extend through 2099.

That makes the orders independently important to understand.

The project should obtain:

1. all four standing criminal protective orders;
2. the September 3, 2026 sentencing transcript;
3. the judge's express good-cause findings, if stated;
4. the exact restrictions imposed;
5. any filed notice of appeal and official appellate case number;
6. appellate briefs, if an appeal is confirmed and briefs are filed; and
7. any later order modifying, staying, or reviewing the protective orders.

## California Field Guide takeaway

Retain only these portable lessons:

- legal labels must be decomposed into observable facts and elements;
- objective evidence must be tied to the proposition it actually proves;
- separate misconduct in one continuous event must not be conflated;
- a complaint can acquire institutional momentum unless each stage re-anchors to evidence;
- third-party conduct should not be attributed to a speaker without an evidentiary/legal basis;
- collateral administrative or judicial restraints may require their own legal analysis; and
- later records can materially alter the understanding of the initial encounter.

Do **not** import Connecticut trespass, protective-order, or appellate doctrine into the California chapters.
