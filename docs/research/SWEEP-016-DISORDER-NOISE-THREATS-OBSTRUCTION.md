# Sweep 016: Disorder, Unreasonable Noise, Fighting Words, Threats, and Obstruction

Qualification targets:

- `CA-F6.1`, disturbance / noise;
- `CA-F6.2`, obstruction / interference;
- `CA-F6.3`, fighting words / threats / analogous speech offenses.

This sweep addresses a recurring public-facility pattern:

> peaceful recording or questioning → employee/patron objects → activity is relabeled "disruptive," "harassing," "hostile," "aggressive," or "interfering" → police are asked to remove/arrest → Penal Code § 415, § 148, § 602.1, or a threat theory is invoked

The central project rule is:

> **Translate the characterization into observable conduct and the elements of an identified law.**

"Disruptive," "harassing," "aggressive," "hostile," and "causing a disturbance" are not self-executing California crimes.

Operational sequence:

> **actual conduct → exact offense/subsection → speech/conduct distinction → constitutional narrowing rule → actual interference/danger/effect → changed facts**

This sweep does not treat the standing peaceful scenario as a defense to conduct that actually changes. If a person later threatens violence, blocks operations, uses sound as a guise to disrupt, physically interferes, or substantially delays a lawful police duty, rerun the branch on those facts.

---

# 1. Penal Code § 415 has three separate offenses

Current primary source:

https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=PEN&sectionNum=415

Section 415 separately covers:

1. unlawful fighting in public or challenging another person in public to fight;
2. maliciously and willfully disturbing another person by **loud and unreasonable noise**; and
3. using offensive words in public that are **inherently likely to provoke an immediate violent reaction**.

A report that someone is "disturbing the peace" is therefore incomplete. Identify the subsection and the conduct.

The 2026 Judicial Council instructions remain consistent with the controlling narrowing cases:

- CALCRIM 2688, fighting/challenge to fight;
- CALCRIM 2689, loud and unreasonable noise;
- CALCRIM 2690, offensive words.

Current Judicial Council instruction index:
https://www.justia.com/criminal/docs/calcrim/2600/

# 2. § 415(2): "loud" is not enough

## In re Brown, 9 Cal.3d 612 (1973)

Status: California Supreme Court; controlling state authority.

*In re Brown* rejected a construction of § 415 that would criminalize all loud public speech merely because someone is disturbed by it.

The court distinguished communicative shouting from volume used as disruptive conduct. For loud shouting/cheering used as communication, § 415's noise branch is constitutionally narrowed to two circumstances:

1. a clear and present danger of imminent violence; or
2. purported communication used as a **guise to disrupt lawful endeavors** rather than a genuine effort to communicate.

Source:
https://law.justia.com/cases/california/supreme-court/3d/9/612.html

The current CALCRIM 2689 instruction states the same two alternatives.

Source:
https://www.justia.com/criminal/docs/calcrim/2600/2689/

## Field rule

> **A raised voice, chanting, criticism, or loud advocacy is not automatically § 415(2). Ask whether the sound created the constitutionally narrowed danger/disruption condition.**

This is not a universal right to yell anywhere. *Brown* expressly recognizes that precisely drafted, content-neutral time/place/manner rules can regulate volume in appropriate places. A hospital quiet-zone rule, meeting rule, school rule, ordinary workplace operational limit, or valid facility restriction must be analyzed under its own source and constitutional test.

The point is narrower: the generic statewide § 415(2) offense cannot be used as a catch-all "you were loud and somebody complained" statute.

# 3. A changed-fact noise comparator: People v. Superior Court (Commons)

## People v. Superior Court (Commons), 135 Cal.App.3d 812 (1982)

Status: published California Court of Appeal.

*Commons* applied *In re Brown* in a materially different setting. The person was laughing and shouting very loudly in a hotel hallway around 1 a.m.; residents came out into the hall, and the court concluded the facts supported probable cause under the "guise to disrupt" branch.

Source:
https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/ca-court-of-appeal/1839130.html

Project use:

> *Brown* protects loud communication from being criminalized merely because it annoys or disturbs. *Commons* shows that context, communicative purpose, place, hour, and actual disruptive use of volume can change the result.

Do not turn either case into a categorical volume rule.

# 4. § 415(3): profanity is not automatically "fighting words"

## Jefferson v. Superior Court, 51 Cal.App.3d 721 (1975)

Status: published California Court of Appeal.

*Jefferson* rejected probable cause under § 415(3) where an officer heard a person use a vulgar epithet but did not know to whom the words were directed.

The court emphasized:

- profanity or a vulgar epithet alone is insufficient;
- context matters;
- the words must be uttered in a provocative manner; and
- there must be a clear and present danger that violence will immediately erupt.

Source:
https://law.justia.com/cases/california/court-of-appeal/3d/51/721.html

## In re John V., 167 Cal.App.3d 761 (1985)

Status: published California Court of Appeal.

*John V.* confirms that § 415(3) is the fighting-words branch and that the inquiry is fact/context specific. The history of antagonism, proximity, manner of delivery, and actual circumstances can matter.

Source:
https://law.justia.com/cases/california/court-of-appeal/3d/167/761.html

## CALCRIM 2690

The current Judicial Council instruction requires:

- offensive words inherently likely to provoke an immediate violent reaction; and
- a clear and present danger that the other person will immediately erupt into violence.

It directs the factfinder to consider all circumstances and the person to whom the statement was addressed.

Source:
https://www.justia.com/criminal/docs/calcrim/2600/2690/

### Field rule

> **"He swore at me" and "he insulted me" do not establish § 415(3). Identify the words, addressee, manner, distance, surrounding conduct, and immediate violence risk.**

# 5. Speech directed at police receives special contextual protection

## City of Houston v. Hill, 482 U.S. 451 (1987)

Status: U.S. Supreme Court.

*Hill* invalidated an ordinance that broadly criminalized verbal interruption/challenge to police. The Court held that the First Amendment protects a significant amount of verbal criticism and challenge directed at officers.

Source:
https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/482/451/

## Duran v. City of Douglas, 904 F.2d 1372 (9th Cir. 1990)

Status: Ninth Circuit, published/binding in California.

A person directed obscene gestures and expletives at an officer. The Ninth Circuit held the speech did not supply lawful grounds for the detention/arrest theory asserted there. Criticism of police, including crass criticism, is protected unless independent unlawful conduct supplies authority.

Source:
https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F2/904/1372/388209/

## United States v. Poocha, 259 F.3d 1077 (9th Cir. 2001)

Status: Ninth Circuit, published/binding.

*Poocha* applied the same rule to profanity directed at a National Park Service officer. Yelling an obscenity, even accompanied by aggressive posture such as clenched fists and puffed chest on those facts, did not turn the expression into fighting words.

Source:
https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/259/1077/575656/

## In re Alejandro G., 37 Cal.App.4th 44 (1995)

Status: published California Court of Appeal.

California does **not** adopt a categorical rule that words addressed to a police officer can never be fighting words. Instead, officer status is part of the totality.

A trained officer is expected to exercise greater restraint than an average citizen. But in *Alejandro G.*, the speaker went beyond vulgar criticism and expressly challenged the officer to fight, accompanied by threats of actual violence. The court held those facts could satisfy § 415(3).

Source:
https://law.justia.com/cases/california/court-of-appeal/4th/37/44.html

### Project synthesis

> **Police criticism/profanity is ordinarily protected; officer status makes immediate violent reaction less readily inferred, not legally impossible. An actual challenge to fight or genuine threat of violence can change the branch.**

Do not use "fighting words" as a synonym for words an officer strongly dislikes.

# 6. Audience anger does not automatically authorize suppression

## Center for Bio-Ethical Reform, Inc. v. Los Angeles County Sheriff's Department, 533 F.3d 780 (9th Cir. 2008)

Status: Ninth Circuit, published/binding.

The Ninth Circuit held in the school-perimeter protest context that a speech restriction based on the audience's reaction to the content is content based. Listener hostility does not become a content-neutral justification merely because officials characterize the reaction as disruption.

Source:
https://openjurist.org/533/f3d/780/center-for-bio-ethical-reform-inc-v-los-angeles-county-sheriff-department

The underlying case also involved school-specific safety/access law. Do not generalize its exact result to every government interior.

## Field rule

> **A patron saying "I don't want to be recorded," becoming angry, complaining to staff, or demanding removal is evidence of the encounter. It does not itself transform otherwise protected speech into disorderly conduct.**

Government may address actual threats, obstruction, unlawful noise, or other independent misconduct. The project should identify that misconduct rather than making listener hostility the missing element.

# 7. Penal Code § 148: verbal challenge is not the same as obstruction

Current § 148(a)(1) punishes willful resistance, delay, or obstruction of a public/peace officer or EMT in the discharge or attempted discharge of a duty when no other punishment is prescribed.

Current primary source:
https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=PEN&sectionNum=148

A § 148 conviction also requires the officer to be acting lawfully in the relevant duty.

## People v. Quiroga, 16 Cal.App.4th 961 (1993)

Status: published California Court of Appeal.

*Quiroga* holds that § 148 does not criminalize mere failure to respond to police orders "with alacrity." The person had a First Amendment right to dispute the officer's actions verbally.

Source:
https://law.justia.com/cases/california/court-of-appeal/4th/16/961.html

## Mackinney v. Nielsen, 69 F.3d 1002 (9th Cir. 1995)

Status: Ninth Circuit, published/binding.

*Mackinney* applied California § 148 and federal First Amendment doctrine. A few seconds of delayed compliance and subsequent verbal protest did not create probable cause for § 148. The Ninth Circuit treated verbal challenge to police as clearly protected.

Source:
https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/69/1002/609814/

### Field rule

> **Questioning an officer, disputing legal authority, criticizing police conduct, or failing to comply instantaneously is not by itself § 148.**

This is not a license to physically resist, flee a lawful detention, or substantially impede an officer's lawful task.

---

# 8. Conduct can cross the § 148 line without an assault

## In re Muhammed C., 95 Cal.App.4th 1325 (2002)

Status: published California Court of Appeal.

The minor repeatedly remained next to a patrol car and continued interacting with an arrestee despite five orders to step away. Officers had to interrupt their processing activity to address him. The court upheld the finding that the conduct caused actual delay.

Source:
https://law.justia.com/cases/california/court-of-appeal/4th/95/1325.html

## In re N.S., G065591 (Cal. Ct. App. Sept. 8, 2026)

Status: published California Court of Appeal; current authority.

*N.S.* is school-specific as to the lawfulness of the underlying search, but its § 148 comparison is useful and current. The juvenile:

- physically left the location where the lawful search was to occur;
- required the officer to pursue him;
- repeatedly refused five commands; and
- delayed the weapon investigation about ten minutes.

The Court of Appeal distinguished *Quiroga*: this was more than slow compliance or verbal criticism because the conduct substantially interfered with and delayed a lawful investigation.

Source:
https://law.justia.com/cases/california/court-of-appeal/2026/g065591.html

### Project rule

The ordinary line is not:

> speech = protected; physical contact = obstruction.

The better rule is:

> **protected verbal challenge does not become § 148 merely because it is annoying or inconvenient, but conduct, including conduct accompanied by words, can violate § 148 when it actually resists, delays, or obstructs an officer's lawful performance.**

Always preserve the lawfulness-of-officer-duty element.

# 9. Public-agency interference: § 602.1(b) requires actual statutory predicates

Current primary source:
https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=PEN&sectionNum=602.1

For a public agency open to the public, § 602.1(b) requires:

1. intentional interference with lawful public-agency business;
2. **by obstructing or intimidating** employees carrying on business or people there to transact business;
3. a qualifying request to leave from the manager/supervisor or a peace officer acting at that person's request; and
4. refusal.

Section 602.1(d)(2) says the section does not apply to constitutionally protected activity.

## Dubner v. City & County of San Francisco, 266 F.3d 959 (9th Cir. 2001)

Status: Ninth Circuit, published.

*Dubner* concerned the related business-establishment form of § 602.1 then in force. It held that refusal to leave did **not** itself establish the missing intentional interference. Photographing/observing from away from the entrance did not supply obstruction/intimidation.

Source:
https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/266/959/582256/

## People v. Turner, A147603 (Cal. Ct. App. 2017)

Status: published California Court of Appeal; § 602.1(a) private-business application.

*Turner* expressly relied on *Dubner* and again treated refusal alone as insufficient. On Turner's different facts, however, multiple requests over hours, repeated interruption of employees' duties, police calls/paperwork, and related conduct were sufficient for probable cause of interference.

Source:
https://law.justia.com/cases/california/court-of-appeal/2017/a147603.html

## Mackey v. Meyer, 675 F. App'x 705 (9th Cir. 2017)

Status: Ninth Circuit memorandum; **unpublished/nonprecedential**.

*Mackey* is the direct California DMV comparator. Dispatch characterized a preacher as "harassing" customers, but video/audio did not show obstruction or intimidation. The panel held the officer lacked probable cause under § 602.1(b).

Source:
https://openjurist.org/675/fappx/705/mark-mackey-v-darren-meyer-4337855

Project use: direct factual cross-check only; do not elevate it over published authority.

### Field rule

> **"Harassing customers" is not itself a § 602.1(b) element. Ask what the person did that allegedly obstructed or intimidated agency employees or members of the public transacting business. Refusal to leave does not manufacture that missing predicate.**

Sweep 012 remains the primary derivative-trespass chapter. Sweep 016 adds this obstruction/interference layer.

# 10. "Harassment" is not one generic California public-facility crime

A person can commit specifically defined offenses that use concepts such as harassment, stalking, threats, repeated following, intimidation, or interference.

But a facility employee's statement:

> "He's harassing people"

does not identify which law has been violated.

For example, Penal Code § 646.9 stalking has defined course-of-conduct / following / credible-threat elements and statutory exclusions for constitutionally protected activity. A one-time complaint that a lawful recorder made someone uncomfortable does not silently become those elements.

Primary source:
https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=PEN&sectionNum=646.9

Field rule:

> **Ask for the conduct and the statute/rule. Do not analyze the adjective as though it were an offense.**

---

# 11. Fighting words and true threats are different doctrines

Section 415(3) focuses on words inherently likely, in context, to provoke an **immediate violent reaction by the listener**.

A **true threat** doctrine asks a different constitutional question: whether the communication expresses threatened unlawful violence and whether the constitutionally required speaker mental state exists.

Do not merge them.

## Counterman v. Colorado, 600 U.S. 66 (2023)

Status: U.S. Supreme Court.

*Counterman* holds that true-threat prosecutions require a subjective mental-state floor. The First Amendment requires proof at least that the speaker consciously disregarded a substantial risk that the communication would be viewed as threatening violence, recklessness.

Source:
https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/600/22-138/

This does not mean every rude, angry, hyperbolic, or offensive remark is a true threat.

---

# 12. California Penal Code § 422 has its own demanding elements

Current primary source:
https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=PEN&sectionNum=422

Section 422 criminal-threat analysis is separate from § 415. California requires, among other things:

1. a willful threat to commit a crime resulting in death or great bodily injury;
2. **specific intent that the statement be taken as a threat**, even without intent actually to carry it out;
3. a statement that, on its face and under the circumstances, is sufficiently unequivocal, unconditional, immediate, and specific to convey gravity of purpose and an immediate prospect of execution;
4. actual sustained fear; and
5. reasonableness of that fear.

## In re George T., 33 Cal.4th 620 (2004)

Status: California Supreme Court.

The California Supreme Court independently reviewed a student's "dark poetry" and concluded the ambiguous poem and surrounding circumstances did not establish a § 422 criminal threat.

Source:
https://law.justia.com/cases/california/supreme-court/2004/s111780.html

Because § 422 requires **specific intent that the statement be taken as a threat**, its statutory mens rea is more demanding than *Counterman*'s constitutional minimum of recklessness regarding threatening character.

### Field rule

> **"He threatened me" is an allegation. Record the exact words, surrounding conduct, addressee, immediacy, specificity, speaker mental-state evidence, actual fear, duration of fear, and whether the claimed offense is § 422, § 415, stalking, or something else.**

The standing scenario assumes no threats. If genuine threats appear, that is a changed fact and the branch must be rerun.

# 13. Public-official criticism and threat analysis

The project should not erase the distinction between:

- "You're violating the Constitution";
- profanity or insults;
- "I'm going to sue you";
- "I'm filing a complaint";
- "I'm requesting your records";
- "I'm publishing this";
- aggressive but nonthreatening criticism;
- a challenge to fight; and
- a serious expression of threatened unlawful violence.

The first group can be protected petition/criticism activity even when unwelcome.

An actual challenge to fight can implicate § 415(1) or, depending on the words/context, § 415(3). A true threat can implicate § 422 or another specialized law.

Never transform legal/political consequences, lawsuit, complaint, records request, publication, into a threat of unlawful violence merely because the official experiences them as hostile.

# 14. Disorder / obstruction decision tree

When an employee, caller, guard, or officer says "disruptive," "harassing," "disturbing," "hostile," or "interfering":

## Step 1: Preserve chronology

Record what happened **before** the label or leave demand.

Do not infer disruption from the later argument over whether the person must leave.

## Step 2: Name the actual offense/rule

Possible branches include:

- § 415(1) fighting/challenge to fight;
- § 415(2) loud/unreasonable noise;
- § 415(3) fighting words;
- § 148(a)(1) officer obstruction;
- § 602.1(b) public-agency interference;
- § 422 criminal threats;
- § 646.9 stalking;
- meeting-specific § 403 / Brown Act rules;
- a valid local/facility operational rule;
- another identified statute.

If nobody can identify the rule, preserve that fact. Do not invent one.

## Step 3: Convert the adjective to conduct

Examples:

- "loud" → decibel/voice level, duration, place, activity interrupted;
- "harassing" → exact words, following, proximity, repeated conduct, targeted person;
- "obstructing" → what doorway, line, task, officer duty, or transaction was actually impeded;
- "intimidating" → conduct creating fear/coercion and who experienced it;
- "threatening" → exact words/conduct and threatened unlawful harm;
- "aggressive" → volume, posture, distance, movement, contact, actual threats.

## Step 4: Apply the constitutional narrowing rule

- communicative noise → *In re Brown*;
- offensive words → *Jefferson* / *Alejandro G.*;
- criticism of police → *Hill* / *Duran* / *Poocha* / *Mackinney*;
- listener hostility → *Center for Bio-Ethical Reform*;
- true threats → *Counterman* plus the actual California statute.

## Step 5: Check actual effect

Ask:

- Did lawful business stop?
- Did a public transaction become impossible?
- Was anyone blocked?
- Did an officer have to abandon/delay a lawful duty?
- Was the delay substantial rather than momentary?
- Was there an immediate violence risk?
- Was the volume communication or a guise to disrupt?
- Was fear of threatened unlawful violence actually sustained where the statute requires it?

## Step 6: Rerun changed facts

If conduct escalates after protected activity begins, do not freeze the legal analysis at the original lawful moment.

A person can begin with protected recording/criticism and later commit an independent offense. Conversely, an official cannot retroactively manufacture an earlier offense merely because a later argument occurs.

# 15. Field rules for common video claims

### "You're causing a disturbance."

> **Which § 415 subsection or other law are you relying on, and what specific conduct satisfies it?**

### "You're being too loud."

> **Volume alone is not the complete § 415(2) test for communicative speech. Preserve whether there was imminent violence or whether the voice was being used as a guise to disrupt lawful activity. Separate valid facility volume rules from the generic criminal statute.**

### "You're harassing customers."

> **Identify the conduct. For § 602.1(b), the question is intentional interference by obstruction or intimidation plus the qualifying refusal, not the label "harassment."**

### "You can't talk to the officer like that."

> **Criticism, profanity, and verbal challenge to police receive substantial First Amendment protection. Actual threats, fighting words in context, or conduct that substantially obstructs a lawful duty are different.**

### "You're interfering with my investigation."

> **What lawful duty was actually delayed or obstructed, and by what conduct? Mere verbal disagreement or momentary slow compliance does not automatically satisfy § 148.**
