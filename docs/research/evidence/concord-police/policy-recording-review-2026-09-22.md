## CPD Policy 423: Public Recording of Law Enforcement Activity

Policy 423 is a highly material facility-specific source.

### Verified propositions

The current policy states that the Concord Police Department recognizes the right of persons to lawfully record department members performing official duties and directs members not to prohibit or intentionally interfere with lawful recordings.

It further provides that:

- recording may occur from a public place or private property where the individual has the legal right to be present;
- recording alone is distinct from actual interference;
- interference may include tampering with a witness/suspect, inciting unlawful conduct, creating a clear officer-safety hazard through proximity, or being so close as to interfere with effective officer communication;
- the individual may not present an undue safety risk;
- when practicable, officers/supervisors should give clear warnings and directions explaining what conduct or location change is required before enforcement;
- officers should exercise restraint and should not use highly discretionary arrests merely as a means of preventing the exercise of the right to record;
- seizure or collection of cameras/media is separately constrained by law and policy.

## Penal Code § 148(g)

California Penal Code § 148(g) states that taking a photograph or making an audio/video recording of a public or peace officer, while the officer is in a public place **or** the recorder is in a place the recorder has the right to be, does not by itself constitute resisting/delaying/obstructing under § 148(a), nor by itself create reasonable suspicion to detain or probable cause to arrest.

This is a highly important bridge for the lobby analysis, but it is not a lobby-access statute and does not itself decide forum classification.

The correct chain remains:

`lawful presence / defined subarea → officer activity being recorded → actual conduct beyond recording → exact facility restriction, if any → forum/recording analysis → removal/trespass analysis`

## Lawful-presence hinge

Policy 423 and § 148(g) both make the recorder's lawful location important.

For Police Headquarters, lawful presence may depend on facts including:

- stated lobby hours;
- whether the individual remains in the ordinary public lobby or crosses a controlled/restricted threshold;
- whether the individual is using or seeking access to an opened public service/channel;
- whether a valid, applicable rule limits conduct in that subarea;
- whether actual conduct interferes with police services, witnesses, victims, or officer communication;
- whether a direction to relocate or leave rests on a lawful rule and proper authority.

Do not collapse `lawful presence` into either `government-owned property` or `door was unlocked`.

## Narrow confidentiality / information-security policies

The same current Department Policy Manual contains narrower rules that are relevant to the **reasonableness and tailoring** of restrictions around records, screens, conversations and secure workspaces.

### Policy 803: Records Unit

Policy 803 requires case reports/files to be maintained securely, limits Records Unit access to authorized personnel, and calls for additional access controls for sensitive reports.

This is strong evidence that police records and records workspaces can present legitimate confidentiality/security interests. It is **not** a rule addressed to ordinary lobby patrons and does not itself prohibit a patron from operating a camera in the public lobby.

### Policy 806: Protected Information

Policy 806 governs protected information and the Department's handling/access/security obligations for restricted law-enforcement data, including systems and information subject to legal or criminal-justice access controls.

Again, this can support protection of actual protected information, screens or systems. It should not be transformed into a building-wide public-camera rule.

### Policy 422: Body-Worn Cameras

Policy 422 includes officer-side provisions for muting/stopping audio in specified private or confidential circumstances, including certain attorney, religious-advisor and physician communications in custody.

This is useful **privacy-context evidence**, not a patron-camera rule. It shows that the Department itself distinguishes between ordinary official activity and conversations for which confidentiality can become materially stronger.

## Current dedicated lobby-camera-rule search

Targeted review of current public-facing CPD pages, the Department Policy Manual, the Community Service Desk materials, the Records Unit materials, and City police-facility pages located Policy 423 but did **not** locate a separate published rule stating that ordinary members of the public are categorically prohibited from photography or audio/video recording throughout the Concord Police lobby.

Approved wording:

> **No dedicated public-facing Concord Police Headquarters lobby-wide ordinary-patron photography/videography prohibition was located in the online sources reviewed as of September 22, 2026.**

Do not shorten this to `Concord PD allows filming in the lobby` or `Concord PD has no camera rule`.

The search finding does not establish:

- absence of a current sign at the physical entrance/lobby;
- absence of an internal security directive;
- absence of narrower restrictions around records, victims, witnesses, interviews, computer screens, confidential conversations, evidence/property, or secure areas;
- a right to record every person or item visible from the lobby;
- a right to remain after a lawful exclusion based on independent grounds.

## Privacy and security are separate from the existence of a blanket ban

A police lobby can contain people and information with substantially different privacy/security interests than an ordinary administrative lobby. Potentially material subjects include:

- victims reporting crimes;
- witnesses and confidential sources;
- juveniles;
- undercover officers;
- protected police records or screens;
- tactical/security information;
- conversations that may carry separate audio-privacy implications;
- doors, access-control systems, and restricted operational areas.

Those interests may support reasonable, appropriately scoped restrictions depending on the forum, location, information exposed, and actual rule. They should not be treated as self-executing proof that every recording is forbidden.

The policy-manual review reinforces a useful narrower rule:

> **A confidentiality duty governing government-held information does not automatically become a reciprocal duty forbidding a member of the public from recording everything visible or audible from a place where that person is lawfully present.**

If the government exposes genuinely protected information at a public counter, the specific information, applicable law, physical configuration and any valid protective restriction must still be identified.

## Public-service purpose

The City's Community Service Desk page expressly calls the desk the department's **primary public-facing point of contact** and locates it in the police lobby. Current services include assistance/information, non-emergency report intake, referrals, and administrative/front-counter services. The Records Unit also serves the public at the lobby counter.

The current Community Service Desk page expressly states that Concord Police **no longer offers LiveScan or fingerprinting services**. Stale City material referring to fingerprinting should not be used as a current lobby function.

This strongly establishes ordinary public access for defined police-service purposes during stated business hours. It does not establish that the lobby was intentionally opened for unrestricted expressive activity.

## Separate community-room channel

The Police Department advertises a public Community Meeting Room for nonprofit community groups discussing issues affecting quality of life in Concord, subject to contract/restrictions.

That channel should be analyzed separately from the lobby. A room intentionally opened for community discussion may have a different forum classification and restriction framework from the transactional public-service lobby immediately outside it.
