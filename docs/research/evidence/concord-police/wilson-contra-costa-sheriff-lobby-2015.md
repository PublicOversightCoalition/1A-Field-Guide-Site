# Local Comparator: *Wilson v. County of Contra Costa* (N.D. Cal. 2015)

**Reviewed:** 2026-09-22  
**Case:** *Wilson v. County of Contra Costa*, No. 14-cv-03491-SI (N.D. Cal.)  
**Material order:** May 6, 2015, ECF No. 103  
**Court:** U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California

Primary accessible order:

https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/district-courts/california/candce/3%3A2014cv03491/279647/103/

## Why this case matters unusually strongly to Sweep 010

This is not a generic out-of-circuit police-lobby analogue. It involved:

- the **Contra Costa County Sheriff's Office**;
- a **public sheriff's-office lobby** in the same county as Concord;
- a person who entered for ordinary law-enforcement/public-service purposes;
- an audio recorder allegedly in plain view;
- officers allegedly performing official duties in the lobby;
- an arrest allegedly triggered when an officer noticed the active recording;
- California Penal Code §§ 602.1(b) and 632 arguments;
- Ninth Circuit *Fordyce* recording doctrine.

It is therefore the closest California/local factual comparator located so far for the question of recording officers while lawfully present in a public law-enforcement lobby.

## What the May 6, 2015 order actually held

At the Rule 12(b)(6) pleading stage, Judge Susan Illston held that the plaintiff had stated a First Amendment retaliation claim against defendant Challand.

The amended complaint alleged that the plaintiff was recording police officers performing their duties in the **public lobby of the CCCSO** and that Challand seized/arrested him after noticing the recording.

The court relied on the Ninth Circuit's recognition in *Fordyce v. City of Seattle*, 55 F.3d 436 (9th Cir. 1995), of a First Amendment right to film matters of public interest. Taking the pleaded facts as true, the court concluded that the retaliation claim could proceed.

The court also declined to grant qualified immunity at that stage. It reasoned that, on the allegations before it, defendants had not established that recording violated Penal Code § 632 and had not met their burden to show Challand was entitled to qualified immunity.

## California audio-recording issue

Defendants argued that the plaintiff's audio recording was unlawful under Penal Code § 632 because it captured a confidential communication without consent.

The court rejected that argument at the pleading stage because the amended complaint alleged that **various members of the public were present in the area and could hear the conversations**. On those allegations, defendants had not established an objectively reasonable expectation that the conversation was not being overheard or recorded.

This is highly relevant to a police-lobby field guide because it demonstrates that:

`police lobby + audio recording ≠ automatically a confidential communication under §632`

But the inverse is equally important:

`Wilson ≠ every conversation in every police lobby is nonconfidential`

The actual acoustic/physical setting, participants, subject matter, privacy measures, and objectively reasonable expectations remain fact-specific.

## Trespass / § 602.1(b) issue

The same order addressed Penal Code § 602.1(b), which concerns intentional interference with lawful business conducted by employees of a public agency open to the public, obstruction of persons attempting to carry on business, and refusal to leave after a qualifying request by a supervisor.

The court did **not** hold that a public sheriff's lobby can never support a § 602.1(b) arrest. Instead, it held that the pleaded facts made probable cause implausible at that stage because the plaintiff alleged that officers physically blocked him from leaving and that he had difficulty standing/walking.

The order expressly recognized that later evidence could potentially show intentional interference and refusal sufficient to support probable cause.

This fits the Field Guide's existing rule:

`public access + recording ≠ immunity from independently satisfied trespass/interference elements`

and equally:

`request to leave + recording ≠ automatic satisfaction of §602.1(b)`.

## Procedural limitations

This case must be used carefully.

1. It is a **federal district-court** decision, not binding Ninth Circuit precedent.
2. The key First Amendment ruling occurred on a **motion to dismiss**, requiring the court to accept well-pleaded factual allegations as true and draw reasonable inferences for the plaintiff.
3. The order did **not** perform a full public-forum classification of the sheriff's lobby.
4. It did **not** adjudicate a neutral, preexisting lobby-wide anti-recording policy comparable to the ordinances/policies litigated in *Hoffman* or *Reyes*.
5. The action did not proceed to a final merits judgment on the surviving recording claim. The docket shows a September 2015 order to show cause for failure to prosecute followed by judgment; the recording issue therefore was not finally resolved after discovery/trial.

Approved status:

`HIGHLY-RELEVANT-LOCAL-DISTRICT-COURT-COMPARATOR / PLEADING-STAGE / NONBINDING / NO-FINAL-MERITS-ADJUDICATION`

## Effect on the Concord Police analysis

*Wilson* materially strengthens the need to avoid this invalid shortcut:

`police lobby is purpose-limited → therefore recording officers there is unprotected`

At least one Northern District of California court, applying Ninth Circuit *Fordyce* doctrine to a **Contra Costa public sheriff's lobby**, allowed a First Amendment retaliation claim based on recording officers in that lobby to proceed and rejected qualified immunity at the pleading stage on the allegations presented.

That does not prove a categorical right to film everything in Concord Police Headquarters. Instead it supports the more exact analysis already emerging in Sweep 010:

`lawful presence + public-facing officer activity + recording conduct + actual privacy/interference facts + actual facility rule + forum standard`

For Concord specifically, the relevance is enhanced by CPD Policy 423 and Penal Code § 148(g), neither of which supports treating recording alone as obstruction.

## Registry / Field Guide consequence

For California police/sheriff lobbies, *Wilson* should be retained as a **local persuasive anchor for officer-recording activity**, separate from the forum-classification anchor.

That produces a useful two-anchor method:

- **forum/access anchor:** controlling general forum doctrine such as *Cornelius* / *Sammartano* plus facility-purpose facts;
- **activity anchor:** *Fordyce*, § 148(g), agency recording policy, and highly relevant local cases such as *Wilson*.

One precedent need not perform both jobs.
