# Sweep 015: Recording-Device Seizure, Retention, Search, and Compelled Access

**Qualification target:** `CA-F5.9`

This sweep addresses a recurring field sequence:

> phone/camera records encounter → officer asks to see it → person offers or refuses → officer takes physical custody → device is retained → government seeks or conducts digital search → government seeks an unlock method

The essential rule is that these are **different Fourth/Fifth Amendment events**.

Operational sequence:

> **encounter stage → government act → initial seizure authority → duration/diligence → digital-search authority → frisk/plain-view/plain-feel/consent limits → news-material overlay → compelled-access method**

Do not collapse:

- a request to view one recording;
- consent to view that recording;
- physical possession of the device;
- temporary securing of the device;
- evidentiary seizure;
- prolonged retention while a warrant is sought;
- digital browsing/search;
- forensic extraction; and
- compelled biometric/passcode access.

---

# 1. Physical seizure and digital search are separate Fourth Amendment questions

A property seizure occurs when government meaningfully interferes with a person's possessory interests. The Fourth Amendment therefore protects more than privacy in digital contents; it also protects the possessory interest in the physical device.

Supreme Court anchors include:

- *United States v. Jacobsen*, 466 U.S. 109 (1984);
- *United States v. Karo*, 468 U.S. 705 (1984); and
- *Soldal v. Cook County*, 506 U.S. 56 (1992).

Karo source:
https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/468/705

For a recording device, ask separately:

1. Could police lawfully take physical custody?
2. Could they lawfully keep it for the time they did?
3. Could they lawfully inspect digital contents?
4. Could they lawfully compel a method of access?

A yes to one does not automatically answer the others.

---

# 2. Riley does not prohibit every physical seizure of a phone

## Riley v. California, 573 U.S. 373 (2014)

Status: U.S. Supreme Court; controlling.

Riley holds that a lawful arrest does not ordinarily authorize a warrantless search of the digital contents of a cell phone under the search-incident-to-arrest doctrine.

Official U.S. Reports:
https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/boundvolumes/573BV.pdf

But Riley expressly distinguishes **securing the physical phone** from searching its digital contents. The defendants conceded that officers could seize and secure their phones to prevent destruction of evidence while seeking a warrant. Riley also explains that, once the phone is secured, the arrestee ordinarily cannot personally delete its data.

Field Guide rule:

> **Riley is a digital-search rule, not a no-physical-seizure rule.**

A valid arrest may permit officers to take custody of a phone found on the arrestee even though Riley generally requires a warrant or another recognized exception before police search the stored digital contents.

That physical custody is still subject to ordinary Fourth Amendment limits, including the basis for seizure and the reasonableness of continued retention.

---

# 3. Temporary seizure on less than probable cause is narrow, not a generic phone-hold rule

## United States v. Place, 462 U.S. 696 (1983)

Status: U.S. Supreme Court.

Place permits a limited temporary detention of luggage on reasonable suspicion for the specific investigative purpose presented there, but held the approximately 90-minute seizure unreasonable on the facts. Duration, diligence, and the intrusion on possessory interests matter.

Source:
https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/462/696

Do not rewrite Place as:

> Police may take any phone for as long as needed whenever they reasonably suspect it contains evidence.

The permissible scope of a limited property detention depends on the actual investigative basis and purpose. A more substantial evidentiary seizure ordinarily requires stronger authority.

---

# 4. Evidentiary seizure ordinarily requires probable cause plus lawful seizure authority

## Arizona v. Hicks, 480 U.S. 321 (1987)

Status: U.S. Supreme Court.

Hicks holds that the plain-view doctrine does not permit evidentiary seizure based merely on reasonable suspicion. The object's incriminating character must be supported by probable cause, along with lawful access to the object.

Source:
https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/480/321/

For an ordinary phone or camera:

> The fact that the object is visibly a phone or camera does not itself make its incriminating character immediately apparent.

If police claim the device itself is evidence, identify:

- probable cause connecting this particular device to an offense;
- the warrant or recognized exception permitting the physical seizure;
- whether officers are merely securing it temporarily or taking it into evidence; and
- whether the same authority permits any digital search.

---

# 5. Preservation exigency must be genuine, not speculative

A recognized exigency can sometimes permit immediate warrantless action where officers have probable cause and reasonably face imminent evidence destruction or another urgent circumstance.

## Riley / Camou

In *Riley*, the Supreme Court rejected a categorical remote-wiping justification for warrantless phone searches after arrest. Once officers secure the device, the arrestee cannot personally delete the data; the Court also identified practical methods to address remote wiping and preserved true case-specific exigency.

In **United States v. Camou, 773 F.3d 932 (9th Cir. 2014)**, the published Ninth Circuit applied Riley and rejected a warrantless phone-content search performed approximately an hour and twenty minutes after arrest. The government failed to show an imminent "now or never" evidence-destruction problem, and even a genuine exigency would have to be limited in scope to the need that justified it.

Source:
https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/ca9/12-50598/12-50598-2014-12-11.html

Field rule:

> **"There might be evidence on the phone" and "someone could theoretically delete it" are not interchangeable with a demonstrated exigency.**

A real imminent destruction threat can change the analysis. The claimed exigency and the action taken must match.

---

# 6. Retention can become unreasonable even if initial seizure was valid

The Fourth Amendment continues to apply after the moment police first take the property.

## United States v. Song Ja Cha, 597 F.3d 995 (9th Cir. 2010)

Status: Ninth Circuit, published.

Song Ja Cha states the important general rule that a seizure reasonable at inception can become unreasonable because of its duration or other circumstances. The case involved a residence rather than a phone, but its duration/diligence principle is binding in the circuit.

Source:
https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/ca9/09-10147/09-10147-2011-02-25.html

## United States v. Dass, 849 F.2d 414 (9th Cir. 1988)

Status: Ninth Circuit, published.

Dass held lengthy detention of mailed packages unreasonable where officers had probable cause but waited days before diligently obtaining warrants. Probable cause did not permit police to retain property indefinitely while obtaining warrants at their leisure.

Source:
https://openjurist.org/849/f2d/414

## United States v. Sullivan, 797 F.3d 623 (9th Cir. 2015)

Status: Ninth Circuit, published.

Sullivan is an important counterweight against creating a bright-line deadline. The Ninth Circuit held a 21-day laptop retention reasonable under the **specific totality of circumstances**, emphasizing the defendant's incarceration, parole status/search conditions, failure to seek return, later consent, and the government's evidentiary interests.

Official opinion:
https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2015/07/29/12-10196.pdf

Sullivan distinguishes Dass rather than overruling its diligence rule.

## Duration factors

For a retained phone/camera, preserve:

- strength of the initial seizure basis;
- whether the owner is free or incarcerated;
- real-world need for the device;
- whether the owner expressly requested its return;
- whether police promptly began a warrant application;
- investigative complexity;
- whether an appropriate magistrate was reasonably available;
- whether police actually needed the device after initial investigation;
- whether a less intrusive preservation method was available; and
- the total duration before warrant/search/return.

There is no safe project slogan such as "24 hours is lawful" or "three days is automatically unconstitutional."

# 7. Very recent Ninth Circuit phone cases are useful comparators but nonprecedential

## Carlson v. Chavez, No. 25-3038 (9th Cir. Sept. 9, 2026)

Status: Ninth Circuit memorandum; **NOT FOR PUBLICATION / nonprecedential under Rule 36-3**.

The majority rejected speculative evidence-destruction exigency and separately concluded that continued retention of a phone became unreasonable after the investigating detective indicated it was not needed. The memorandum relies on established warrant/exigency and duration principles but is not itself precedential authority.

Official memorandum:
https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/memoranda/2026/09/09/25-3038.pdf

Project use: current factual comparator for a citizen's phone taken as supposed evidence; do not cite it as binding doctrine.

## Langham v. Noyd, No. 24-7292 (9th Cir. Dec. 23, 2025)

Status: Ninth Circuit memorandum; **NOT FOR PUBLICATION / nonprecedential**.

The majority treated a 36-day phone retention before pursuit of a search warrant as unreasonably prolonged on the facts. The dissent disputed the clearly-established-law analysis. The memorandum is a useful duration/diligence comparator, not a precedential deadline.

Official memorandum:
https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/memoranda/2025/12/23/24-7292.pdf

## United States v. Sessions, No. 21-50125 (9th Cir. Jan. 18, 2024)

Status: Ninth Circuit memorandum; **NOT FOR PUBLICATION / nonprecedential**.

Sessions illustrates the opposite factual direction: retention of a lawfully seized phone was upheld where the owner was incarcerated, did not seek return, and a later independent investigation obtained a warrant. It reinforces Sullivan's totality approach.

Official memorandum:
https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/memoranda/2024/01/18/21-50125.pdf

# 8. Terry frisk does not become a search for evidence merely because a phone is present

## Minnesota v. Dickerson, 508 U.S. 366 (1993)

Status: U.S. Supreme Court.

A Terry frisk is a protective weapons search. Dickerson permits seizure of nonthreatening contraband detected through lawful touch only when its incriminating character is immediately apparent and the officer does not exceed the protective frisk by additional manipulation.

Source:
https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/508/366

California's published **People v. Garcia, B187453 (Cal. Ct. App. Dec. 14, 2006)**, reviewed in Sweep 014, similarly rejects converting a Terry patdown into a generalized search for identification.

For recording devices:

> Feeling a rectangular phone during a lawful patdown ordinarily establishes that an object feels like a common lawful device. It does not by itself make the phone contraband, establish probable cause that it contains evidence, or authorize digital browsing.

Different facts can matter, for example, an independently supported evidentiary nexus, a warrant, a lawful arrest, or a genuine safety concern involving a different object.

# 9. Containers and devices: physical access does not equal digital-content access

Traditional container doctrine can authorize physical inspection of some physical containers under recognized exceptions. Modern digital devices are different for stored data.

Camou, applying Riley, rejected treating a cell phone as an ordinary container for purposes of the vehicle exception to justify a warrantless digital-content search.

Project rule:

> **A phone can be physically located inside a pocket, bag, case, or vehicle without its digital contents becoming just another physical container compartment.**

Analyze the authority to:

1. open the physical bag/case;
2. seize the physical device; and
3. search the device's digital contents

separately.

---

# 10. Consent is limited to what was actually authorized

## Florida v. Jimeno, 500 U.S. 248 (1991)

Status: U.S. Supreme Court.

The scope of consent is measured by objective reasonableness, what the typical reasonable person would have understood from the exchange. A person can delimit the scope of consent.

Source:
https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/500/248

For the project scenario, offering to show **one identified recording** should not be silently converted into consent to browse unrelated photographs, messages, accounts, contacts, location history, cloud files, or applications.

Useful evidence-preserving language:

> **I am offering only this identified recording. I do not consent to any broader search of my device.**

A clear limitation is useful evidence of scope, not a magic incantation. The actual exchange and conduct still matter.

If an officer orders physical surrender despite the refusal of consent:

> **I do not consent to seizure or search. If you are ordering me to hand it over, I am complying under protest and not consenting.**

Physical compliance with an asserted police command is not necessarily voluntary consent. The validity of the order must be analyzed independently.

---

# 11. Search incident to arrest: physical custody versus stored data

A lawful custodial arrest can support seizure of physical property associated with the arrestee under ordinary arrest/property doctrines.

But Riley rejects the proposition:

> arrest → automatic authority to inspect everything stored on the phone.

For ordinary digital contents, identify:

- a search warrant;
- valid consent;
- genuine case-specific exigency;
- parole/probation or another recognized special search condition; or
- another actual exception.

Do not assume a special-condition case applies to an ordinary member of the public.

---

# 12. Compelled biometric unlocking: Payne now supplies a binding Ninth Circuit rule, but narrowly

## United States v. Payne, 99 F.4th 495 (9th Cir. 2024)

Status: Ninth Circuit, published/binding.

Payne involved a **California parolee** subject to broad parole search conditions. The Fourth Amendment digital search was sustained on that special parole basis. That portion of the case must not be generalized to an ordinary person without such a search condition.

The Fifth Amendment holding is separately important. Officers physically selected and used Payne's thumb to unlock the phone. The Ninth Circuit held that compelled use non-testimonial on those facts because it required no cognitive exertion and did not force Payne to reveal the contents of his mind.

Source:
https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/ca9/22-50262/22-50262-2024-04-17.html

The court expressly cautioned that the opinion should **not** be read to all biometric-unlock situations. The testimonial result might differ if police required the person to independently select which finger would unlock the device.

Field rule:

> **Do not say "biometrics are always unprotected by the Fifth Amendment." Payne is narrower.**

And do not use Payne's parole-search holding to bypass Riley for an ordinary person.

---

# 13. California published biometric case: People v. Ramirez

## People v. Ramirez, H049957 (Cal. Ct. App. 2023)

Status: published California Court of Appeal.

Ramirez held, on its warrant-based facts, that compelling a fingerprint to unlock a phone was non-testimonial. The warrants and incorporated probable-cause materials were central to the Fourth Amendment analysis; the court also found good faith as an alternative suppression ground.

Source:
https://law.justia.com/cases/california/court-of-appeal/2023/h049957.html

Ramirez is consistent with Payne's narrow physical-characteristic reasoning. It is not authority for warrantless digital searching of an ordinary person's phone.

---

# 14. Alphanumeric passcodes remain a distinct Fifth Amendment problem

Payne repeatedly contrasts a biometric physical characteristic with forcing a person to reveal information held in the mind, such as a phone passcode. But the case did **not** itself decide every compelled-passcode/foregone-conclusion scenario.

## In re Grand Jury Subpoena Dated April 18, 2003, 383 F.3d 905 (9th Cir. 2004)

Status: Ninth Circuit, published.

In the document-production context, the Ninth Circuit held that compelled production can be testimonial and explained that the foregone-conclusion doctrine depends on the government's prior knowledge of existence, possession, and authenticity rather than using the compelled act to discover those facts.

Source:
https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/383/905/495139/

That doctrine informs passcode litigation but is not a published Ninth Circuit holding that resolves every compelled phone-passcode scenario.

**Project status:** ordinary device seizure/search analysis is supported, but the precise compelled **alphanumeric passcode / foregone-conclusion** application remains a specialized **RESEARCHED-UNRESOLVED edge**. Do not turn that edge into either:

> Police can always force the passcode if they have a warrant.

or:

> A passcode can never be compelled.

The exact order, warrant, requested act, government's prior knowledge, and testimonial implications matter.

# 15. Federal Privacy Protection Act can independently restrict seizure of newsgathering materials

## 42 U.S.C. § 2000aa

The federal Privacy Protection Act generally restricts government officers/employees, in connection with criminal investigation or prosecution, from searching for or seizing defined **work product** or **documentary materials** possessed by persons connected to public dissemination, subject to enumerated statutory exceptions.

Current text:
https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/42/2000aa

Definitions:
https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/42/2000aa-7

Remedies:
https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/42/2000aa-6

The definitions expressly include photographs, motion-picture films, video/audio recordings, and other electronically recorded media.

Important limits/exceptions include, depending on the subsection:

- probable cause that the possessor committed or is committing the offense to which the materials relate;
- specified limitations on that suspect exception;
- immediate need to prevent death or serious bodily injury;
- subpoena/compliance and destruction/concealment conditions for documentary materials; and
- exclusions in the definitions for contraband, fruits of crime, and instrumentalities.

## Credential-neutral project point

The PPA's text asks about the defined materials and a purpose to disseminate public communication. It does not make possession of a government-issued "press credential" the statutory trigger.

That does **not** mean every phone carried by every person automatically receives PPA protection. The dissemination purpose, material category, criminal-investigation context, and exceptions must actually fit.

Persuasive outside-circuit cases can be used only as comparators:
- *Garcia v. Montgomery County, Maryland* (D. Md. 2015), video-card seizure from person filming police; factual PPA questions survived;
- *Sennett v. United States*, 667 F.3d 531 (4th Cir. 2012), independent photographer/newsgatherer and suspect-exception analysis.

The statute itself is the primary authority.

# 16. California has a separate newsperson warrant-protection layer

## Penal Code § 1524(g)

Current California law states that no search warrant shall issue for items described in Evidence Code § 1070.

Official source:
https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=PEN&sectionNum=1524.

Section 1524(f) broadly defines documentary evidence to include photographs, audio/video recordings, films, computer printouts, and similar material.

## Evidence Code § 1070 / California Constitution art. I, § 2(b)

Evidence Code § 1070 protects qualifying newspersons against contempt for refusing to disclose sources or unpublished information. "Unpublished information" includes notes, outtakes, photographs, tapes, and other undisclosed data.

Official source:
https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=EVID&sectionNum=1070.

California Constitution:
https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=CONS&sectionNum=SEC.+2.&article=I

## Miller v. Superior Court, 21 Cal.4th 883 (1999)

The California Supreme Court describes the state shield law as affording very strong contempt immunity for protected unpublished information, subject to constitutional conflicts such as a criminal defendant's fair-trial rights. Unpublished information need not be confidential.

Source:
https://scocal.stanford.edu/opinion/miller-v-superior-court-people-32069/

### Scope caution

The California shield-law text uses defined newsperson/media-connection categories. This project does **not** assume that every ordinary citizen with a phone automatically satisfies § 1070 merely because they plan to post a recording.

That is materially different from the PPA's statutory dissemination-purpose language.

Field Guide sequence:

> federal PPA applicability → California §1524(g)/§1070 applicability → ordinary Fourth Amendment warrant/seizure rules

Do not merge the three into one generic "journalist privilege."

---

# 17. First Amendment sensitivity does not erase ordinary evidence law

Recording police/public officials can be protected expressive/newsgathering activity. Seizing expressive material for the purpose of suppressing publication can raise additional First Amendment concerns.

But the project should not claim:

> Because the recording is First Amendment material, police can never lawfully seize it as evidence.

A properly supported evidentiary seizure can still be lawful, subject to the Fourth Amendment and any additional PPA/shield-law restrictions that actually apply.

Conversely:

> "It's evidence" is not a blank check to suppress, browse, retain, or destroy expressive material.

Purpose, authority, duration, dissemination protections, and search scope remain separate.

# 18. California recording-device decision tree

## Step 1: Identify the encounter stage

Is this:

- consensual contact;
- Terry detention;
- lawful arrest;
- warrant execution;
- parole/probation/special-condition search; or
- another specialized setting?

Do not import a parole rule into an ordinary street/public-facility encounter.

## Step 2: Identify the government act

Is the officer:

- asking to see a recording;
- asking you to hand over the device;
- briefly holding it;
- seizing it into evidence;
- retaining it while seeking a warrant;
- browsing digital content;
- imaging/extracting data;
- demanding a passcode; or
- compelling biometric unlocking?

Name the act before naming the authority.

## Step 3: Test initial physical seizure authority

Ask whether there is:

- a warrant;
- lawful arrest/property authority;
- probable cause plus a recognized warrant exception;
- genuine preservation exigency;
- a narrowly justified Place-type temporary detention;
- valid consent;
- a special search condition; or
- another specific rule.

The statement "your video is evidence" is a conclusion, not the authority.

## Step 4: Test duration and diligence

If police keep the device:

- when was it taken?
- when did they begin seeking a warrant?
- when was the warrant submitted/issued?
- did the owner request return?
- is the owner free and dependent on the device?
- is continued retention still necessary?
- what changed after the initial investigation?

Use Song Ja Cha / Dass / Sullivan rather than a fixed hour/day rule.

## Step 5: Test digital search authority separately

Riley ordinarily requires a warrant for stored contents even after lawful arrest.

Check:

- warrant;
- consent and exact scope;
- genuine case-specific exigency;
- valid parole/probation/special search condition;
- another recognized exception.

## Step 6: Test frisk / physical container doctrines separately

A Terry frisk is for weapons. Plain feel requires immediately apparent incriminating character without exploratory manipulation.

A lawful physical search of a bag/case does not itself authorize browsing the phone discovered inside it.

## Step 7: Check dissemination/news-material statutes

If the device contains material prepared/held for public dissemination:

- check the Privacy Protection Act;
- check §1524(g)/Evidence Code §1070 only if the California newsperson definitions actually fit;
- preserve exceptions.

## Step 8: Analyze compelled access method

- physical thumb/fingerprint selected by police: Payne supplies a narrow non-testimonial rule;
- biometric requiring user choice/cognitive act: Payne expressly leaves room for a different result;
- alphanumeric passcode: separate testimonial/foregone-conclusion analysis; do not state a categorical rule.

# 19. Practical field language

These phrases preserve distinctions; they are not magic words.

If asked to show one recording:

> **I am offering only this identified recording. I do not consent to any broader search of my device.**

If ordered to surrender the device:

> **I do not consent to seizure or search. If you are ordering me to hand it over, I am complying under protest and not consenting.**

If police say they are keeping it:

> **Are you seizing this device as evidence? What legal basis are you relying on, and are you seeking a warrant?**

If the encounter continues:

> **I am requesting return of my device if you are not obtaining a warrant or no longer need to retain it.**

Do not physically resist an attempted seizure.

Preserve:

- exact time of seizure;
- officer identities;
- asserted offense/evidence nexus;
- whether a warrant was mentioned;
- whether return was requested;
- property/evidence receipt;
- case/report number;
- later warrant and inventory;
- date/time of return; and
- whether data was searched/copied/altered/deleted.

# 20. Common bad field rules

| Bad slogan | Better rule |
| --- | --- |
| "Riley means cops can't take my phone." | Riley generally protects digital contents; physical seizure is separately analyzed. |
| "They can take it because it has evidence." | Identify probable cause and the warrant/exception authorizing physical seizure, then separately analyze retention and digital search. |
| "Once seized, they can hold it until the case ends." | Continued retention must remain reasonable; duration and diligence matter. |
| "If they arrest me, they can search the phone." | Riley generally rejects digital search incident to arrest. |
| "A frisk lets them take anything they feel." | Terry is weapons-protective; plain-feel seizure requires immediately apparent incriminating character without extra manipulation. |
| "I showed them one video, so they can inspect the phone." | Consent scope is what was reasonably authorized; expressly limiting the offered recording helps preserve the boundary. |
| "Remote wiping always creates exigency." | Riley/Camou require a genuine case-specific imminent need, not a categorical possibility. |
| "Biometrics are always Fifth-Amendment-free." | Payne is narrow and expressly warns against extending its result to every biometric scenario. |
| "Passcodes can never be compelled." | The Ninth Circuit act-of-production/foregone-conclusion framework is fact sensitive; no categorical project rule is stated. |
| "My video is journalism, so it can never be seized." | Check PPA/shield-law applicability and exceptions plus ordinary Fourth Amendment authority. |
