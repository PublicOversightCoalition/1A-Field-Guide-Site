# Sweep 011: California Lawful Vantage, Visual Privacy, and Exposed Information

**Issue:** #64  
**Package:** GAP-01  
**Research date:** September 28, 2026  
**Status:** Defined GAP-01 slice reviewed; affected qualification rows must be re-evaluated individually.

## Scope

This sweep supplies the California overlay for `NC-VANTAGE-01.A–F` and the overlapping functional rows `CA-F2.1` and `CA-F2.3`.

The research question is not whether a camera is categorically lawful or unlawful. It is:

> What California/federal authority lets the Field Guide distinguish ordinary observation or recording from a lawful vantage from physical intrusion, limited-audience privacy, device-assisted constructive intrusion, voyeuristic viewing, confidential-audio recording, or actual access to a computer/device?

This sweep deliberately separates:

1. the observer's exact location and right to remain;
2. general-public visibility from visibility to only a limited audience or unusual vantage;
3. ordinary visual observation/photography/video from physical intrusion;
4. ordinary visual capture from a device that makes otherwise inaccessible private activity obtainable;
5. visual recording from audio/confidential communications;
6. seeing a displayed screen/document from accessing a computer, account, system, or stored data;
7. acquisition from later publication/use; and
8. direct civilian/privacy authority from Fourth Amendment police-search cases used only as factual analogy.

## California privacy cases reviewed

### Hill v. National Collegiate Athletic Assn., 7 Cal.4th 1 (1994)

California's constitutional privacy framework requires a legally protected privacy interest, a reasonable expectation of privacy in the circumstances, and a serious invasion; competing interests can affect the ultimate analysis.

Use here: privacy is contextual rather than an on/off label. The state constitutional privacy right can apply to nongovernmental as well as governmental conduct.

Source:
https://law.justia.com/cases/california/supreme-court/4th/7/1.html

### Shulman v. Group W Productions, Inc., 18 Cal.4th 200 (1998)

For the common-law intrusion tort, the California Supreme Court described two essential ideas that matter to lawful-vantage analysis:

- the defendant must intentionally intrude into a private place, conversation, or matter in a manner highly offensive to a reasonable person; and
- ordinary observation, photography, or recording of a person in a public place generally does not establish the private-place element merely because the person did not want to be observed.

The court also made clear that newsgathering does not create a general constitutional privilege to violate otherwise applicable privacy law.

Use here: strong direct authority against both extremes. Public-place observation is materially different from penetration of seclusion, but a camera is not a universal immunity from privacy law.

Source:
https://scocal.stanford.edu/opinion/shulman-v-group-w-productions-inc-31911/

### Sanders v. American Broadcasting Companies, Inc., 20 Cal.4th 907 (1999)

The California Supreme Court rejected the proposition that being visible or audible to some coworkers automatically destroys all privacy against covert recording. A workplace not generally open to unfettered public access could support a limited expectation against secret television recording even though coworkers could see or hear the interaction.

Use here: **limited visibility is not necessarily general-public exposure**. The Field Guide must ask who could ordinarily see/hear the matter, from where, and by what means.

Source:
https://law.justia.com/cases/california/supreme-court/4th/20/907.html

### Hernandez v. Hillsides, Inc., 47 Cal.4th 272 (2009)

Employees could possess a reasonable visual-privacy expectation against hidden video monitoring in a shared enclosed office. The court separately analyzed whether the intrusion was sufficiently serious/offensive and concluded the claim failed on that second element on the particular facts.

The opinion also recognizes that a camera's persistent lens can be more penetrating than ordinary human observation and discusses California's statutory visual-privacy protections.

Use here: recording technology can change the privacy analysis even where ordinary human observation by a limited set of people could occur.

Source:
https://law.justia.com/cases/california/supreme-court/2009/s147552/

### Flanagan v. Flanagan, 27 Cal.4th 766 (2002)

For Penal Code § 632, a communication is confidential when a party has an objectively reasonable expectation that it is not being overheard or recorded. The question is not simply whether the speaker expected the contents never to be repeated later.

Use here: audio is a separate branch from visual observation.

Source:
https://law.justia.com/cases/california/supreme-court/4th/27/766.html

## California statutory findings

### Civil Code § 1708.8: physical and constructive invasion

Subdivision (a) addresses knowing entry/trespass undertaken to capture a visual image, sound recording, or other physical impression of a plaintiff engaged in private, personal, or familial activity where the invasion is offensive to a reasonable person.

Subdivision (b) separately creates constructive-invasion liability for offensively attempting to capture such activity through a device, without physical trespass, when the image/sound/impression could not have been achieved without trespass unless the device were used.

The statute defines private/personal/familial activity through reasonable-expectation-of-privacy concepts and makes clear that failed capture is not necessarily a defense.

Field consequence:

> A physically lawful position is important, but it does not end the California privacy analysis when a device is used to obtain private activity that ordinary lawful observation could not have obtained.

Do not rewrite § 1708.8(b) as a ban on ordinary camera zoom. Apply its actual statutory test.

### Penal Code § 647(i): private-property peeking posture

Section 647(i) applies when a person, while loitering/prowling/wandering **upon the private property of another**, peeks in the door or window of an inhabited building or structure without visible or lawful business with the owner/occupant.

Field consequence:

> The private-property location predicate matters. Do not rewrite § 647(i) into a general rule that looking through every window from an ordinary public sidewalk is itself this offense.

That does not resolve other privacy laws.

### Penal Code § 647(j): instrumentality and protected private zones

Section 647(j)(1) reaches viewing, by listed instrumentalities including cameras/mobile phones and similar devices, the interior of a bedroom, bathroom, changing/fitting/dressing/tanning room, or another area where the occupant has a reasonable expectation of privacy, with intent to invade privacy.

Other subdivisions address concealed-camera recording beneath clothing or of a person in specified private/undress circumstances.

Field consequence:

> A transparent barrier does not create a universal "window exception." Subject area, reasonable privacy expectation, method, and statutory intent matter.

### Penal Code § 632: confidential audio

Section 632 separately regulates electronic amplification/recording of confidential communications. Its definition excludes communications made in public gatherings, open public proceedings, and other circumstances where the parties may reasonably expect the communication may be overheard or recorded.

Field consequence:

> A video that is visually lawful can still create a separate audio question.

Section 632.7 is a distinct communications statute involving specified cellular/cordless communications and should not be silently merged into ordinary in-person § 632 analysis.

### Penal Code § 502: actual computer/system access

Section 502 protects computer data/systems from specified unauthorized access and defines/access-regulates conduct involving computers, systems, networks, services, programs, and data.

Field consequence:

> Seeing a display that has been left visible from a lawful position and actually accessing a computer/system/account are different factual events. The latter requires its own § 502 and other access-law analysis.

This sweep does not claim that merely photographing a screen visible from a lawful vantage constitutes § 502 access.

## Public/private property and right to remain

### Robins v. Pruneyard Shopping Center, 23 Cal.3d 899 (1979)

California recognized state-constitutional petition/speech protection on the facts of a large privately owned shopping center open to the public. The decision is a special private-property speech doctrine, not a rule converting every private business into public property.

Source:
https://scocal.stanford.edu/opinion/robins-v-pruneyard-shopping-center-30510/

### Golden Gateway Center v. Golden Gateway Tenants Assn., 26 Cal.4th 1013 (2001)

The judgment rejected a claimed state-constitutional leafleting right in the private residential portion of a complex not freely/openly accessible to the public. The three-justice lead opinion would have imposed a broad state-action limitation under article I, section 2(a), but Chief Justice George concurred in the judgment on a narrower ground and expressly did not join that broad conclusion.

Field consequence:

> "Private business" and "open to customers" do not automatically establish a constitutional right to remain for every expressive purpose. Identify the actual access/property right; preserve Pruneyard as a special California private-shopping-center doctrine rather than a universal rule, and do not overread the fractured Golden Gateway opinions.

Source:
https://scocal.stanford.edu/opinion/golden-gateway-center-v-golden-gateway-tenants-assn-32146/

## Fourth Amendment window/vantage cases: analogy only

The following cases concern **government searches**. They are retained solely because they illustrate why vantage facts matter. They do **not** create a civilian right to photograph private property.

### People v. Berutko, 71 Cal.2d 84 (1969)

Police were lawfully present in a common area and observed through an aperture in window coverings. The court treated the lawful/common vantage and what the resident exposed from that vantage as important to the Fourth Amendment analysis.

Source:
https://law.justia.com/cases/california/supreme-court/2d/71/84.html

### Lorenzana v. Superior Court, 9 Cal.3d 626 (1973)

Research result retained for the converse proposition: police use of a private side-yard vantage not open/common to the public changes the constitutional analysis.

Source:
https://law.justia.com/cases/california/supreme-court/3d/9/626.html

### People v. Camacho, 23 Cal.4th 824 (2000)

Research result retained for the same caution: officers' presence in an area of private property where the public was not invited mattered despite visibility through an uncovered window.

Source:
https://law.justia.com/cases/california/supreme-court/4th/23/824.html

**Project rule:** never cite these Fourth Amendment cases as though they hold that a private citizen may photograph anything police could constitutionally observe.

## Transparent barriers: resulting analytical rule

Glass, an uncovered window, or a windshield is a fact, not a conclusion.

Record:

1. the observer's exact vantage and right to remain;
2. whether the view was available to the general public or only from an abnormal/limited/private vantage;
3. blinds, curtains, tint, privacy film, covers, fencing, orientation, and other shielding;
4. whether the observer crossed, opened, touched, moved, reached through, or defeated anything;
5. whether the device materially obtained something ordinary lawful observation could not;
6. the subject/space and whether specialized privacy law applies;
7. whether audio was captured; and
8. whether later acquisition/use/publication raises a different legal issue.

## Visible documents/screens: resulting analytical rule

Do not collapse three different events:

1. information is visible because its custodian displayed or left it exposed;
2. an observer records what can ordinarily be seen from the observer's lawful vantage;
3. an observer accesses a device/system, manipulates property, defeats shielding, or uses a method that obtains what ordinary observation could not.

The governing confidentiality/privacy law must identify whom it regulates and what conduct it restricts. A confidentiality label alone does not answer the observer's legal duties, and visibility alone does not answer every privacy question.

Existing HIPAA/common-space research remains useful for the same proposition-separation method: the covered entity's safeguard duties and the visitor's independently applicable law are different questions.

## Remaining boundaries that do not keep GAP-01 open

These become changed-fact or specialized checks rather than activation-blocking holes in the ordinary scenario:

- courtrooms/court-specific rules;
- school/child-specific privacy statutes;
- medical/mental-health or victim-specific confidentiality regimes;
- intimate-image distribution/publication statutes;
- highly specialized surveillance equipment;
- trade-secret/proprietary-information disputes;
- biometric/facial-recognition collection;
- publication/private-facts liability distinct from acquisition;
- federal premises;
- special contractual confidentiality duties.

If a facility scenario materially supplies one of those facts, open the specialized branch rather than assuming Sweep 011 resolved it.
