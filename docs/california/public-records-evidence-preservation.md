# California Public Records and Evidence Preservation

> **Jurisdiction warning:** The California Public Records Act governs California state and local agencies. The federal Freedom of Information Act governs federal agencies. For federal records, use the [Federal FOIA chapter](../federal/foia.md).

For adverse government action associated with records activity, see [Records Requests and Retaliation](records-request-retaliation.md). For testing whether the underlying agency mandate is constitutionally valid rather than merely written down, see [Restriction Audit](restriction-audit.md).

**Research status:** The core California Public Records Act (CPRA) definition, request, search, electronic-record, enforcement, personal-account, preservation, and ordinary law-enforcement evidence-disclosure branches have been reviewed at the primary-text and controlling-case level. Sweep 018 adds current investigatory-record, mandatory incident/arrest information, critical-incident audio/video, peace-officer-record, redaction-cost, and CCTV/custodian distinctions. Specialized exemption families, local retention schedules, discovery, and record-specific privacy/safety rules remain changed-fact layers.

This chapter addresses a recurring audit problem: a public employee, officer, or other government worker creates evidence of an encounter, sometimes on an agency device and sometimes on a personal phone, and the auditor wants to identify, preserve, and obtain that evidence.

The analysis must keep seven questions separate:

1. **Is the item a public record?**
2. **Does the agency have an obligation to search for it?**
3. **Is it disclosable, or does an exemption/redaction apply?**
4. **How and when must the agency respond?**
5. **How long must the record be retained under law or an applicable retention schedule?**
6. **Does a request or preservation notice itself stop routine destruction?**
7. **What additional law may apply if destruction is deliberate or litigation is reasonably foreseeable?**

A correct answer to one question does not automatically answer the others.

---

# Short field rule

A work-related recording does **not** cease to be a potential public record merely because a public employee made or stored it on a personal phone or personal account.

Under California Supreme Court authority, the key inquiry is whether the writing substantively relates to the conduct of the public's business. Device ownership and account location are not categorical exclusions.

But:

> **public record** is not synonymous with **automatically disclosable**, and **CPRA request** is not synonymous with **automatic preservation hold**.

The CPRA is principally a disclosure/access statute. Other statutes, approved retention schedules, litigation-preservation rules, and destruction laws determine whether and how long a particular record must be preserved.

# 1. What counts as a public record?

## Government Code § 7920.530: public-record definition

Government Code § 7920.530 defines a public record to include a writing containing information relating to the conduct of the public's business that is **prepared, owned, used, or retained** by a state or local agency, regardless of physical form or characteristics.

Primary source:  
https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=GOV&sectionNum=7920.530

## Government Code § 7920.545: `writing` includes recordings

The CPRA's definition of `writing` is deliberately broad. It includes handwriting, printing, photographing, electronic transmission, and every other means of recording upon a tangible thing, including letters, words, pictures, sounds, symbols, and combinations of them, regardless of storage method.

Primary source:  
https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=GOV&sectionNum=7920.545

**Field implication:** video, photographs, and audio can qualify as CPRA writings. The fact that the evidence is a phone video rather than a memorandum does not remove it from the Act's definition.

# 2. Personal devices and accounts: City of San Jose

## Controlling rule

In **City of San Jose v. Superior Court, 2 Cal.5th 608 (2017)**, the California Supreme Court held that a city employee's writings concerning public business are not excluded from the CPRA simply because they were sent, received, or stored using a personal account.

The Court rejected a device/location rule. A record otherwise meeting the CPRA definition does not lose its status merely because it is outside government servers or located in an employee's personal account.

Opinion:  
https://law.justia.com/cases/california/supreme-court/2017/s218066.html

California Supreme Court case page:  
https://supreme.courts.ca.gov/case/s218066-san-jose-city-v-sc-smith

## What makes a personal-device item relate to public business?

*City of San Jose* cautions that not every communication involving a public employee is a public record. At minimum, the writing must relate **in some substantive way** to the conduct of the public's business.

The Court identified relevant considerations including:

- the content of the writing;
- the context or purpose for which it was written;
- the audience;
- whether the employee was acting or purporting to act within the scope of employment.

Primarily personal material containing only incidental references to agency business generally will not qualify merely because a public employee created it.

## Auditor-specific application: employee records the encounter on a personal phone

Consider these facts:

- the employee is on duty;
- the employee is dealing with the auditor because of the employee's public job;
- the employee intentionally takes out a personal phone;
- the employee records the official encounter to document the auditor, report the incident, show a supervisor, or otherwise preserve what occurred for work-related purposes.

Those facts create a **strong public-record case** under § 7920.530 and *City of San Jose*: the recording is a `writing`, its content concerns an official encounter, its purpose is connected to agency business, and the employee is acting or purporting to act within employment.

Do not convert that into the absolute statement `every recording made by a government employee at work is a public record.` A purely personal recording made for an unrelated private reason can present a different question. The legal test remains content, context, purpose, and relationship to public business.

### Field rule

> A public employee's personal phone is not a CPRA safe harbor. Ask what the employee was documenting and why, not who paid for the phone.

# 3. The agency's search duty reaches qualifying personal accounts/devices

*City of San Jose* also addresses the practical search problem.

The CPRA requires reasonable efforts to locate responsive public records; the search need only be reasonably calculated to locate responsive material and need not be extraordinarily extensive or intrusive.

For records potentially located in employees' nongovernmental accounts, the California Supreme Court stated that the agency's first step should be to communicate the request to the employees in question. The agency may reasonably rely on employees to search their own personal files, accounts, and devices for responsive public records.

This does **not** mean a requester normally has a right to seize or personally inspect an employee's entire private phone. It means the agency cannot categorically ignore responsive public-business material merely because the employee used a private device or account.

## Useful request language

> All video, audio, photographs, and other recordings created, received, used, or retained by agency employees relating to the interaction at [facility] on [date] at approximately [time], including responsive public records created or stored on personal devices or personal accounts used in connection with public business.

A useful additional sentence is:

> Please communicate this request to employees or other custodians reasonably likely to possess responsive public records, including qualifying records in personal accounts or devices, consistent with *City of San Jose v. Superior Court*.

This identifies the legal search problem without demanding passwords, private-device surrender, or unrelated private content.

# 4. Public-record status is separate from disclosure

The CPRA begins from broad public access, but a record can be a **public record** and still be wholly or partly withheld under a statutory exemption or privacy/privilege rule.

Government Code § 7922.000 places the burden on the agency to justify withholding by either:

1. an express provision of the CPRA; or
2. facts demonstrating that the public interest served by withholding clearly outweighs the public interest served by disclosure.

Primary source:  
https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=GOV&sectionNum=7922.000

Government Code § 7922.525 also requires disclosure of reasonably segregable nonexempt portions after exempt material is removed.

Primary source:  
https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=GOV&sectionNum=7922.525

## Field Guide distinction

Do not argue:

> `It is a public record, therefore you must give me the entire unredacted file.`

Use:

> `The item appears to be a public record. If the agency withholds or redacts any portion, identify the legal basis and release reasonably segregable nonexempt material.`

# 5. Core request and response rules

This chapter does not attempt to reproduce every CPRA exemption. These provisions form the operational core for an auditor requesting evidence.

## § 7921.300: requester's purpose generally does not control access

Access cannot be limited based on the purpose for which an otherwise disclosable record is requested.

Primary source:  
https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=GOV&sectionNum=7921.300

## § 7922.500: no delay or obstruction

An agency may not delay or obstruct inspection or copying of public records.

Primary source:  
https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=GOV&sectionNum=7922.500

## § 7922.525: inspection

Public records are generally open to inspection during office hours, subject to statutory exceptions, and reasonably segregable nonexempt portions must be available.

Primary source:  
https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=GOV&sectionNum=7922.525

## § 7922.530: copies and personal copying equipment

A request that reasonably describes an identifiable record or records triggers the agency's duty to make copies promptly available subject to applicable law and fees. The provision also allows a person inspecting a disclosable record on agency premises to use personal equipment to photograph/copy it, subject to statutory conditions and reasonable safety/order limits.

Primary source:  
https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=GOV&sectionNum=7922.530

## § 7922.535: determination deadline

As of the 2026 text, an agency ordinarily has **10 days after receipt** of a request for copies to determine whether the request, in whole or in part, seeks copies of disclosable public records in the agency's possession and to notify the requester of that determination and reasons. If records are determined disclosable, the notice must also state the estimated date and time they will be made available.

The statute permits a written extension for specified unusual circumstances, not to exceed **14 additional days**.

This is a **determination timeline**, not a universal command that every responsive record be produced within 10 days.

Primary source:  
https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=GOV&sectionNum=7922.535

## § 7922.540: written denial

A denial of a written request must be in writing, identify the names and titles or positions of each person responsible for the denial, and justify withholding under § 7922.000.

Primary source:  
https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=GOV&sectionNum=7922.540

## §§ 7922.570-7922.585: electronic records

Electronic public records generally must be made available in electronic form when requested under the statutory rules. The Act addresses available formats, certain extraction/programming costs, records no longer available electronically, system security, and computer software. The fact that public-record information is stored in a computer does not remove its public-record status.

Primary sources:

- § 7922.570: https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=GOV&sectionNum=7922.570
- § 7922.575: https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=GOV&sectionNum=7922.575
- § 7922.580: https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=GOV&sectionNum=7922.580
- § 7922.585: https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=GOV&sectionNum=7922.585

## §§ 7922.600-7922.605: duty to assist

Subject to statutory qualifications, an agency must reasonably assist a requester to make a focused and effective request by helping identify responsive records/information, describing information technology or physical locations in which records exist, and suggesting ways to overcome practical barriers to disclosure.

Primary sources:

- § 7922.600: https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=GOV&sectionNum=7922.600
- § 7922.605: https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=GOV&sectionNum=7922.605

# 5A. Law-enforcement incident evidence: current CPRA decision tree

Detailed research record: [Sweep 018, Law-Enforcement Records and Specialized Evidence Disclosure](../research/SWEEP-018-LAW-ENFORCEMENT-RECORDS-DISCLOSURE.md).

For post-incident evidence, use:

> **record/custodian → investigatory-record status → mandatory information carveout → critical-incident audio/video override → peace-officer-record override → privacy/safety/redaction → electronic format/cost → retention separately**

## § 7923.600: investigatory records

Government Code § 7923.600 is the current recodified law-enforcement investigatory-record provision, formerly part of § 6254(f).

Primary source:

https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=GOV&sectionNum=7923.600.

Current § 7923.630 expressly preserves the pre-recodification construction of former § 6254(f).

https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=GOV&sectionNum=7923.630.

Under **Williams v. Superior Court (Freedom Newspapers, Inc.), 5 Cal.4th 337 (1993)**, qualifying investigatory files are broadly exempt and the ordinary exemption does not simply expire when the investigation ends.

https://law.justia.com/cases/california/supreme-court/4th/5/337.html

Under **Haynie v. Superior Court, 26 Cal.4th 1061 (2001)**, records of an actual inquiry undertaken to determine whether a violation of law may occur or has occurred can be investigatory records even when the inquiry does not result in charges. But the court expressly cautioned that not everything law enforcement does is an investigation.

https://law.justia.com/cases/california/supreme-court/4th/26/1061.html

**American Civil Liberties Union Foundation v. Superior Court, 3 Cal.5th 1032 (2017)** and **Castañares v. Superior Court, 98 Cal.App.5th 295 (2023)** preserve that limit for bulk/routine or assistance-oriented collection. Targeted inquiry into suspected law violation differs from routine collection or determining what noncriminal assistance is needed.

https://law.justia.com/cases/california/supreme-court/2017/s227106.html

https://law.justia.com/cases/california/court-of-appeal/2023/d082048.html

### CCTV implication

Routine facility CCTV is not automatically a police investigatory record merely because police later request it. A police-held copy compiled into an investigation can present different exemption facts from the original facility-held copy.

**Field rule:** identify the custodian and purpose. Request important footage from every plausible public custodian rather than assuming all copies have the same legal status.

## §§ 7923.610 and 7923.615: the mandatory information floor

Even when the underlying report/record is exempt, the CPRA requires specified information to be made public.

Section 7923.610 covers specified arrestee information, including arrest/booking timing and location, factual circumstances, charges, bail, and related statutory categories.

https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=GOV&sectionNum=7923.610.

Section 7923.615 covers specified information about complaints or requests for assistance and agency responses, including time, substance, location, response, factual circumstances, and other listed incident information, subject to statutory safety/investigation and victim protections.

https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=GOV&sectionNum=7923.615.

**Important:** these provisions require specified **information**. They do not necessarily compel the complete underlying arrest report, 911 audio, CAD record, or radio recording.

A useful response to an investigatory-file denial is:

> If you withhold the underlying record under Government Code § 7923.600, please nevertheless provide all information required by §§ 7923.610 and 7923.615, as applicable.

## § 7923.625: defined critical-incident audio/video

Government Code § 7923.625 specifically limits withholding of audio/video that **depicts** a defined critical incident:

- officer discharge of a firearm at a person; or
- officer force resulting in death or great bodily injury.

Primary source:

https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=GOV&sectionNum=7923.625.

The statute supplies its own active-investigation delay, reassessment, privacy-redaction, and subject/representative-access rules. Continued delay becomes more demanding over time, and after one year requires the statute's heightened evidentiary showing.

**Sacramento Television Stations Inc. v. Superior Court, 112 Cal.App.5th 306 (2025)** treats § 7923.625 as a specific legislative limitation on the older investigatory-record regime for qualifying recordings and requires the agency to satisfy the statute's actual delay standard.

https://law.justia.com/cases/california/court-of-appeal/2025/c102316.html

**Field rule:** not every body-camera recording is a § 7923.625 critical-incident recording. Ordinary stops, trespass complaints, and non-GBI force remain governed by other law unless another disclosure mandate applies.

## Penal Code §§ 832.7–832.8: specified peace-officer records

Current Penal Code § 832.7 begins from personnel-record confidentiality but expressly makes enumerated categories public notwithstanding that ordinary rule, Government Code § 7923.600, or other law, subject to the statute's own limitations.

Current sources:

https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=PEN&sectionNum=832.7.

https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=PEN&sectionNum=832.8.

The current public categories include:

- officer firearm discharge at a person;
- force resulting in death or great bodily injury;
- sustained findings of unreasonable/excessive force or failure to intervene against clearly unreasonable/excessive force;
- sustained sexual-assault findings;
- sustained dishonesty findings tied to crime or officer-misconduct reporting/investigation;
- sustained prejudice/discrimination findings;
- sustained unlawful arrest or unlawful search findings; and
- the statute's specified prohibited-agreement category.

For categories requiring a **sustained** finding, § 832.8's definition matters. A complaint alone does not become a sustained finding.

The release package can include investigative reports, photographs, audio/video, interviews, findings, and discipline-related materials as specified by current § 832.7.

### Current statute, not the old blanket slogan

**Copley Press, Inc. v. Superior Court, 39 Cal.4th 1272 (2006)** applied the older confidentiality regime. Later legislation created § 832.7(b)'s affirmative public categories. Do not use *Copley* as though those later amendments do not exist.

**Becerra v. Superior Court, 44 Cal.App.5th 897 (2020)** and **First Amendment Coalition v. Superior Court, 98 Cal.App.5th 884 (2023)** recognize the later disclosure mandate and its relationship to other exemptions.

https://law.justia.com/cases/california/court-of-appeal/2023/a165888.html

### Officer names

**Long Beach Police Officers Association v. City of Long Beach, 59 Cal.4th 59 (2014)** held officer names in the on-duty-shooting context generally disclosable absent a sufficient particularized countervailing showing on the record there. Current § 832.7 expressly preserves access recognized by *Long Beach*.

https://law.justia.com/cases/california/supreme-court/2014/s200872.html

## Penal Code § 832.18: retention, not the main disclosure rule

Section 832.18 addresses BWC policies/best practices and retention. It should not be cited as though it independently makes body-camera footage public.

https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=PEN&sectionNum=832.18.

Use § 7923.625, § 832.7, or other applicable CPRA law for disclosure, and § 832.18 when retention/policy actually matters.

## Redaction costs: National Lawyers Guild v. City of Hayward

**National Lawyers Guild v. City of Hayward, 9 Cal.5th 488 (2020)** holds that ordinary staff time spent redacting exempt material from otherwise disclosable electronic police video is not requester-billable "data extraction."

https://law.justia.com/cases/california/supreme-court/2020/s252445.html

The ordinary electronic-record provisions remain §§ 7922.530, 7922.570, and 7922.575.

## Practical request sequence

After an encounter, request the actual:

- incident/report record;
- 911 audio;
- CAD/dispatch event;
- radio traffic;
- BWC/dash video;
- photographs;
- relevant facility CCTV;
- exclusion/trespass notice;
- related employee recordings/communications.

Then layer the statute:

1. If § 7923.600 is invoked, request §§ 7923.610/.615 mandatory information.
2. If a defined critical incident is depicted, invoke § 7923.625.
3. If current § 832.7(b) applies, identify the specific category.
4. Request reasonably segregable nonexempt material and the current legal basis for every redaction/withholding.
5. Separately request the retention policy/schedule if preservation is important.

Do not confuse CPRA access with civil/criminal discovery, subpoena rights, or a retention hold.

# 6. Preservation is not the same thing as disclosure: City of Gilroy

## The 2026 California Supreme Court rule

In **City of Gilroy v. Superior Court, 19 Cal.5th 38 (2026)**, filed January 15, 2026, the California Supreme Court held that the CPRA itself does **not** impose a duty on agencies to preserve for three years every record responsive to a CPRA request that the agency has withheld as exempt.

Official opinion page:  
https://courts.ca.gov/opinion/published-extended-post/2026-01-15/s282937

The Court emphasized that the CPRA does not itself contain a general retention obligation. Government Code § 7921.010(c) expressly recognizes destruction `pursuant to law`, and separate statutes govern retention and evidence preservation.

### What *Gilroy* means in the field

These statements are **not** safe field rules:

> `I requested it, so the CPRA automatically freezes it.`

> `Once I say I am going to request that recording, deletion automatically violates the CPRA.`

Neither proposition follows from *Gilroy*.

## The deliberate-destruction limitation matters

The Court carefully limited what it decided. In footnote 20, it explained that its preservation holding concerned destruction connected with the normal adoption, implementation, and operation of routine records-retention practices and policies. The Court expressly stated that it had no occasion to decide intentional destruction in other contexts that might reflect deliberate misconduct.

That means *Gilroy* should **not** be converted into:

> `An employee is free to delete a requested recording whenever they want.`

It says no such thing.

The correct analysis after a deletion is:

1. Was the item a public record?
2. What retention statute/schedule/policy governed it?
3. Was the deletion routine and authorized under that law/policy?
4. Had litigation become reasonably foreseeable, triggering separate evidence-preservation law?
5. Was the destruction intentional and outside ordinary retention practices?
6. Do Government Code §§ 6200-6201 or other destruction/spoliation provisions apply?

# 7. A preservation notice is still valuable: but do not describe it as magic

Even though a CPRA preservation notice does not automatically create a CPRA litigation hold, it remains extremely useful evidence.

If the employee records the encounter, a calm on-camera statement can establish knowledge and identify the record before it disappears:

> You're recording this interaction in connection with your work here. I intend to request that recording under the California Public Records Act. Please preserve the original recording and do not delete or alter it.

This can document:

- the existence of the recording;
- who apparently created it;
- when and where it was created;
- the work-related context;
- notice that the public sought the record;
- an explicit preservation request;
- what the employee or agency did afterward.

Do not tell an employee:

> `I gave you a preservation notice, so deleting this is automatically a felony.`

The notice may later be relevant to knowledge, intent, litigation foreseeability, compliance with retention law, or credibility, but the elements of any destruction offense or spoliation remedy must still be established.

# 8. Retention rules vary by agency and record type

There is no single California retention period for every state/local public record.

The California State Archives explains that state executive agencies operate under the State Records Management Act and approved records-retention schedules, while local government is not governed by that same standardized state program. Local-government retention depends on agency type, specific statutes, and local schedules/policies.

California State Archives records-management source:  
https://www.sos.ca.gov/archives/records-management-and-appraisal/records-management-handbook

## State executive agencies

The State Records Management Act, Government Code §§ 12270-12279, and the State Administrative Manual establish the state executive-agency records-management system. State agencies use approved records-retention schedules. State guidance explains that unscheduled state records are not simply disposable at an employee's discretion.

California State Archives:  
https://www.sos.ca.gov/archives/records-management-and-appraisal

State Administrative Manual, Chapter 1600:  
https://www.dgs.ca.gov/Resources/SAM/TOC/1600/Chapter-View

## Cities: Government Code § 34090

For cities, Government Code § 34090 provides a statutory mechanism for destruction of city records after they are no longer required, subject to approval/consent requirements and enumerated exceptions. It must be read with the city's adopted retention schedule and any more specific law governing the record.

Primary source:  
https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=GOV&sectionNum=34090

## Routine city/city-county recordings: Government Code § 34090.6

Government Code § 34090.6 contains specialized retention/destruction provisions for specified routine video monitoring and telephone/radio communications. Its definitions and conditions matter; it should not be generalized to an ad hoc personal-phone recording by an employee.

Primary source:  
https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=GOV&sectionNum=34090.6

## Counties: Government Code § 26202

Government Code § 26202 provides county record-destruction authority under specified circumstances. County retention should be checked against that statute, other applicable laws, and the county's adopted schedule/policy.

Primary source:  
https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=GOV&sectionNum=26202

### Field rule

> Do not assume `two years`, `one year`, `60 days`, or any other retention period without identifying the agency type, record type, governing statute, and actual approved/adopted schedule.

When preservation matters, request the retention policy itself.

# 9. Government Code §§ 6200-6201: deliberate destruction requires careful analysis

Government Code § 6200 imposes criminal liability on an **officer having custody** of specified records/maps/books/papers/proceedings who willfully steals/removes/secretes, destroys/mutilates/defaces, or alters/falsifies them. Section 6201 separately reaches specified acts by a person who is not the officer described in § 6200.

Primary sources:

- § 6200: https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=GOV&sectionNum=6200
- § 6201: https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=GOV&sectionNum=6201

## Published California authority: People v. Sperl

In **People v. Sperl, 54 Cal.App.3d 640 (1976)**, a published Court of Appeal decision, the Los Angeles County Marshal was convicted under § 6200 based on removal/secretion/destruction of official county radio logs.

The defendant argued that the logs were not public records because no ordinance or statute expressly required that they be maintained. The court rejected that argument. It explained that an official government record need not be expressly mandated by statute to fall within the public-record concept used there; a record kept as necessary or convenient to the discharge of official duty can qualify. The court also rejected the notion that a public official may treat official government records as personal property merely because the official controls the office.

Case source:  
https://law.justia.com/cases/california/court-of-appeal/3d/54/640.html

Related published authority includes **People v. Pearson, 111 Cal.App.2d 9 (1952)**, involving official vice-investigation records, and **Loder v. Municipal Court, 17 Cal.3d 859 (1976)**, which treated an arrest record as within § 6200's protection.

- *People v. Pearson*: https://law.justia.com/cases/california/court-of-appeal/2d/111/9.html
- *Loder v. Municipal Court*: https://law.justia.com/cases/california/supreme-court/3d/17/859.html

## Why this still does not answer the personal-phone hypothetical automatically

*Sperl* is important, but it involved official county department records and a county Marshal. Section 6200 itself uses the narrower phrase **officer having custody** and has its own statutory object/custody/willfulness requirements. The Field Guide therefore does **not** equate the CPRA's broad disclosure definition with § 6200's criminal elements.

A work-related personal-phone recording may be a CPRA public record under *City of San Jose* without automatically establishing that:

- the employee is an `officer` within § 6200;
- the recording is within § 6200's covered record/paper categories on those facts;
- the employee had the required custody relationship;
- deletion was willful and unauthorized rather than lawful routine disposition;
- § 6201 or another statute does or does not apply instead.

### Field rule

If you see an employee delete a work-related recording after being told it will be requested:

- document exactly what occurred;
- do not announce that a felony has definitely occurred;
- request the applicable retention/destruction policy;
- request records concerning deletion/disposition of the recording;
- preserve your own recording and timeline;
- determine whether §§ 6200-6201, an agency-specific retention law, or litigation-preservation rules actually apply.

# 10. Litigation preservation is a separate layer

*City of Gilroy* expressly recognized that law outside the CPRA can create preservation duties once litigation is reasonably foreseeable.

The Court cited California spoliation/discovery authorities for the principle that destruction of evidence after litigation begins, or in anticipation of discovery when litigation is objectively probable and likely, can trigger separate legal consequences.

This does **not** mean an ordinary CPRA request automatically makes litigation reasonably foreseeable. That determination depends on circumstances.

## Important distinction

> CPRA request pending

is not automatically identical to:

> litigation objectively probable and likely to arise.

If an encounter escalates into an arrest, injury claim, formal government claim, attorney preservation letter, lawsuit, or similarly concrete dispute, the separate litigation-preservation analysis becomes increasingly important.

# 11. Practical workflow: public employee records you back

## Step 1: establish the recording and work context

Useful factual questions:

- Are you recording this interaction?
- Are you making that recording in connection with your work here?
- Is that an agency device or your personal phone?

The third question can be evidentially useful, but **device ownership is not dispositive** under *City of San Jose*.

## Step 2: give a narrow preservation notice

> I intend to request that recording under the California Public Records Act. Please preserve the original recording and do not delete or alter it.

Do not overstate the legal effect of this statement.

## Step 3: promptly send the CPRA request to the agency

Identify:

- date;
- approximate time;
- facility/location;
- employee if known;
- subject of encounter;
- categories of recordings sought;
- personal-device/public-business records where applicable.

## Step 4: ask the agency to search likely custodians

Use *City of San Jose* to make clear that a responsive work-related record cannot be categorically excluded merely because it is in an employee's personal account/device.

## Step 5: if the record is withheld

Ask for:

- written legal basis for withholding;
- release of reasonably segregable portions;
- estimated availability date for disclosable records;
- identification of person(s) responsible for a written denial as required by statute.

## Step 6: if the record is missing or deleted

Request:

- retention schedule governing that record category;
- agency records-retention/destruction policy;
- existing records documenting deletion, loss, disposition, or destruction;
- metadata or audit logs if maintained;
- communications concerning preservation/deletion;
- date and manner of deletion;
- identity/position of the person who authorized or performed disposition, to the extent maintained in public records;
- any record showing routine automatic deletion.

Then analyze the facts under the correct retention/destruction law rather than assuming the CPRA alone prohibited deletion.

# 12. Suggested request templates

## A. Recording request

> Pursuant to the California Public Records Act, I request all video, audio, photographs, and other recordings created, received, used, or retained by agency employees relating to the interaction at [location] on [date] at approximately [time], including responsive public records created or stored on personal devices or personal accounts used in connection with public business. Please communicate this request to employees or other custodians reasonably likely to possess responsive records.

## B. Preservation language

> Please preserve responsive records while this request is being processed. This preservation request is intended to identify the records at issue and avoid preventable loss; it is not an assertion that the CPRA itself creates a universal retention period.

## C. Missing/deleted-record follow-up

> If any responsive record existed but is no longer available, please identify any existing public records documenting its deletion, destruction, disposition, loss, retention period, or automatic-deletion process, including the applicable retention schedule/policy and responsive deletion or audit logs maintained by the agency.

These templates should be adapted to the actual agency and incident rather than used as incantations.

# 13. Enforcement

Government Code § 7923.000 allows a person to institute proceedings for injunctive or declaratory relief or a writ of mandate to enforce the right to inspect or receive public records.

Primary source:  
https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=GOV&sectionNum=7923.000

## City of Gilroy: declaratory relief can remain meaningful after records are gone

*City of Gilroy* held that declaratory relief under the CPRA is available at least where a declaration would resolve an ongoing dispute about the parties' rights and obligations in a way likely to affect future records requests or future conduct.

Destroyed records cannot be recreated by a disclosure order, but a court can sometimes still resolve a recurring dispute about inadequate searches, exemption practices, or other CPRA obligations.

Do not turn this into a universal promise that every completed or historical CPRA dispute supports declaratory relief. *Gilroy* did not define every circumstance in which such relief is available.

# 14. Remaining specialized work

The ordinary California law-enforcement evidence-disclosure branch is now doctrine-reviewed through Sweep 018. This chapter does **not** claim exhaustive treatment of:

- every exemption in Government Code Division 10 Parts 5 and 6;
- juvenile, medical, coroner, victim-specific, or other specialized confidentiality statutes;
- criminal discovery, Pitchess motions, subpoenas, or civil discovery;
- every ALPR, facial-recognition, drone, proprietary surveillance, or database statute beyond the ordinary classification rules;
- every state-agency, city, county, special-district, school, transit, court, or other retention statute;
- the complete case law construing Government Code §§ 6200-6201, especially personal-device application and the technical scope of `officer having custody`;
- all California discovery/spoliation sanctions;
- litigation-hold doctrine beyond the preservation distinction needed here;
- attorney-fee rules and procedural litigation strategy; or
- local agency records schedules, which must be checked facility by facility when material.

These are specialized or changed-fact branches, not an unresearched statewide gap in the ordinary incident/dispatch/BWC/CCTV/officer-record decision tree.
