# Public Libraries

## Field rule

**A public library is not one constitutional forum. Identify the exact area, access condition, activity, and rule before drawing a First Amendment or trespass conclusion.**

A library may contain an ordinary public entrance, reading and collection areas, service counters, children's or teen areas, study rooms, intentionally opened meeting rooms, display/distribution channels, restrooms, staff workspaces, and exterior sidewalks or plazas. Those areas can have different purposes, controllers, access conditions, and constitutional classifications.

Contra Costa County Library is the first system-level library sweep for this chapter. The resulting rules are useful statewide, but local library systems still require their own policy and facility research.

# 1. Start with the specific library area

Do not begin with:

> This is a public library, so it is a public forum.

Begin with:

> What exact area am I asking to use, for what activity, under what access rule?

For library investigations, useful area categories include:

| Area/channel | Typical access question | Current doctrinal posture |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Exterior ordinary public sidewalk | Is it municipal/public right-of-way or library-specific property? | Traditional-forum analysis may apply; verify boundary |
| Entrance/lobby | Open for ordinary library access or subject to screening/check-in? | Public access does not itself determine forum status |
| Stacks/reading/service areas | Open for reading, research, borrowing, staff assistance, and related use | Exact Ninth Circuit classification for all expressive activity remains unresolved |
| Children's/teen area | Age/purpose restrictions? | Area-specific access conditions can lawfully matter; analyze actual rule |
| Study room | Reservation, occupancy, purpose, and time conditions? | Room/channel-specific analysis |
| Meeting/community room | Intentionally opened to outside groups or speakers? | May be limited/designated forum depending policy/practice; Faith Center is controlling for historical Antioch room |
| Exhibit/display/distribution channel | Open to outside groups/materials? | Separate intentionally opened communication channel; restrictions need forum analysis |
| Restroom | Publicly accessible ancillary space? | Access status does not itself create general expressive rights |
| Staff/restricted area | Actually closed to public? | Restricted access; do not enter under standing scenario |
| Special self-service period | Card/PIN/orientation agreement required? | Different access condition from ordinary staffed hours |

The same building can contain several of these at once.


# 2. Controlling Ninth Circuit library case: Faith Center

## Faith Center Church Evangelistic Ministries v. Glover

**Citation:** 480 F.3d 891 (9th Cir. 2007), amended published opinion.

Official opinion:

https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2007/03/09/0516132.pdf

The case involved the meeting room at the Antioch branch of the Contra Costa County Library.

The Ninth Circuit held that the **Antioch Library meeting room on the record before it was a limited public forum**. The court considered the County's policy, the kinds of speakers and activities admitted, the application process, fees, exclusions, actual practice, and the relationship between meeting-room activity and the library's primary function.

Most important for this project, the court rejected reliance on other library meeting rooms because the relevant forum is defined by the **access sought by the speaker**. In that case, the forum was the Antioch meeting room.

## What to carry into the field guide

**Retained rule:** classify the specific library area or communication channel, not the library building as a whole.

Faith Center does not establish that:

- every Contra Costa branch interior is a limited public forum;
- every California public library is a limited public forum;
- every meeting or study room has the same classification;
- filming is automatically permitted or prohibited;
- a violation of a room rule automatically creates criminal trespass.

The decision is a strong library-specific application of the broader forum rule already retained from Cornelius and Sweep 005.

# 3. California library-exterior anchor: Prigmore v. City of Redding

**Citation:** *Prigmore v. City of Redding*, 211 Cal.App.4th 1322, 150 Cal.Rptr.3d 647 (2012), Third District Court of Appeal.

Published opinion:

https://law.justia.com/cases/california/court-of-appeal/2012/c068866.html

*Prigmore* involved leafleting on the **outdoor grounds of the Redding Municipal Library**, not expressive activity inside the building.

The court approved defining the forum by the access actually sought and treated three outdoor components together on the record before it:

1. the public open space on the entry side of the library;
2. the entry/exit-door area; and
3. the adjacent parking lot.

It held those outdoor areas to be a **traditional public forum** on the particular facts, emphasizing unrestricted public access, physical characteristics encouraging congregation, the entrance area's benches and newspaper rack, adjacency to parks and other public buildings, and the library's information-dissemination function.

## What Prigmore adds

This is published California appellate authority and is especially useful because it applies the same area-specific principle used throughout this project:

> the forum is defined by the access sought, not automatically by the outer boundary of the government property.

It also distinguished the outdoor forum from the building interior and declined to use the older California "basic incompatibility" formulation as the forum-classification test.

## What Prigmore does not establish

Do not convert *Prigmore* into:

> Every sidewalk, entrance, plaza, or parking lot around every California library is automatically a traditional public forum.

The holding depended on the physical and functional characteristics of the Redding property. A branch investigation should still establish ownership/control, public-thoroughfare function, access, boundaries, customary use, seating/congregation features, and the exact expressive activity.

The court's footnote 11 broadly observed that federal courts had treated a library interior as a limited public forum, citing *Faith Center* and *Kreimer*. For this project, that footnote is **not** treated as a categorical holding about all library interiors: *Faith Center* actually defined the forum before it as the Antioch meeting room. Ordinary reading, stacks, circulation, and service areas still require their own analysis.

## Restrictions reviewed in Prigmore

At the preliminary-injunction stage, the Court of Appeal largely sustained relief against Redding's outdoor policy. Among other things, the case addressed:

- confinement of leafleting to a small designated "free speech area";
- a 72-hour advance-reservation requirement;
- restrictions on solicitation;
- a prohibition on offensively coarse speech/gestures; and
- leafleting of vehicles in the parking lot.

The court treated most challenged restrictions as likely invalid on the record, but upheld the parking-lot vehicle-leafleting ban based on the safety rationale before it.

**Field-use rule:** *Prigmore* is strong authority for scrutinizing artificial speech zones and advance-permission systems in a library's established outdoor public forum. It is not authority for ignoring reasonable, content-neutral safety rules or for assuming the same forum status indoors.

# 4. Faith Center's old religious-services dispute is historical, not current County policy

The 2007 Faith Center opinion involved a County meeting-room policy that barred religious worship services.

The current Contra Costa meeting-room policy document now shows that restriction **struck through**, with an effective date of July 6, 2009:

https://ccclib.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/72/2026/03/LP-RMS-MeetingRoomRules_2025E_ADA.pdf

On remand after the Ninth Circuit appeal, a federal district court held the religious-services restriction unconstitutional under the then-prevailing Establishment Clause framework. That 2009 district-court reasoning relied on doctrine later displaced by the Supreme Court's modern Establishment Clause approach.

In Kennedy v. Bremerton School District, 597 U.S. 507 (2022), the Supreme Court stated that it had abandoned the Lemon test and its endorsement-test offshoot.

Official opinion:

https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/21pdf/21-418diff_k4l8.pdf

**Current-use rule:** retain Faith Center's forum analysis where it remains applicable, but do not describe the old religious-services ban as current Contra Costa policy or carry the 2009 Lemon analysis forward as the current general Establishment Clause test.

# 5. Ordinary library floors: public access and forum status remain distinct

The ordinary library floor presents a different question from a meeting room intentionally opened for community use.

No published controlling Ninth Circuit or California appellate decision located in Sweep 006 categorically classifies all ordinary reading, stacks, service, and circulation areas of California public libraries for every kind of expressive activity.

That means the handbook should resist two shortcuts:

> The doors are open to everyone, therefore the entire interior is a public forum.

and:

> Libraries are quiet-use facilities, therefore no First Amendment activity is protected inside.

Neither proposition follows automatically.

## Persuasive authority: Kreimer

In Kreimer v. Bureau of Police for Town of Morristown, 958 F.2d 1242 (3d Cir. 1992), the Third Circuit treated the library before it as a limited public forum for activity compatible with the purposes for which it had been opened.

Source:

https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F2/958/1242/371694/

Kreimer emphasized library purposes involving receipt of information, reading, writing, and quiet contemplation. It upheld a rule requiring patrons to be engaged in activities associated with public-library use.

Kreimer is **persuasive, not controlling**, in California.

Other persuasive courts have used different forum terminology. Doe v. City of Albuquerque, 667 F.3d 1111 (10th Cir. 2012), for example, used designated-public-forum language when reviewing a broad exclusion from city libraries.

A newer Tenth Circuit decision, *Spiehs v. Morgan*, No. 25-3174 (10th Cir. Sept. 8, 2026), addressed restrictions on displaying signs inside a public library. The court assumed the library's designated-public-forum classification for the appeal and upheld content-neutral restrictions aimed at preserving reading and study from disruption. The decision is **persuasive only** in California and concerns signs, not filming, but it reinforces the need to analyze the specific activity and the library-purpose rationale rather than treating "public library" as a universal forum label.

Source:

https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/ca10/25-3174/25-3174-2026-09-08.html

The practical lesson is not to choose a label by analogy alone. Determine the local policy, purpose, practice, area, and activity first.

# 6. Contra Costa's current "library activity" requirement

The Contra Costa County Board of Supervisors approved an amended Patron Conduct Policy on February 10, 2026.

Current policy:

https://ccclib.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/72/2026/03/Patron-Conduct-Policy_ADA.pdf

Board adoption record:

https://contra-costa.legistar.com/LegislationDetail.aspx?GUID=FE18DC5D-D8D2-4476-A6C2-C7215C123DC5&ID=7870598&Options=&Search=

The policy requires patrons in the building to be engaged in activities associated with use of a public library. It provides examples including reading, studying, quiet conversation, working, use of library materials/equipment, and participation in a library program/activity.

## Recording and observation remain unresolved

The current policy does not expressly answer whether peaceful:

- observing public library operations;
- photographing or videorecording;
- documenting public officials performing public duties;
- asking questions; or
- gathering information for later speech

is itself an activity associated with use of the library.

Sweep 006 did not locate a dedicated current public-facing Contra Costa policy specifically governing ordinary patron photography/videography inside library facilities.

**Do not write:** "Contra Costa has no photography policy."

**Write:** "No dedicated public-facing photography/videography policy was located in the sources reviewed as of September 21, 2026."

That distinction matters because an internal, branch-specific, newly adopted, or posted rule may exist even when it has not been located online.

**Current status:** AUTHORITY-UNRESOLVED.

# 7. A camera objection is not automatically disruption

Contra Costa's Patron Conduct Policy protects other patrons' use of the library and staff members' ability to perform their duties. It also contains rules concerning threats, harassment, blocking, noise, disruptive behavior, restricted areas, and misuse of designated spaces.

Those are legitimate factual predicates to investigate.

Under the project's standing scenario, however, none of them is silently established merely because:

- an employee dislikes being recorded;
- a patron notices the camera;
- someone becomes uncomfortable;
- an employee approaches the recorder;
- someone calls security or police.

The investigator should document:

- where the recorder was standing;
- what was being recorded;
- distance from other people;
- volume;
- whether a doorway, aisle, desk, queue, or computer was obstructed;
- whether the recorder followed or crowded anyone;
- whether staff work actually stopped or was materially impeded;
- what exact policy provision was invoked.

The policy may matter. The government's characterization of the conduct does not substitute for the underlying facts.

# 8. Children's and teen areas are a separate access problem

Contra Costa has a specific Children/Teen Areas policy:

https://ccclib.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/72/2026/03/LP-Children-Teen-Area-Policy_ADA.pdf

The policy reserves children's areas primarily for children age 11 and under, their parents/guardians, and patrons using the children's collection at staff discretion. Teen areas are primarily for patrons age 12-18, persons accompanying them, and patrons using the teen collection at staff discretion.

An unaccompanied adult can therefore encounter a legitimate **area/purpose restriction** even though the broader library is open to the public.

This is why the standing scenario requires lawful presence in the **specific area**, not merely lawful entry into the building.

The presence of minors can also make privacy and safety interests especially important, but the children/teen policy itself is not a categorical public-camera statute. Any recording rule still needs its own source and analysis.

# 9. Meeting rooms: system policy, branch implementation, sometimes different controllers

Contra Costa's room directory is unusually useful:

https://ccclib.org/rooms/

The system makes meeting and study rooms available under varying rules. Some are controlled or booked by the County Library; some community rooms are booked through a city or town.

Examples currently include rooms associated with:

- City of Brentwood;
- City of Clayton;
- Town of Danville;
- City of San Pablo; and
- other local entities.

A room inside a library building therefore does not automatically mean the County Library controls every condition of its use.

For each room, record:

1. physical location;
2. owner/controller;
3. booking authority;
4. applicable County and local rules;
5. classes of eligible users;
6. permitted subjects/purposes;
7. access hours;
8. whether the room is open to the general public during a reservation;
9. branch-specific restrictions;
10. forum classification for the particular requested use.

Faith Center is controlling authority for the historical Antioch meeting-room record. It is not a template that automatically classifies every listed room today.

# 10. Displays and distribution are their own communication channel

Contra Costa maintains rules for non-library displays and distribution:

https://ccclib.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/72/2024/10/LP-rulesforexhibitsdisplay-October2024E.pdf

The Library permits qualifying non-library materials in designated display/distribution areas, subject to authorization, available space, priority rules, and subject/use restrictions.

Because the government has intentionally opened a communication channel to outside materials, that channel requires its **own forum analysis**.

The current rules include restrictions involving religious use and instructions on how to vote for a candidate or political party, while allowing announcements of political meetings.

Sweep 006 does not reach a constitutional conclusion about those restrictions.

Questions reserved for deeper review include:

- what exact forum the County has created;
- which subject restrictions define the forum versus discriminate within it;
- whether standards are reasonable in light of the channel's purpose;
- whether they are viewpoint neutral;
- whether branch-specific rules add materially different restrictions.

**Current status:** AUTHORITY-UNRESOLVED.

# 11. Patron privacy records are not a visitor-camera prohibition

Contra Costa's privacy materials identify California Government Code section 7927.105 as protecting qualifying library patron registration and use records.

Library privacy page:

https://ccclib.org/privacy/

California State Library law compilation:

https://www.library.ca.gov/uploads/2025/01/California-Library-Laws-2025.pdf

A confidentiality statute governing records maintained by the library is not the same thing as a statute prohibiting a visitor from using a camera.

Do not reason:

> Patron borrowing records are confidential, therefore filming anywhere in the library is illegal.

Separate issues may still create lawful restrictions, including constitutional privacy, confidential communications, harassment, children's areas, particular programs, copyrighted performances, disruption, and a valid facility policy. Each requires its own authority and factual predicates.

# 12. Suspension and exclusion

Contra Costa's current Suspension Policy was approved by the Board of Supervisors on February 10, 2026:

https://ccclib.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/72/2026/03/Suspension-Policy_ADA.pdf

The policy establishes escalating suspension periods and can impose systemwide exclusions from all Contra Costa County Library facilities for qualifying misconduct.

For longer suspensions, the published policy provides formal appeal or review procedures. The published policy does not state the same formal appeal procedure for suspensions of one week or less.

## Due process is duration- and context-sensitive

Persuasive authority includes Neinast v. Board of Trustees of Columbus Metropolitan Library, 346 F.3d 585 (6th Cir. 2003):

https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/346/585/510700/

On the facts of a one-day eviction, the Sixth Circuit found adequate process where the patron was told the charge and had an opportunity to give his side. It did not announce one procedure for every library suspension.

A nonbinding Eastern District of California screening decision, Van Den Heuvel v. Dorothy, treated a weeks-to-month countywide library exclusion as plausibly implicating a liberty interest at the pleading stage:

https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/district-courts/california/caedce/2%3A2021cv02176/403475/24/

**Current statewide framework:** Sweep 014 now supplies the general prospective-exclusion analysis. Binding Ninth Circuit *Reza* requires attention to the factual basis, supported future risk, scope, duration, and alternative access of a government-forum ban. General procedural due process remains context-sensitive under *Mathews*. No single California/Ninth Circuit case sets one mandatory procedure for every library suspension length and circumstance.

See [Sweep 014, Public-Agency Exclusion, Prospective Trespass, and Identification](../research/SWEEP-014-PUBLIC-EXCLUSION-PROSPECTIVE-TRESPASS-ID.md).

For an actual exclusion, record:

- immediate removal versus prospective suspension;
- duration;
- one branch versus systemwide scope;
- stated conduct;
- written notice;
- opportunity to respond;
- appeal/review mechanism;
- emergency/safety rationale;
- whether later access is conditioned on reentry procedures.

# 13. A suspension policy does not finish the trespass analysis

Contra Costa's Suspension Policy states that a suspended person who returns before the return date will be asked to leave, police will be contacted if the person refuses, and the person is subject to arrest for trespass under California Penal Code section 602.

The policy does not identify a particular section 602 subdivision or walk through its elements.

That creates an important criminal-bridge issue. Sweep 014 researched the statewide branch and preserves one specific boundary as **RESEARCHED-UNRESOLVED**: whether an individualized prior exclusion alone makes otherwise generally open property `not open to the general public` under § 602(o).

This does not mean an arrest after reentry would necessarily be unlawful. It means the public-facing suspension policy is not itself the complete criminal-law analysis, and a different statute/access fact may independently resolve the encounter.

For an actual event:

1. identify the administrative suspension and its scope;
2. identify the authority to order removal;
3. identify the specific Penal Code subdivision;
4. test every statutory element against the actual area and access facts;
5. separately analyze any detention or arrest under the Fourth Amendment.

See [Trespass and Removal](trespass-removal.md).

# 14. Special-access periods can change the access analysis

Contra Costa currently operates Self-Service Sundays at **Concord and San Pablo**, with Pittsburg listed as coming soon. Access requires advance enrollment, a library card in good standing, orientation, a signed user agreement, and card/PIN entry. Library staff are not present during the self-service period.

Current program page:

https://ccclib.org/self-service-sundays/

That is not the same access condition as ordinary staffed public hours.

A branch Registry entry should distinguish:

- ordinary staffed hours;
- special card/enrollment-controlled access;
- room-only after-hours access;
- closed hours;
- temporary closures;
- emergency restrictions.

The legal question begins with the access condition that actually existed at the time.

# 15. Contra Costa branch research protocol

After the system-level sweep, a branch investigation should answer these facts before any field test:

| Field | Branch question |
| --- | --- |
| Building/property control | County-owned, city-owned, leased, shared, or mixed? |
| Exterior | Ordinary public sidewalk/plaza or library-controlled approach? |
| Hours | Normal staffed hours and any special-access periods |
| Entrance | Open entry, card access, security, check-in, or other condition? |
| Main floor | Reading/stacks/service layout and ordinary public circulation |
| Designated areas | Children's, teen, computer, quiet, maker, or other purpose-limited spaces |
| Rooms | Study/meeting/community rooms; controller and booking authority |
| Display channels | What public materials may be posted/distributed and where? |
| Restrooms | Publicly accessible or conditioned? |
| Restricted areas | Staff doors, workrooms, secured spaces |
| Recording rule | Posted sign, written policy, staff instruction, or none located? |
| Removal authority | Who may order a person from the area and under what rule? |
| Criminal bridge | Exact statute/subdivision if law enforcement is invoked |
| Prior enforcement | Complaints, exclusions, police calls, bodycam/CAD/incident records |
| Current flags | Supported, unresolved, facial/as-applied issue, comparator, criminal-authority gap |

The first branch should be selected for **research value and reproducibility**, not because it appears likely to create a confrontation.

These are system-level findings. They are not a Registry status for every Contra Costa library branch.

# 17. What remains unresolved

The next research layer should address:

- California/Ninth Circuit law governing peaceful photography/videography in ordinary public-library interiors;
- the status of peaceful governmental observation and information gathering under a "library activity" rule;
- California Speech Clause treatment of library interiors and opened library channels;
- First Amendment retaliation in a library-recording context;
- procedural due process for short, intermediate, and long systemwide exclusions;
- exact California trespass theories after a valid or disputed library suspension;
- the constitutional status of current Contra Costa display/distribution restrictions;
- branch-specific property and room control;
- privacy issues tied to minors, computers, records transactions, and sensitive patron activity;
- seizure/search of a camera or phone if an encounter escalates;
- historical enforcement and chilling at the pilot branches, including warnings, removals, suspensions, police calls, citations/arrests/prosecutions, and warning-only events involving leafleting, solicitation, recording, petitioning, or similar expressive activity.

The pilot Registry should not infer a clean historical record merely because no reported case exists. Use the [Historical Enforcement & Chilling Review](facility-investigation-methodology.md#historical-enforcement-and-chilling-review) and preserve any resulting scoped Rights-Risk Signal separately from the current policy/forum classification.

Until those questions are resolved, use **UNRESOLVED** rather than fill gaps with assumptions.
