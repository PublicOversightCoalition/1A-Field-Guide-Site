# Public-Forum Doctrine

**Research status:** Core federal forum architecture, Ninth Circuit government-building doctrine, the principal California Speech Clause overlay, and the practical activity-by-forum matrix have been reviewed. Facility classifications remain area-specific and are maintained in the Facility Registry rather than inferred categorically from a building type.

## Short field rule

**Public ownership is not the same thing as a public forum, and public access is not the same thing as a public forum.**

Classify the **specific place or channel of communication actually at issue**. A single government property can contain areas with different forum classifications and different rules.

Under the federal First Amendment:

- traditional public forums, such as ordinary public streets, parks, and sidewalks, receive the strongest forum protection;
- government may intentionally create a designated public forum and then is generally held to the same forum standard while it remains open;
- a limited public forum is opened only for specified speakers or subjects; restrictions within the forum must fit the forum's purpose and be reasonable and viewpoint neutral;
- nonpublic government property is not opened by tradition or designation for general expressive activity; restrictions still must be reasonable in light of the forum's purpose and viewpoint neutral.

California's Speech Clause can be broader than the First Amendment in some settings. Do **not** assume the federal classification automatically resolves the California constitutional question.

## What the classification means for actual activity

The forum label is only useful if it answers the next question: **what kind of expression am I trying to engage in?**

The Field Guide now maintains a separate practical matrix for common activity:

- speech / conversation;
- signs / display;
- leaflets / literature;
- petitioning;
- assembly / demonstration;
- solicitation;
- photography / video / audio recording;
- public comment;
- tabling / physical setup;
- expressive clothing; and
- press / information gathering.

Use [What Can I Actually Do in Each Forum?](forum-activity-guide.md) for that activity-by-activity analysis.

The shortest operational distinction is:

> **TPF:** general expression is ordinarily within the forum's historic function, subject to valid restrictions.  
> **LPF:** ask whether the speaker, subject, and activity fit the channel the government opened.  
> **NPF:** government may preserve the property for its intended function, but restrictions still require the applicable reasonableness and viewpoint-neutrality support.  
> **Recording:** run the recording analysis separately in every forum.

# 1. Define the forum before classifying it

The first question is not merely, `What building am I in?` It is:

> **What exact property, room, walkway, meeting period, bulletin board, display space, or other channel is the speaker seeking to use for what expressive activity?**

In *Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Defense & Educational Fund*, the Supreme Court instructed courts to identify the forum by looking to the access sought. Where only limited access is sought, the forum can be narrower than the whole government property.

The Ninth Circuit applied the same area-specific approach in *Camenzind v. California Exposition & State Fair*. The court separately analyzed the exterior unticketed area and the enclosed ticketed event area because they were physically separated and governed by different policies.

**Field consequence:** Do not say `the courthouse is a public forum` or `the DMV is a nonpublic forum` without identifying the area and activity. The front public sidewalk, exterior plaza, lobby, service counter, employee corridor, hearing room, and meeting-comment period can present different questions.

Authorities:

- *Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Defense & Educational Fund, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 788 (1985).
- *Camenzind v. California Exposition & State Fair*, 84 F.4th 1102 (9th Cir. 2023).

---

# 2. Federal forum categories

## Traditional public forum

Traditional public forums are places that historically have been devoted to assembly, debate, and public communication. The canonical examples are ordinary public streets, sidewalks, and parks.

A government generally may impose content-neutral time/place/manner rules in such a forum if the rules are narrowly tailored to serve a significant governmental interest and leave open ample alternative channels of communication. Content-based exclusions ordinarily face strict scrutiny, and viewpoint discrimination is forbidden.

Authorities:

- *Perry Education Association v. Perry Local Educators' Association*, 460 U.S. 37, 45-46 (1983).
- *United States v. Grace*, 461 U.S. 171 (1983).
- *Ward v. Rock Against Racism*, 491 U.S. 781, 791 (1989).

### Government-building boundary warning

The public sidewalk outside or around a government building can be a traditional public forum even though the building interior is not. In *Grace*, the sidewalks forming the perimeter of the Supreme Court grounds were treated differently from the Court grounds/building. Physical proximity to a sensitive government facility does not automatically erase the forum character of an ordinary public sidewalk.

At the same time, a walkway that merely resembles a sidewalk is not automatically a traditional public forum. *United States v. Kokinda* illustrates that the purpose, location, physical characteristics, and use of the particular walkway matter.

---

## Designated public forum

Government can intentionally open property that is not a traditional public forum for public expressive activity. While the government maintains that designation, restrictions are generally analyzed under the same standards that govern a traditional public forum.

Intent matters. Government does not create a designated public forum merely by allowing some speech, by tolerating occasional expressive activity, or by opening property for ordinary government business.

Authorities:

- *Perry*, 460 U.S. at 45-46.
- *Cornelius*, 473 U.S. at 802.
- *Pleasant Grove City v. Summum*, 555 U.S. 460, 469-70 (2009).

---

## Limited public forum

A limited public forum is opened only for certain groups or for discussion of certain subjects. In the Ninth Circuit, restrictions must be **reasonable in light of the forum's purpose and viewpoint neutral**.

This classification frequently matters for public meetings, school or university channels, government-created message spaces, and other contexts in which government invites some private speech without opening the forum for every speaker and every topic.

In *Norse v. City of Santa Cruz*, 629 F.3d 966 (9th Cir. 2010) (en banc), the Ninth Circuit held that a city-council meeting opened to public participation is a limited public forum. The court rejected the claim that all First Amendment protection disappears after public comment ends and emphasized viewpoint neutrality and actual disruption in the meeting-removal context.

The category can be confusing because Supreme Court and circuit opinions have not always used identical terminology. The Field Guide therefore records both the label and the actual governing rule instead of relying on the label alone.

Authorities:

- *Christian Legal Society Chapter of the University of California, Hastings College of the Law v. Martinez*, 561 U.S. 661 (2010).
- *Wright v. Incline Village General Improvement District*, 665 F.3d 1128 (9th Cir. 2011).
- *Hopper v. City of Pasco*, 241 F.3d 1067 (9th Cir. 2001).
- *Norse v. City of Santa Cruz*, 629 F.3d 966 (9th Cir. 2010) (en banc).

---

## Nonpublic forum

Government property does not become a public forum simply because members of the public may enter it.

In a nonpublic forum, government may reserve property for its intended use and may draw distinctions based on subject matter or speaker identity when the distinctions are **reasonable in light of the forum's purpose and viewpoint neutral**.

That is a lower level of scrutiny than applies in a traditional/designated public forum, but it is not `no First Amendment protection`.

Authorities:

- *Perry*, 460 U.S. at 46.
- *Cornelius*, 473 U.S. at 806.
- *Minnesota Voters Alliance v. Mansky*, 585 U.S. 1 (2018).
- *Sammartano v. First Judicial District Court*, 303 F.3d 959 (9th Cir. 2002).

### Reasonableness still has content

In *Sammartano*, the Ninth Circuit agreed that a judicial/municipal complex was a nonpublic forum. It nevertheless held, at the preliminary-injunction stage, that challenged expressive-clothing restrictions were likely invalid because the record did not adequately connect the restrictions to the asserted safety/order interests and at least one restriction was viewpoint discriminatory.

**Field rule:** `Nonpublic forum` does not mean officials may prohibit expression for any reason they choose. Ask how the restriction relates to the purpose of the area and whether comparable viewpoints are treated alike.

*Mansky* likewise shows that a nonpublic-forum rule must supply an objective, workable standard rather than leaving officials without a sensible basis for distinguishing permitted from prohibited expression.

---

# 3. A government building can be open to the public and still be a nonpublic forum

This is the distinction most likely to matter in an ordinary audit.

In *Sammartano*, the Ninth Circuit agreed that a complex used for county and judicial business was a nonpublic forum. Its function was conducting governmental/court business, not serving as a place traditionally or intentionally opened for assembly and debate.

That proposition must be used carefully:

- *Sammartano* does **not** hold that every government lobby in California is categorically a nonpublic forum;
- it does **not** mean a nonpublic forum is exempt from the First Amendment;
- it does **not** mean any employee can order any visitor out for any reason;
- it does **not** convert a policy violation automatically into criminal trespass;
- it does show why `the lobby is open to the public` is not enough, standing alone, to prove traditional/designated-public-forum status.

This directly complements the trespass chapter's distinction between **access status**, **forum status**, **facility policy**, and **criminal authority**.

Authority:

- *Sammartano*, 303 F.3d 959.

For the dedicated lobby/foyer/waiting-area analysis, including recording and privacy claims, see [Indoor Public Common Spaces](indoor-public-common-spaces.md).

---

# 4. Viewpoint discrimination is a separate red flag

Forum classification determines how much subject-matter control government has. It does not generally authorize viewpoint discrimination against private speakers within an otherwise permitted subject.

A rule or enforcement decision can be viewpoint discriminatory when government allows discussion of a subject but disfavors one side, perspective, affiliation, or opinion on that subject.

In a nonpublic or limited forum, government may often limit the forum to its intended subjects or classes of speakers. But once a subject is within the forum's permissible scope, excluding a speaker merely because officials oppose the speaker's viewpoint is constitutionally suspect.

Authorities:

- *Cornelius*, 473 U.S. at 806, 812.
- *Rosenberger v. Rector & Visitors of the University of Virginia*, 515 U.S. 819, 828-30 (1995).
- *Sammartano*, 303 F.3d at 970-72.

**Evidence-preservation consequence:** If an official says a rule applies because the recorder is `anti-police,` `an auditor,` critical of the agency, supportive of the agency, or otherwise because of the message being expressed, preserve that statement. It can matter independently of the ultimate forum classification.

---

# 5. Content-neutral time/place/manner restrictions

In a traditional or designated public forum, government may impose reasonable content-neutral time/place/manner restrictions. Under *Ward*, the familiar test asks whether the restriction:

1. is justified without reference to the content of the regulated speech;
2. is narrowly tailored to serve a significant governmental interest; and
3. leaves open ample alternative channels for communication.

`Narrowly tailored` in this context does not mean the government must always choose the least restrictive conceivable alternative. But the restriction may not burden substantially more speech than necessary to further the interest.

This doctrine can validate genuine safety, pedestrian-flow, congestion, noise, or operational rules while still requiring a real fit between the asserted interest and the restriction.

Authorities:

- *Ward*, 491 U.S. 781.
- *Camenzind*, 84 F.4th 1102.
- *Prigmore v. City of Redding*, 211 Cal.App.4th 1322 (2012).

# 6. California Constitution: analyze separately

California Constitution article I, section 2(a) states an independent liberty-of-speech guarantee.

California doctrine is not simply a copy of the federal public-forum test. California courts have recognized speech rights in some privately controlled properties that would not be public forums under the federal First Amendment, most famously certain shopping-center settings.

For the Field Guide's government-property focus, two cautions are especially important.

## California's government-property forum test is not perfectly settled

In *International Society for Krishna Consciousness of California, Inc. v. City of Los Angeles*, 48 Cal.4th 446 (2010), the California Supreme Court expressly declined to decide whether LAX was a public forum under the California Speech Clause because the solicitation restriction at issue was valid even if the relevant public areas were assumed to be public forums.

In *Camenzind*, the Ninth Circuit therefore noted that California's forum standard differs from the federal test and that the state-law test has generated uncertainty. The panel held that the exterior, unticketed area at Cal Expo was a public forum under the California Speech Clause and that the enclosed, ticketed portion during a privately organized festival was nonpublic. The panel also expressly acknowledged that, absent controlling California Supreme Court authority on the point, it was predicting how that court would decide the state-law question. Judge VanDyke dissented from part of that state-law analysis.

**Project status consequence:** Treat *Camenzind* as binding published Ninth Circuit authority for federal courts within the circuit, while separately identifying its California-law classification as a federal appellate prediction of unsettled state constitutional law rather than pretending the California Supreme Court itself announced that exact test.

## Do not use the old `basic incompatibility` test as the classification rule

An older California Court of Appeal decision, *U.C. Nuclear Weapons Labs Conversion Project v. Lawrence Livermore Laboratory*, described government property on a continuum and asked whether proposed expressive activity was basically incompatible with the normal activity of the place. Older Ninth Circuit decisions, including *Carreras* and *Kuba*, adopted that formulation when predicting California law.

That is now a research trap. In **San Leandro Teachers Association v. Governing Board, 46 Cal.4th 822 (2009)**, the California Supreme Court observed that the `basic incompatibility` formulation had not appeared in California appellate cases since *U.C. Weapons Labs* and noted that *Grayned* used incompatibility to evaluate a restriction **after** forum status had been determined, not to classify the forum itself. *Camenzind* later described the California Supreme Court as having declined to adopt that classification test and warned that the older Ninth Circuit cases applying it rest on a doubtful foundation.

**Field Guide rule:** Do not classify a California property as a public forum merely because the proposed speech seems compatible with the property's normal activity. Use current controlling California authority and the more recent guideposts identified here; where the state-law classification remains unsettled, mark it `UNRESOLVED`.

*San Leandro* is also useful for a narrower point. The California Supreme Court treated internal school mailboxes as nonpublic under federal forum doctrine and then separately rejected the claim that the California Speech Clause required a different result on those facts. California's broader speech clause therefore does not automatically convert every restricted government communication channel into a public forum.

*Prigmore v. City of Redding*, 211 Cal.App.4th 1322 (2012), later declined to apply the old `basic incompatibility` formulation and instead analyzed the outdoor grounds of the Redding Municipal Library using the federal forum framework. On its property-specific record, the court treated the public open space at the entrance, the entry/exit-door area, and the adjacent parking lot as a traditional public forum. That holding is useful California authority for library exteriors, but it does not classify every library exterior, or the library interior, the same way. See `public-libraries.md`.

Authorities:

- *San Leandro Teachers Ass'n v. Governing Board*, 46 Cal.4th 822 (2009).
- *U.C. Nuclear Weapons Labs Conversion Project v. Lawrence Livermore Laboratory*, 154 Cal.App.3d 1157 (1984), historical context; do not use its `basic incompatibility` formulation as the current classification rule.
- *Camenzind*, 84 F.4th 1102.

## California can protect more speech without making every public-facing place a public forum

Cases such as *Robins v. Pruneyard Shopping Center*, *Ralphs Grocery Co. v. UFCW Local 8*, and *Park Management Corp. v. In Defense of Animals* show that California's Speech Clause uses a broader and fact-sensitive concept in some private-property settings. Those cases are important context, but they should not be converted into the slogan `California says every place open to the public is a public forum.`

Authorities:

- Cal. Const. art. I, § 2(a).
- *International Society for Krishna Consciousness of California, Inc. v. City of Los Angeles*, 48 Cal.4th 446 (2010).
- *Robins v. Pruneyard Shopping Center*, 23 Cal.3d 899 (1979), aff'd, 447 U.S. 74 (1980).
- *San Leandro Teachers Ass'n v. Governing Board*, 46 Cal.4th 822 (2009).
- *Ralphs Grocery Co. v. United Food & Commercial Workers Union Local 8*, 55 Cal.4th 1083 (2012).
- *Park Management Corp. v. In Defense of Animals*, 36 Cal.App.5th 649 (2019).
- *Camenzind*, 84 F.4th 1102.

# 7. Forum doctrine does not by itself answer the right-to-record question

Forum doctrine regulates expressive use of government property, but a recording dispute can involve additional doctrines:

- whether recording the particular subject is protected First Amendment activity;
- whether a facility rule is directed at speech/press activity, conduct, privacy, security, court administration, or another interest;
- whether the recorder is in a place they have a right to be;
- whether a statute independently protects recording, such as Penal Code § 148(g) in its scope or open-meeting statutes;
- whether the location is governed by a specialized statute, court rule, school rule, security regulation, or agency policy;
- whether the person is actually interfering with operations;
- whether exclusion/removal has an independent lawful basis.

Accordingly:

> **Forum classification is an important layer, not a universal recording permission slip or prohibition.**

The recording, public-meeting, trespass/removal, police-encounter, and facility-specific chapters must still be consulted.

---

# 8. Forum doctrine does not itself create criminal trespass

A First Amendment conclusion and a criminal-trespass conclusion are different questions.

For example:

1. an area may be a nonpublic forum;
2. a viewpoint-neutral facility restriction may be constitutionally permissible there;
3. a visitor may violate the restriction;
4. but the state must still identify the actual legal authority supporting exclusion, detention, citation, or arrest.

Conversely, a person's conduct can satisfy an independently valid trespass statute even if the person is engaged in some expressive activity. The constitutional and statutory questions have to be analyzed together rather than collapsed into either slogan.

See `trespass-removal.md` for Penal Code §§ 602 and 602.1.

# 9. Facility-registry classification protocol

For every future registry entry, classify **areas**, not merely facilities.

Minimum forum fields:

| Field | What to record |
| --- | --- |
| Facility | Agency/building name |
| Specific area | Public sidewalk, plaza, parking lot, lobby, counter queue, hearing room, corridor, secured area, etc. |
| Access status | Open to general public, appointment-only, screened, ticketed, employee-only, closed hours, etc. |
| Federal forum classification | TPF / designated / limited / nonpublic / unresolved |
| California Speech Clause classification | Public / nonpublic / unresolved; do not assume federal result controls |
| Authority | Controlling case/statute/rule/policy supporting classification |
| Expressive activity analyzed | Filming, leafletting, signs, public comment, solicitation, conversation, etc. |
| Restrictions | Actual rule and source |
| Confidence | HIGH / MEDIUM / LOW / UNRESOLVED |
| Last verified | Date |

A registry entry should use `UNRESOLVED` rather than infer a forum classification merely from a building's ownership or the presence of a public service counter.

# 10. Field questions during a forum/access dispute

These questions are primarily for preserving the factual record:

- What exact area are you saying has a restriction?
- Is this area open to members of the public for agency business?
- What rule or policy are you enforcing?
- May I see or obtain a copy of that rule?
- Does the rule prohibit recording, all expressive activity, or only particular conduct?
- Is the same rule applied regardless of the speaker's viewpoint?
- What conduct are you saying is disrupting or interfering with operations?
- Are you ordering me to leave this specific area or the entire property?
- What legal authority supports that removal order?

These are not magic phrases and do not require officials to litigate constitutional doctrine on scene. Their value is factual clarity for later review.

# 11. Doctrine-reviewed propositions from Sweep 005

The following narrow propositions have reached `DOCTRINE-REVIEWED` or `DOCTRINE-REVIEWED-IN-PART` status:

1. **Specific forum first:** forum analysis can be area/channel-specific rather than building-wide.
2. **Public access ≠ public forum:** public entry for government business does not itself establish a traditional/designated public forum.
3. **Traditional forum baseline:** ordinary public streets, parks, and sidewalks are canonical traditional public forums, subject to context-specific boundary analysis.
4. **Designated forum requires intentional opening:** government does not create one merely by permitting isolated speech.
5. **Limited forum:** restrictions must be reasonable in light of forum purpose and viewpoint neutral.
6. **Nonpublic forum:** restrictions must be reasonable in light of forum purpose and viewpoint neutral; nonpublic does not mean no First Amendment protection.
7. **Government-building example:** *Sammartano* treated the judicial/municipal complex before it as nonpublic but, at the preliminary-injunction stage, found the challengers likely to succeed against restrictions that lacked adequate reasonableness support and included viewpoint discrimination.
8. **California separate analysis:** California's Speech Clause can differ from the federal test; *ISKCON* left LAX classification unresolved, and *Camenzind* contains a published Ninth Circuit prediction/application of California law with a partial dissent.
9. **Forum ≠ trespass:** forum classification does not itself establish criminal trespass or removal authority.
10. **Forum ≠ recording doctrine:** recording disputes may require additional constitutional, statutory, privacy, security, and facility-specific analysis.

# 12. Still unresolved

Sweep 005 does **not** yet establish categorical forum answers for:

- ordinary city/county administrative lobbies;
- police and sheriff lobbies;
- DMV and other state-agency service centers;
- public-library branches and particular library areas beyond the initial Contra Costa system sweep;
- courthouse interiors, courtrooms, clerk windows, and exterior courthouse plazas beyond authorities already identified;
- public college/university grounds and building interiors;
- K-12 grounds;
- transit stations and platforms;
- public hospital/clinic spaces;
- parking lots as a general category;
- government social-media pages or other digital forums;
- government employee workspaces;
- temporary/private events on public property beyond the facts and holdings of reviewed cases.

Those belong in facility-specific sweeps. The registry should mark them `UNRESOLVED` until supported by adequate authority.
