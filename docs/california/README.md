## Federal-first reading order

This handbook is a **California overlay on the nationwide federal baseline**, not a substitute for it. For any field question, use this order:

1. Start with [Constitution First](../constitution-first.md) and the relevant [National core](../national-core/complaint-driven-police-encounter.md) doctrine.
2. Identify the controlling federal constitutional rule and the facts that make that rule applicable.
3. Add California constitutional provisions, statutes, regulations, and cases only where they validly supplement the federal baseline, provide additional protection, impose a lawful state-specific restriction, or otherwise change the practical analysis.
4. Add county, city, agency, facility, or property-specific rules only after the superior federal and state layers are identified.

A state rule that appears more restrictive does **not** automatically control merely because it is state law. The handbook must distinguish a valid state-law overlay from a restriction that would conflict with controlling federal constitutional law, federal preemption, or another superior rule. Likewise, a California rule that provides greater protection should be identified as an additional state protection rather than mistaken for the source of the underlying federal right.

The working sequence is therefore: **federal constitutional baseline → nationwide doctrine → California overlay → local/facility application**.

## Standing scenario

The canonical factual baseline is now [NC-STANDING-01 v1](../national-core/standing-scenario.md).

California's [standing-scenario.md](standing-scenario.md) is the jurisdiction overlay/crosswalk. It maps the neutral facts to California Penal Code, Brown Act, Ninth Circuit, identification, trespass/removal, assembly, and recording doctrine.

Unless a scenario says otherwise, the person begins lawfully present at the specific vantage/area, peacefully gathers information, and has no silent fighting, threats, unreasonable noise, obstruction, intimidation, restricted-area entry, unlawful-assembly participation, assumed credential, or other invented misconduct.

Another person's objection or anger does not silently change those facts. Later orders, closures, detention, warnings, restrictions, or changed conduct create new branches that California law then analyzes.

The handbook does not depend on professional status or credentials. Identification demands remain a separate legal question rather than an assumed duty built into the baseline.

## Federal premises in California

Federal premises are maintained separately under `docs/federal/`.

For a federal facility located in California, the project does **not** assume that the California Penal Code, CPRA, or other California facility rules automatically govern merely because the building is physically in California. Federal-premises research begins with federal constitutional law, the applicable federal statute/regulation, agency/facility rules, and federal cases. California law is checked only when a specific jurisdictional or incorporation doctrine makes it relevant.

This separation prevents the California state/local handbook from being accidentally treated as the governing conduct or records code for USPS property, federal courthouses, GSA-controlled buildings, or other federal premises.

## Field-rule philosophy

A field rule should be short enough to remember but narrow enough to be defensible.

Bad field rule:

> If the public can walk into the lobby, it is a public forum.

Better field rule:

> Public access and public-forum status are different questions. Classify the specific area and activity. A public-facing government interior can be a nonpublic or limited forum, while an adjacent ordinary public sidewalk can have a different classification. See `public-forum-doctrine.md`.

Bad field rule:

> There is a "No Recording" sign, so recording here is illegal.

Better field rule:

> A sign is evidence that a restriction is being asserted; it is not proof of the restriction's legal validity or criminal enforceability. Identify the written policy, who adopted it, the authority supporting it, the specific area/activity it governs, and any independent removal or criminal statute. See `facility-investigation-methodology.md`.

Bad field rule:

> The agency owns the building, so any employee can make rules for visitors.

Better field rule:

> Identify who adopted the rule, the Constitution/statute/charter/enabling law that gives the entity power, the instrument used to adopt it, any delegation to the employee or security actor enforcing it, superior-law preemption, and the separate removal or criminal-enforcement bridge. See `restriction-audit.md` and Sweep 013.

Bad field rule:

> Public property means they cannot trespass you.

Better field rule:

> Identify the actual trespass provision. For a public agency open to the public, Penal Code § 602.1(b) requires intentional interference by obstruction or intimidation plus refusal after the qualifying request; § 602(o) instead concerns property not open to the general public; § 602(q) concerns a public building when it is regularly closed. Other law or a lawful facility restriction may independently matter.

Bad field rule:

> They trespassed me once, so returning to any public part of the building is automatically criminal.

Better field rule:

> Separate the administrative exclusion from the later criminal offense. Check the order's issuing authority, scope, duration, current access facts, and the Penal Code provision actually asserted on reentry. California's § 602(o) effect of an individualized prior ban on otherwise generally open property remains Researched-Unresolved. See `trespass-removal.md` and Sweep 014.

Bad field rule:

> If police detain me in California, I always have to show physical ID.

Better field rule:

> A request, verbal identity, carrying a document, producing a document, a search for ID, false identification, citation, and booking are different questions. Sweep 014 did not locate a general pedestrian carry-ID statute; `Landeros` bars refusal from manufacturing suspicion where none otherwise exists, while `Vanegas` leaves the broader otherwise-lawful-detention affirmative-ID issue unresolved.

Bad field rule:

> Someone complained that I was loud, so that proves disturbing the peace.

Better field rule:

> Identify the actual § 415 subsection and conduct. For communicative noise, *In re Brown* narrows § 415(2) to imminent-violence danger or use of purported communication as a guise to disrupt lawful endeavors; separate valid facility volume rules may still apply.

Bad field rule:

> Swearing at or criticizing a police officer is automatically disorderly conduct or obstruction.

Better field rule:

> Profanity, criticism, and verbal challenge to police receive substantial First Amendment protection. Fighting words, genuine threats, challenges to fight, or conduct that actually delays/obstructs a lawful police duty are separate changed-fact branches. See `disorderly-conduct.md` and Sweep 016.

Bad field rule:

> Staff said I was harassing customers, so § 602.1 is established.

Better field rule:

> Translate "harassing" into conduct. Public-agency § 602.1(b) requires intentional interference by obstruction or intimidation plus the qualifying leave request/refusal; refusal does not create a missing interference predicate.

Bad field rule:

> Police declared an unlawful assembly, so everyone nearby became a participant.

Better field rule:

> Separate § 408 participation from § 409 remaining. California's lawful-purpose unlawful-assembly branch is constitutionally narrowed to actual violence or imminent danger; participation requires knowing participation, while a lawful § 409 warning can reach nonparticipants who willfully remain once its predicates exist.

Bad field rule:

> If there is police tape, crossing or remaining behind it is automatically § 409.5.

Better field rule:

> Identify the actual closure authority and statutory predicates. A § 409.5 calamity/command-post closure has specific source, scope, notice, and criminal elements; specialized closure statutes can use different wording.

Bad field rule:

> Only credentialed press have a First Amendment right to record a protest or police activity.

Better field rule:

> Special California statutes can give qualifying news representatives additional access/protection, but ordinary constitutional recording and newsgathering analysis is separate. § 409.7's undefined media-qualification terms should not be converted into a universal credential requirement or automatic self-publisher exemption.

Bad field rule:

> Riley means police can never take my phone.

Better field rule:

> Separate physical seizure from digital search. Police may sometimes lawfully secure or seize a phone, but the initial seizure needs its own authority, continued retention must remain reasonable, and *Riley* generally requires a warrant or another recognized exception before searching digital contents. See `police-encounters.md` and Sweep 015.

Bad field rule:

> If I offer to show police my video, they can browse the rest of my phone.

Better field rule:

> Consent has a scope. Offering one identified recording does not automatically equal consent to inspect unrelated messages, photos, accounts, or files. State the limit clearly and preserve whether the device was later taken by order rather than consent.

Bad field rule:

> Police reports and 911 records are exempt, so there is nothing I can get.

Better field rule:

> Separate the underlying investigatory record from mandatory public information. If § 7923.600 applies, §§ 7923.610 and 7923.615 can still require specified arrest, complaint/request, response, and incident information; later statutes can also require particular recordings or officer records.

Bad field rule:

> All bodycam footage becomes public after 45 days.

Better field rule:

> Government Code § 7923.625's specialized timeline applies to recordings depicting a defined critical incident—officer firearm discharge at a person or force causing death/GBI. Other BWC footage follows other CPRA rules, and retention under Penal Code § 832.18 is a separate question.

Bad field rule:

> Officer personnel records are confidential, period.

Better field rule:

> Start with current Penal Code § 832.7. It preserves confidentiality generally but expressly makes enumerated incident and sustained-misconduct categories public, with its own redaction and delay rules. Older confidentiality cases cannot be applied as though those amendments never occurred.

Bad field rule:

> The employee used a personal phone, so the recording is private.

Better field rule:

> Under *City of San Jose*, a work-related writing does not lose potential public-record status merely because it is created or stored on a personal account/device. Analyze content, context, purpose, scope of employment, disclosure exemptions, and the agency's search duty separately.

Bad field rule:

> I requested the video, so deleting it automatically violates the CPRA.

Better field rule:

> *City of Gilroy* holds that the CPRA itself is not a general retention statute. Give a preservation notice, promptly request the record and applicable retention schedule, and distinguish lawful routine disposition from other retention law, litigation-preservation duties, or deliberate destruction.

Bad field rule:

> Recording police is always legal.

Better field rule:

> Penal Code § 148(g) expressly says recording a public/peace officer in public, or from a place the recorder has a right to be, does not **by itself** establish § 148 obstruction, reasonable suspicion, or probable cause.

Bad field rule:

> If I am detained, police have to read me Miranda rights.

Better field rule:

> A Terry detention is a Fourth Amendment seizure but is not automatically Miranda custody. Analyze the justification for the detention separately from whether custodial interrogation rules have attached.

Bad field rule:

> *Hiibel* says I have to identify whenever police detain me.

Better field rule:

> *Hiibel* upheld Nevada's specific stop-and-identify statute during a valid Terry stop. In California, identify the particular statute, context, and applicable case law before treating an ID request as compulsory, and never provide a false identity.

The guide should teach the actual elements, not magic phrases.
