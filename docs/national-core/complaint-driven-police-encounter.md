# Complaint-Driven Police Encounter: Disputed Trespass and Immediately Available Exculpatory Evidence

**Canonical scenario ID:** `NC-COMPLAINT-01`

**Standing baseline:** [NC-STANDING-01 v1](standing-scenario.md). Unless this scenario expressly changes a fact, the canonical standing facts remain in force. Complaint labels do not silently override those facts.

This national-core scenario addresses a recurring encounter that is structurally similar across jurisdictions even though the underlying trespass, disorderly-conduct, obstruction, false-report, and exclusion statutes vary by state.

The recurring pattern is:

> A person is peacefully recording or observing from a place they believe they may lawfully be. A private citizen, employee, security worker, or government employee objects. The recorder may never have entered the disputed property, may have entered and already left when requested, may still be present after a request to leave, or may only have received prospective notice not to enter. The complainant calls police and characterizes the recorder as trespassing, hostile, escalating, disruptive, threatening, suspicious, or otherwise criminal. Police arrive while some or all of the encounter has been continuously recorded.

The national core supplies the Fourth Amendment, First Amendment, evidence-handling, and caller-reliability framework. Each jurisdiction overlay supplies the actual elements of trespass, disorderly conduct, obstruction/interference, false reporting, misuse of emergency services, and any local exclusion authority.

This scenario does **not** presume that the complainant lied, that the recorder is correct about the property boundary, or that police lack reasonable suspicion when they arrive. It requires those propositions to be tested rather than assumed.

---

## Core principle

A complaint is **information to evaluate**, not a legal conclusion.

The encounter must be reduced to:

`exact location → alleged facts → source/reliability → actual offense elements → initial RS/PC → contradictory evidence → continuing RS/PC → disposition`

Labels such as `trespasser`, `hostile`, `escalating`, `disruptive`, `suspicious`, or `threatening` should be translated into observable conduct before they are treated as evidence of an offense.

The question is not:

> Did somebody use a crime-like word?

It is:

> What did the caller say actually happened, how does the caller claim to know it, what offense could those facts satisfy, and what information did police possess when they acted?

---

# 1. Fix the physical location first

Property location can be outcome-determinative.

Document separately:

- where the recorder was when the disputed conduct allegedly occurred;
- whether the recorder ever crossed the relevant property boundary;
- whether the place that visually resembles a public sidewalk is actually public right-of-way or privately owned frontage;
- whether the recorder was inside an area open to the public;
- whether the recorder had already left before police arrival;
- where the recorder was standing when the complainant called;
- where the recorder was standing when police made contact.

Do not silently convert:

> person standing on public right-of-way adjacent to property

into:

> person presently trespassing on that property.

Likewise, do not assume that an area is public property merely because members of the public commonly walk through it. Property ownership, public access, and public-forum status are separate questions.

---

# 2. “Trespassed” must be decomposed into four different factual postures

The phrase `you have been trespassed` is too ambiguous for legal analysis.

Every jurisdiction overlay must separately analyze at least these four postures.

## NC-COMPLAINT-01.A: Never entered the disputed property

The recorder remained outside the relevant property boundary.

Questions:

- Does the state's asserted trespass offense require entry, occupation, presence, or remaining?
- What facts support an inference that entry occurred?
- Is the complainant describing actual location or merely using the label `trespassing`?
- Is immediately available video or other objective evidence capable of resolving the boundary/location question?

A prospective warning not to enter does not retroactively establish that a prior trespass occurred.

## NC-COMPLAINT-01.B: Entered, then left when requested

The recorder entered an area but departed after a qualifying request to leave.

Questions:

- Was the initial entry lawful or privileged?
- Does the jurisdiction's offense require refusal/failure to leave after notice?
- Did any completed offense occur before the request?
- Was the request made by a person legally authorized to give it?
- Does later prospective exclusion have separate legal requirements?

Leaving when asked does **not** establish a universal nationwide defense to every trespass offense. It means the jurisdiction must identify what offense, if any, was complete under its own law.

## NC-COMPLAINT-01.C: Currently remains after a request to leave

The recorder is presently on the disputed property after someone has requested departure.

Questions:

- Is the location actually the complainant's/government's property?
- Is the area open to the public?
- Was the requester authorized?
- What statutory/regulatory provision governs remaining?
- Are additional elements required, such as intent, interference, notice, restricted-area status, or lack of lawful business?
- If government property is involved, what constitutional/forum restrictions apply to the exclusion?

This posture creates materially different immediate risk from a person who is already off the property.

## NC-COMPLAINT-01.D: Prospective no-entry notice only

The recorder is outside the property and is told not to enter or return in the future.

Questions:

- Who has authority to issue the notice?
- What property/areas and duration does it cover?
- Does state law make prior notice independently relevant to a later offense?
- Does government property require process, cause, tailoring, or constitutional review before a prospective exclusion?
- Is police merely conveying a private owner's instruction, or invoking an enforceable statutory exclusion process?
- Does communicating or documenting the notice require the recorder's identity at all?
- If police request or demand identification, what **separate** statute, detention rule, citation/arrest procedure, booking rule, or other authority creates any legal duty to identify?
- Can the notice be communicated to an unidentified person even if later enforcement would require proof that the same person received it?

A prospective notice is not proof of a completed past trespass. The existence of a notice also does **not by itself answer** whether the recipient has a legal duty to identify; the jurisdiction must identify independent authority for any compulsory identification requirement.

## NC-COMPLAINT-01.M: Conditional access / derivative trespass

This branch applies when an otherwise lawful or arguably lawful presence becomes the subject of a removal/trespass sequence because continued access is conditioned on ceasing expressive or information-gathering activity.

Recurring pattern:

`record/speak/observe → "stop the activity or leave" → activity continues → exclusion/removal order → remaining or reentry → trespass enforcement`

The branch does not presume that the underlying activity is protected everywhere or that every removal order is invalid. It prevents the later trespass label from erasing the legal question that generated the duty to leave.

Questions:

- What exact activity triggered the conditional-access demand?
- Was the demand expressly conditional, such as `stop recording or leave`, or does the chronology otherwise connect the restriction to the activity?
- What rule, policy, statute, regulation, property authority, forum doctrine, or other law allegedly authorized the underlying restriction?
- Was any independent misconduct identified **before** the exclusion order, such as obstruction, actual disruption, restricted-area entry, threat, safety interference, or another supplied fact?
- If government property is involved, what forum/access and First Amendment rules govern conditioning continued access on ceasing the activity?
- If private property is involved, what actual property/exclusion authority applies, and where was the observer physically located?
- Was the removal/exclusion order itself lawful under the applicable authority?
- If the person remained or later reentered, what distinct trespass elements then apply?
- Did police or later decisionmakers analyze only the downstream refusal-to-leave fact while ignoring the upstream restriction/exclusion that created it?
- What evidence preserves the chronology: video, policy text, body camera, incident report, written exclusion notice, or witness account?

**National-core rule:** preserve and analyze the entire causal chain. A downstream trespass allegation does not make the legality of the upstream recording/speech restriction or exclusion analytically irrelevant.

# 3. Convert characterization into alleged facts

When a caller says the recorder is:

- trespassing;
- hostile;
- escalating;
- disruptive;
- threatening;
- suspicious;
- harassing;
- refusing to cooperate;

the research/field record should ask what observable conduct supports the characterization.

Examples:

`hostile` may mean:

- spoke critically;
- raised voice;
- used profanity;
- approached someone;
- made a threat;
- made no threat but refused to stop recording.

Those are legally different facts.

`disruptive` may mean:

- interrupted a government proceeding;
- blocked a service line;
- interfered with staff work;
- caused other people to complain;
- merely continued recording despite objections.

Again, those are different facts.

The national rule is **allegation-to-elements analysis**. The state overlay determines which facts satisfy the actual offense.

# 4. Caller and 911 reliability are part of the Fourth Amendment analysis

A call to police can contribute to reasonable suspicion or probable cause, but the fact that information traveled through 911 does not make every allegation automatically reliable.

## Florida v. J.L., 529 U.S. 266 (2000)

The Supreme Court held that a bare anonymous tip that a person possessed a gun lacked sufficient indicia of reliability to justify a stop and frisk. The tip accurately identified a person at a location, but reliability as to identity did not establish reliability as to the alleged illegality.

Source:

https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/529/266

## Navarette v. California, 572 U.S. 393 (2014)

The Court found reasonable suspicion where the 911 report had materially stronger reliability indicators, including a claimed eyewitness account, close temporal connection, and features of the 911 system that reduced anonymity/accountability concerns.

Source:

https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/12-9490

**National-core rule:** assess the totality of circumstances supporting the caller's basis of knowledge and reliability. Do not use either slogan:

- `911 call = reasonable suspicion`; or
- `caller allegation can never justify a stop`.

For an identifiable employee, citizen, or security worker who personally claims to have observed conduct, the reliability analysis may be stronger than for an unknown anonymous caller. The underlying factual allegation still must matter to an actual offense.

---

# 5. Trespass probable cause remains an offense-elements inquiry

## District of Columbia v. Wesby, 583 U.S. 48 (2018)

The Supreme Court's unlawful-entry analysis illustrates that probable cause turns on the totality of circumstances, including facts bearing on whether suspects knew or should have known they lacked permission. Officers need not accept a suspect's innocent explanation at face value when other circumstances support a contrary inference.

Source:

https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/15-1485

**National-core use:** *Wesby* does not create one nationwide substantive trespass offense. It demonstrates the Fourth Amendment method: identify the actual offense, consider the totality of facts relevant to its elements, and evaluate what a prudent officer could reasonably infer.

Each state must therefore map `NC-COMPLAINT-01` to its own trespass elements.

---

# 6. Police do not have to become “Dick Tracy”

The national core must preserve the limiting principle.

## Baker v. McCollan, 443 U.S. 137 (1979)

In the context of custody under a facially valid warrant, the Supreme Court rejected a constitutional requirement that officials independently investigate every claim of innocence or perform an error-free investigation.

Sources:

https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/443/137

https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-443/pdf/USREPORTS-443-137.pdf

This prevents the Field Guide from claiming:

> Police must exhaust every possible lead, prove guilt on scene, or investigate every defense before acting.

But *Baker* does not answer every warrantless-arrest or immediately-available-evidence problem. Federal circuits have expressly distinguished an obligation not to disregard material exculpatory information from a duty to conduct an exhaustive investigation.

# 7. Immediately available exculpatory evidence

For this scenario, **immediately available exculpatory evidence** means objective evidence that:

1. directly bears on a material factual predicate of the suspected offense;
2. is known to the officer or affirmatively identified to the officer;
3. can be accessed with little or no additional investigative burden;
4. is sufficiently specific that the officer can understand what allegation it allegedly contradicts; and
5. could materially undermine reasonable suspicion or probable cause if it shows what the person claims.

The strongest recurring example is continuous contemporaneous video.

This term does **not** mean every video is necessarily exculpatory, complete, authentic, dispositive, or required to be viewed in every circumstance.

---

# 8. Multi-circuit federal authority: “no exhaustive investigation” is not “ignore what is in front of you”

These circuit cases are not universally binding nationwide. They are preserved in the national core because they illuminate a recurring federal Fourth Amendment question and become binding/persuasive according to the applicable circuit.

## Eighth Circuit: Kuehl v. Burtis, 173 F.3d 646 (8th Cir. 1999)

*Kuehl* states that an officer contemplating arrest is not free to disregard plainly exculpatory evidence even when incriminating evidence, standing alone, could suggest probable cause. The Eighth Circuit emphasized the totality of all information and distinguished a reasonably thorough investigation from requiring a mini-trial.

Source:

https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/173/646/548042/

**Field significance:** this is one of the clearest formulations that “probable cause existed if I look only at the incriminating side” is not the complete inquiry.

## Eleventh Circuit: Kingsland v. City of Miami, 382 F.3d 1220 (11th Cir. 2004)

*Kingsland* explicitly distinguished the rule that officers need not independently investigate every asserted claim of innocence from the narrower problem of turning a blind eye to immediately available exculpatory information. It also rejected selective investigation as a permissible way to manufacture or preserve probable cause.

Sources:

https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/382/1220/570494/

Eleventh Circuit opinion archive entry:
https://media.ca11.uscourts.gov/opinions/pub/

**Field significance:** this is a particularly useful response to the overbroad slogan `police don't have to investigate your innocence`.

## Tenth Circuit: Baptiste v. J.C. Penney Co., 147 F.3d 1252 (10th Cir. 1998)

Police responded to a shoplifting allegation, viewed surveillance footage recording the very conduct the security guards characterized as theft, and nevertheless relied on the guards' interpretation. The Tenth Circuit held the video had to be considered independently in the totality of circumstances and concluded probable cause was lacking.

In footnote 8, the court stated that, absent exceptional circumstances, when a videotape of the conduct at issue is known and readily accessible to an officer investigating the alleged crime, the officer must view it rather than delegate the probable-cause determination to the accuser.

Source:

https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/147/1252/634968/

### Later Tenth Circuit treatment: Craft v. White, No. 19-2213 (10th Cir. Jan. 14, 2021) (unpublished)

In an **unpublished Order and Judgment**, the Tenth Circuit characterized the categorical video language in *Baptiste* footnote 8 as **dictum** and declined to find a clearly established duty where the officer was not at the scene, did not possess the video, and sought a warrant rather than making the same kind of immediate warrantless arrest.

Official source:

https://www.ca10.uscourts.gov/opinion/19-2213

**Field significance:** *Craft* is useful later Tenth Circuit treatment but is not a published precedential limitation on *Baptiste*. It reinforces why the national core should not misstate *Baptiste* footnote 8 as a Supreme Court rule or universal categorical duty.

## Ninth Circuit: Yousefian v. City of Glendale, 779 F.3d 1010 (9th Cir. 2015)

The Ninth Circuit states that an officer may not ignore exculpatory evidence that would negate probable cause. On the particular facts, however, the suspect's self-defense account did not negate probable cause because victim/witness evidence still supported the assault allegation.

Official source:

https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2015/03/05/12-57269.pdf

**Field significance:** contradictory evidence is not automatically dispositive. The question is whether the evidence actually negates or materially dissipates the basis for probable cause.

## Ninth Circuit: United States v. Holcomb, No. 23-469 (9th Cir. July 17, 2026)

In a search context, the Ninth Circuit held that probable cause dissipated after officers discovered and viewed video showing that the alleged crime described in the warrant almost certainly had not occurred. The opinion reiterates that officers may not disregard facts tending to dissipate probable cause.

Official source:

https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2026/07/17/23-469.pdf

**Field significance:** when officers actually view objective video that undermines the alleged offense, probable cause is not frozen at the moment it was first established.

# 9. What the national core can and cannot presently say

## Supported national propositions

The national core may safely state:

- reasonable suspicion and probable cause depend on the totality of facts known to police, not merely a complainant's legal label;
- caller/tip reliability is relevant to whether an accusation supports a seizure;
- police need not perform an exhaustive investigation or eliminate every possible innocent explanation before acting;
- federal appellate courts in multiple circuits hold that plainly or immediately available exculpatory information can be material to probable cause and may not simply be disregarded under the circumstances addressed by those cases;
- probable cause is capable of dissipating when later information materially undermines its basis;
- objective video can be especially important when it records the same conduct the complainant is characterizing as criminal.

## Researched-Unresolved national proposition

The national core should **not** presently state:

> Every officer in every jurisdiction has a categorical constitutional duty to watch every exculpatory video a suspect offers before detention or arrest.

No Supreme Court case located in this review states that rule, and the circuit authorities differ in facts and formulation. An unpublished Tenth Circuit order, *Craft v. White*, later described *Baptiste*'s broad video-viewing footnote as dictum in a materially different warrant case.

For qualification purposes, the universal categorical-duty proposition is **Researched-Unresolved**, while each circuit overlay must determine what binding rule applies there.

# 10. Field articulation: identify the proposition, not merely “I have video”

The useful field move is not to demand a generalized investigation.

A person can identify:

1. the allegation;
2. the exact fact the evidence addresses;
3. the continuity/time span of the evidence;
4. its immediate availability; and
5. the limited scope of the offer.

### General field formulation

When the recording actually covers the relevant period and supports the statement, use the disputed **factual predicate** as X rather than merely repeating a legal label:

> **The allegation is X. I have continuous video from before the call through your arrival showing that X did not occur. It is queued and I am offering to play that specific recording for you. I do not consent to any broader search of my device.**

Replace X with the concrete alleged fact whenever possible. For example, prefer:

> **The allegation is that I entered the property.**

over:

> **The allegation is that I trespassed.**

The first identifies the factual predicate the recording can objectively confirm or contradict; the second may merely repeat somebody else's legal conclusion.

A disruption variant could be:

> **The allegation is that I was yelling and disrupting operations. I have continuous video from before the call through your arrival showing the entire interaction, including the volume and content of the conversation. It is queued and I am offering to play that specific recording for you. I do not consent to any broader search of my device.**

If the officer declines:

> **For the record, I offered you continuous video that directly addresses the allegation and you declined to review it. What facts are you relying on now, and am I free to leave?**

These are evidence-preservation formulations, not magic words and not guarantees against detention or arrest.

Avoid unnecessarily converting the encounter into a case-law recital. Naming *Kuehl*, *Kingsland*, *Baptiste*, *Yousefian*, or *Holcomb* may be useful when appropriate, but identifying exactly what the objective evidence proves is usually more important than reciting a citation.

# 11. Evidence-offered checkpoint

Every jurisdiction's encounter tree must preserve this event.

```text
material allegation
        |
objective contradictory evidence exists?
        |
        +-- no --> continue ordinary RS/PC analysis
        |
        +-- yes
             |
      officer informed of:
      - exact evidence
      - exact allegation addressed
      - immediate availability
             |
        +----+----+
        |         |
   reviewed     declined/not reviewed
        |         |
 what did it    why declined?
 actually show? what other facts support RS/PC?
        |         |
        +----+----+
             |
     does RS/PC remain?
```

The research record should capture:

- what evidence was offered;
- when it was offered;
- what proposition the person said it established;
- whether the officer viewed it;
- whether the officer declined;
- any reason given for declining;
- what other inculpatory facts the officer identified;
- whether the asserted offense changed;
- whether detention continued;
- whether arrest occurred.

# 12. Showing a phone does not imply consent to search the phone

When the evidence is on a phone or camera, the Fourth Amendment/device-search question remains separate.

A field user may clarify:

> **I am offering only this identified recording. I do not consent to any broader search of my device.**

The national scenario does not prescribe whether a person should physically hand over an unlocked device. That choice can create practical and legal risks and should be analyzed separately under the applicable device-search doctrine.

The key evidence checkpoint is that the relevant recording was identified and made reasonably available, not that the person surrendered unrestricted access to the device.

## Edge case: officer threatens or seeks a warrant for the recording device

A less common variation may occur when a person identifies exculpatory video and the officer responds by threatening to seek, or stating an intent to seek, a warrant for the phone/camera instead of reviewing the specifically offered recording.

Do **not** infer from the warrant reference alone that the officer acted unlawfully, retaliated, or intimidated the person. A lawful warrant can be sought when the governing requirements are satisfied.

Preserve the event because it raises separate questions:

- what completed or suspected offense supposedly supplies probable cause;
- what facts support that probable cause after considering information tending to dispel it;
- what nexus allegedly connects evidence of that offense to the device;
- what data/device scope the officer proposes to search or seize;
- whether the person was offering only a particular recording;
- whether the warrant statement was made in response to that limited offer;
- whether the statement was used to seek consent, device surrender, identification, or other compliance;
- whether the officer reviewed or declined the specifically identified exculpatory recording before escalating to broader device process.

The existence of a recording can help establish that relevant evidence may be stored on a device. It does **not by itself establish that the underlying suspected crime occurred**.

That distinction should remain explicit:

> **Crime probable cause asks whether there is adequate reason to believe the offense occurred. Evidence nexus asks why evidence of that offense is likely to be found in the place or device to be searched. A nexus cannot substitute for crime probable cause.**

In *Warden v. Hayden*, 387 U.S. 294 (1967), the Supreme Court explained that evidentiary material must have a nexus to criminal behavior and that probable cause for evidence concerns reason to believe the evidence sought will aid a particular apprehension or conviction.

Source:

https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/387/294

For phones, *Riley v. California*, 573 U.S. 373 (2014), holds that police generally must obtain a warrant before searching digital information on a seized cell phone, subject to recognized case-specific exceptions such as exigent circumstances.

Source:

https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/13-132

A statement such as `I recorded the entire incident` may therefore support a nexus between a particular recording and the incident while the recording itself may simultaneously be exculpatory and undermine the factual basis for the suspected offense. Those are separate Fourth Amendment questions.

This remains an edge-case evidence checkpoint rather than a required standalone qualification branch unless later research shows it is sufficiently recurring or doctrinally distinct to warrant promotion.

# 13. First Amendment retaliation overlay

If police action is allegedly motivated by recording, criticism, refusal to answer consensual questions, or other protected expression, a First Amendment retaliation issue may arise.

## Nieves v. Bartlett, 587 U.S. 391 (2019)

The Supreme Court generally made the existence of probable cause a barrier to a retaliatory-arrest claim, subject to a narrow exception involving objective evidence that similarly situated people not engaged in the same protected speech were not arrested.

Source:

https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/17-1174

**Field significance:** the probable-cause analysis is not merely a Fourth Amendment side issue. Whether the alleged trespass/disorderly conduct actually supplied probable cause can materially affect a later retaliation claim.

The national scenario must therefore preserve:

- protected activity;
- statements suggesting retaliatory motive;
- alleged offense;
- actual probable-cause facts;
- immediately available contradictory evidence;
- comparator/differential-enforcement evidence where it exists.

# 14. False accusations and false reports remain primarily a state overlay

The national scenario records whether a caller made factual statements that objective evidence appears to contradict.

It does **not** automatically label the caller a criminal or state that a false 911 call is a felony.

Each jurisdiction must research:

- knowingly false crime reports;
- knowingly false emergency reports;
- misuse of 911/emergency systems;
- obstruction or interference statutes;
- enhanced penalties tied to injury, emergency response, bias, or other aggravating facts;
- required mental state;
- who qualifies as the recipient of the false report;
- whether the statement is a factual assertion or merely a mistaken legal conclusion.

Distinguish:

> **“He is trespassing.”**

which may be an incorrect legal characterization,

from:

> **“I personally watched him enter our lobby, ordered him out five times, and he is still inside.”**

when contemporaneous video may show that those factual assertions never happened.

A state false-report analysis should focus on knowingly false material facts, statutory elements, and proof of the required mental state, not merely the fact that the complainant disliked recording or reached the wrong legal conclusion.

# 16. Records and post-incident evidence

This scenario should automatically queue preservation/retrieval of evidence likely to establish what information existed at each stage:

- 911 audio and transcript where available;
- CAD/dispatch entries;
- caller identity where lawfully disclosable;
- body-worn camera;
- dash camera;
- facility CCTV;
- private/security CCTV if obtainable;
- officer notes/reports;
- security/incident reports;
- written trespass or exclusion notices;
- property maps, assessor records, easements/right-of-way records where boundary is disputed;
- employee/security policy;
- radio traffic;
- citations/arrest paperwork;
- complaint/investigation records where applicable.

The analytical purpose is to reconstruct:

`what was alleged → what police were told → what police observed → what contradictory evidence was offered → what police reviewed → what police relied upon → what action followed`

# 17. Scenario safeguards

This scenario assumes the ordinary standing facts unless a specific variant says otherwise:

- no threats;
- no assault;
- no intentional obstruction;
- no fighting;
- no deliberate interference with emergency activity;
- no intentional targeting of uninvolved private persons;
- no entry into clearly restricted areas;
- no intentional entry/remain/filming into child-designated areas;
- normal conversational conduct unless the evidence establishes otherwise.

New facts can change the legal analysis.

The scenario also does not presume that remaining on property after a disputed removal order is tactically advisable. Legal validity, arrest risk, and field strategy remain separate questions.

---

# 18. Authority classification for qualification

For any jurisdiction:

- U.S. Supreme Court authority is controlling on federal constitutional propositions.
- The jurisdiction's own federal circuit authority is binding on federal district courts in that circuit and must be identified separately.
- Other-circuit cases such as *Kuehl*, *Kingsland*, or *Baptiste* are persuasive unless they are from the applicable circuit.
- State appellate authority controls/persuades according to that state's hierarchy on state-law offense elements.
- Facility policy or a complainant's characterization is evidence of what was asserted, not self-validating law.

Do not flatten this research into a single nationwide slogan.

---

# 19. Field Guide shorthand

The project may use this shorthand internally and in field-oriented summaries:

> **No duty to become a detective does not mean permission to ignore plainly material evidence already in front of the officer.**

That sentence is an explanatory synthesis, **not a quotation from one controlling nationwide case**. The underlying authority must still be cited by circuit.

A second useful shorthand is:

> **“Trespassed” is not one legal event. Ask whether the allegation concerns prior entry, refusal to leave, current presence, or future notice.**

Again, the state overlay supplies the actual offense.
