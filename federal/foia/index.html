    Federal Freedom of Information Act (FOIA)¶

    The Freedom of Information Act, 5 U.S.C. § 552, is the federal records-access statute.

    For a California field investigation, the first question is jurisdiction:

    Federal agency records → FOIA.
    California state/local agency records → California Public Records Act (CPRA).

    Calling a CPRA request a "FOIA request" does not convert the state/local agency into a federal agency.

    What FOIA does¶

    FOIA establishes a statutory right for any person to request federal agency records, subject to the Act's exemptions and exclusions.

    A request generally must:

    • be in writing;
    • reasonably describe the records sought; and
    • be sent to the federal agency/component likely to maintain the records.

    No universal special form is required. Agencies may impose agency-specific submission procedures and identity requirements for some records.

    FOIA requests seek records, not answers to interrogatories. An agency ordinarily is not required to create a new record, conduct research, or answer questions simply because the requester phrases the request as a question.

    Primary sources: - 5 U.S.C. § 552 - FOIA.gov, "How to Make a FOIA Request" - DOJ Office of Information Policy (OIP), FOIA Guide

    Drafting a useful request¶

    A strong operational request identifies records by objective search criteria.

    Good building blocks include:

    • agency/component;
    • date range;
    • facility/location;
    • sender/recipient or custodian;
    • subject/event;
    • record type;
    • incident/case/request number;
    • desired electronic format.

    Example structure:

    Pursuant to the Freedom of Information Act, 5 U.S.C. § 552, I request electronic copies of the following agency records:

    1. All current policies, directives, orders, memoranda, training materials, and signage instructions governing public photography, video recording, or audio recording at [facility], including superseded versions in effect from [date] through [date].
    2. All incident reports, security reports, dispatch records, and other records documenting enforcement of those restrictions at [facility] during [date range].
    3. All records reasonably identifying the legal or operational authority cited for those restrictions.

    Then define useful scope:

    Please search the offices/components reasonably likely to maintain responsive records, including [identified component/custodian if known]. Please provide records in native electronic format where practicable.

    Do not automatically request "all records relating to cameras since the agency was created." A request must reasonably describe records, and extreme breadth can create delay and scope disputes.

    Why records requests matter to a restriction audit¶

    FOIA can reveal whether a purported mandate is:

    • actually written;
    • formally authorized;
    • current or superseded;
    • supported by training or merely improvised;
    • justified by a documented security/privacy concern;
    • selectively enforced;
    • repeatedly challenged;
    • changed after complaints or litigation.

    High-value categories include:

    • policy versions;
    • implementing directives;
    • training slides;
    • signage templates;
    • post orders;
    • security instructions;
    • incident reports;
    • enforcement statistics;
    • complaints;
    • after-action reviews;
    • correspondence discussing the rule;
    • records concerning a specific enforcement incident.

    Valid FOIA exemptions can still protect particular material.

    Time limits: 20 working days is generally a determination deadline¶

    Under 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(6)(A), an agency generally has 20 working days to determine whether to comply with a perfected request and notify the requester.

    That is not a universal guarantee that all responsive records will be produced within 20 working days.

    The statute allows extensions for defined "unusual circumstances." Agencies also use processing tracks, and complex/voluminous requests may take substantially longer.

    If the deadline passes, constructive exhaustion can become relevant to judicial review, but exhaustion and litigation timing are technical subjects. Check the agency response, tolling/clarification history, appeal rights, and current FOIA litigation doctrine before filing suit.

    Clarification / narrowing¶

    If an agency says the request is not reasonably described or is too burdensome, do not immediately abandon the request.

    Ask the FOIA Public Liaison:

    • what specific part cannot be searched as written;
    • which custodians or record systems are implicated;
    • what date range or search terms would make the request workable;
    • whether the agency can process separable portions first;
    • whether rolling production is available.

    A narrowing agreement should be explicit so the record shows what was and was not withdrawn.

    Exemptions are not a magic word¶

    FOIA has nine exemptions. In condensed form they cover:

    1. classified national-security information;
    2. certain internal personnel rules/practices;
    3. information withheld by another federal statute;
    4. trade secrets and confidential commercial/financial information;
    5. privileged inter/intra-agency communications;
    6. personal privacy;
    7. specified law-enforcement interests;
    8. financial-institution supervision; and
    9. geological/geophysical well information.

    An agency should identify the exemption relied on for withheld material.

    Foreseeable-harm requirement¶

    The FOIA Improvement Act of 2016 added an important disclosure rule: an agency generally may withhold under an exemption only when it reasonably foresees that disclosure would harm an interest protected by that exemption, or disclosure is prohibited by law.

    Exemption 3 has its own statutory structure because it incorporates other nondisclosure statutes.

    Segregability¶

    Even when part of a record is exempt, agencies must consider partial disclosure and take reasonable steps to release reasonably segregable nonexempt material.

    A response that treats one exempt passage as a reason to withhold an entire document should be tested against segregability.

    Exemption 5 deserves special attention in restriction research¶

    Agencies may invoke Exemption 5 for qualifying privileges such as deliberative process, attorney-client privilege, and attorney work product.

    Do not assume that because a requester asks for "the legal basis" the agency must disclose privileged legal advice.

    Instead, separately request nonprivileged materials such as:

    • final policy;
    • final directive;
    • final training;
    • factual incident records;
    • nonprivileged implementation records;
    • dates/titles of policy changes;
    • publicly distributed guidance;
    • records showing actual enforcement.

    The 2016 amendments also provide that the deliberative-process privilege does not apply under Exemption 5 to records created 25 years or more before the request.

    Privacy and law-enforcement exemptions¶

    For public-facility investigations, Exemptions 6 and 7 frequently matter.

    Privacy interests can justify redaction of personal identifying information even when the event itself is responsive.

    Law-enforcement records may invoke one or more Exemption 7 subparts, including interference with proceedings, privacy, confidential sources, techniques/procedures, and safety.

    The existence of a law-enforcement exemption does not mean every law-enforcement record is categorically withheld. The threshold, subpart, foreseeable-harm rule where applicable, and segregability still matter.

    Glomar and statutory exclusions¶

    In some national-security/privacy/law-enforcement contexts, an agency may issue a Glomar response: it neither confirms nor denies the existence of responsive records.

    FOIA also contains three narrow statutory exclusions for especially sensitive law-enforcement/national-security situations. Those exclusions are distinct from a Glomar response.

    Do not assume a no-records-style response necessarily proves no records exist when these doctrines are genuinely in play.

    Fees¶

    FOIA fee treatment depends in part on requester category and the type of processing involved.

    Categories include:

    • commercial-use requester;
    • educational institution;
    • noncommercial scientific institution;
    • representative of the news media; and
    • other requesters.

    Fee waivers are a separate issue from requester category.

    When cost matters, include a fee provision such as:

    I request advance notice if fees are expected to exceed $[amount]. If applicable, I request classification in the appropriate requester category and a fee waiver/reduction because disclosure is in the public interest and is not primarily in my commercial interest.

    Do not claim news-media status or a fee waiver without facts supporting the statutory/regulatory criteria.

    Expedited processing¶

    FOIA provides expedited processing for qualifying requests, including when there is a demonstrated compelling need, and agencies may establish additional categories by regulation.

    For a requester primarily engaged in disseminating information, statutory compelling need can include urgency to inform the public about actual or alleged federal government activity.

    A request for expedition generally must explain and certify the supporting facts. Agencies must decide the expedition request within 10 calendar days.

    Expedition is not automatic merely because the subject feels important.

    Adverse determinations and administrative appeal¶

    An adverse determination should explain the basis for denial and appeal rights.

    The FOIA Improvement Act requires agencies to provide at least 90 days to file an administrative appeal.

    Appeal issues can include:

    • exemption application;
    • adequacy of search;
    • no-records determination;
    • scope;
    • fee category/waiver;
    • expedited-processing denial;
    • segregability;
    • Glomar response;
    • procedural defects.

    A strong appeal identifies the specific error rather than merely repeating the original request.

    FOIA Public Liaison and OGIS¶

    Each agency has a FOIA Public Liaison to assist requesters.

    The Office of Government Information Services (OGIS), within the National Archives, is the federal FOIA ombuds office and provides neutral dispute-resolution services.

    OGIS does not compel an agency to release records and is not a substitute for a timely administrative appeal. Seeking OGIS help does not automatically stop the appeal deadline.

    Federal court enforcement¶

    FOIA authorizes federal district-court review of agency withholding under the statute.

    Before litigation, determine:

    • whether administrative remedies must be exhausted;
    • whether constructive exhaustion applies;
    • whether the agency cured a missed statutory deadline before suit;
    • which agency is the proper defendant;
    • venue;
    • what relief is available;
    • whether the case concerns withholding, search adequacy, delay, or an alleged policy/practice.

    FOIA litigation is its own technical layer. The Field Guide should not convert "20 days passed" into "automatic win."

    Preservation and FOIA¶

    FOIA is an access/disclosure statute, not a universal federal records-retention code.

    Federal records management is also governed by the Federal Records Act and agency schedules. Litigation or reasonably anticipated litigation can create separate preservation obligations.

    For time-sensitive evidence, request the record promptly and separately identify the applicable retention policy/schedule where loss is a concern.

    Do not state that sending a FOIA request automatically freezes every responsive federal record forever.

    Retaliation / adverse response¶

    A FOIA request can become part of a wider First Amendment-retaliation fact pattern, especially when accompanied by criticism, journalism, petitioning, recording, publication, or other protected activity.

    Do not assume every delay, denial, fee estimate, referral, or exemption is retaliation.

    Preserve evidence if an agency response escalates beyond ordinary FOIA administration:

    • threats;
    • selective exclusion;
    • law-enforcement contact;
    • unusual surveillance or identification demands;
    • retaliatory policy enforcement;
    • adverse treatment tied expressly to the requester's criticism or records activity;
    • materially different treatment of comparators.

    See Records Requests and Retaliation.

    FOIA field checklist¶

    1. Confirm the target is a federal agency/component.
    2. Search existing agency reading rooms / FOIA libraries first.
    3. Identify the record, custodian, date range, and likely component.
    4. Write a records request, not interrogatories.
    5. Ask for electronic/native format where useful.
    6. State a fee ceiling and any supported fee-category/waiver request.
    7. Ask for expedition only when the criteria are genuinely met.
    8. Save the submitted request and tracking number.
    9. Calendar the response and appeal deadlines.
    10. Preserve every scope negotiation.
    11. Require the legal basis for withholding.
    12. Check foreseeable harm and segregability.
    13. Appeal adverse determinations when warranted.
    14. Use the FOIA Public Liaison / OGIS strategically.
    15. Preserve evidence of any materially adverse response separately from the FOIA merits.
