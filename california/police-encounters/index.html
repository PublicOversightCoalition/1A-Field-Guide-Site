    Police Encounters — Detention, Identification, Questioning, Arrest, and Devices¶

    Research status: This chapter reviews the core federal constitutional and California authorities governing the recurring transition from peaceful First Amendment activity to police contact. The principal encounter sequence is covered at the doctrine level: consensual contact, Fourth Amendment seizure/detention, reasonable suspicion, identification demands, protective frisks, probable-cause arrest, Fifth Amendment/Miranda questioning, and recording-device seizure/search. Sweep 014 reconciles California identification authority; Sweep 015 now separates physical device seizure, retention/diligence, frisk/plain-feel/container rules, consent scope, Riley digital-search limits, news-material statutes, and compelled biometric access. The ordinary device branch is supported; the general lawful-detention affirmative-ID issue and the specialized compelled alphanumeric-passcode/foregone-conclusion edge remain expressly RESEARCHED-UNRESOLVED rather than converted into categorical rules.

    A First Amendment audit can quickly implicate several constitutional layers:

    • First Amendment: what expressive/recording activity is protected and what restrictions may government impose?
    • Fourth Amendment: when may government seize the person, frisk, arrest, seize property, or search it?
    • Fifth Amendment: when may government compel testimonial self-incrimination and what rules govern custodial interrogation?
    • Fourteenth Amendment: the incorporation bridge through which relevant federal Fourth/Fifth Amendment protections apply to California state and local actors, plus its own due-process/equal-protection protections.

    California Constitution article I, sections 13 and 15 supply parallel state search/seizure and self-incrimination protections. Their complete independent state-doctrine layer remains separately queued.

    Encounter map¶

    Analyze each stage separately:

    1. Consensual contact — may the person decline questions or terminate the encounter?
    2. Seizure/detention — has police conduct restrained liberty under the Fourth Amendment?
    3. Reason for stop / reasonable suspicion — for a California pedestrian stop within this project's current scope, what reason was stated before covered questioning, and what specific, articulable facts actually justified the detention when it began?
    4. Scope/duration — is the investigation reasonably related to its basis and pursued diligently?
    5. Identification — is identification merely requested, or does a specific law require it here?
    6. Frisk — is there a separate reasonable basis to believe the person is armed and dangerous?
    7. Arrest — did facts known to police establish probable cause?
    8. Questioning/self-incrimination — is the person merely detained, or in Miranda custody and being interrogated?
    9. Phone/camera — is police asking for consent, physically seizing the device, or searching digital contents?
    10. Afterward — release, property return, records preservation, and remedies are distinct questions.

    The legal threshold can change during the encounter. Do not collapse the entire interaction into one label.

    For crowd-control, dispersal, police-line, and emergency-perimeter encounters, see Assemblies, Dispersal Orders, and Emergency Scenes and Sweep 017. That branch separately preserves warning audibility, observer/participant status, egress/opportunity to comply, force, and statutory-media protections.

    1. Consensual encounter versus Fourth Amendment seizure¶

    Police do not need reasonable suspicion merely to approach a person in a public place, identify themselves, ask questions, or request identification so long as the circumstances would communicate to a reasonable person that the person may decline the requests or otherwise terminate the encounter.

    A Fourth Amendment seizure occurs when physical force or a show of authority restrains liberty under the governing doctrine. A show-of-authority seizure generally requires submission to that authority.

    A useful field question is:

    Am I free to leave?

    This helps clarify the officer's asserted posture. It is not a magic constitutional test; courts examine the totality of circumstances.

    People v. Tacardon, 14 Cal.5th 235 (2022)¶

    The California Supreme Court rejected a bright-line rule that use of a spotlight necessarily detains a person. On the facts before it, the deputy approached a parked vehicle with a spotlight but without emergency lights, blocking the vehicle, drawing a weapon, or initially issuing a command. The court required a totality-of-circumstances analysis.

    Official sources:

    • https://supreme.courts.ca.gov/case/s264219-people-v-tacardon
    • https://www.courts.ca.gov/opinions/archive/S264219.PDF

    Field rule: police attention is not automatically a detention. Commands, physical restraint, blocking movement, threatening displays, emergency lights, weapons, multiple officers, tone, and other circumstances can matter.

    2. Terry detention — reasonable suspicion¶

    Terry v. Ohio, 392 U.S. 1 (1968) recognizes a limited investigative seizure short of arrest. Police may briefly detain a person when reasonable suspicion is supported by specific and articulable facts that criminal activity is afoot. Reasonable suspicion is less than probable cause, but more than a hunch.

    Official source:

    https://www.supremecourt.gov/pdfs/USReports/USREPORTS-392_PDFA.pdf

    People v. Flores, 15 Cal.5th 1032 (2024)¶

    The California Supreme Court held the facts there insufficient for reasonable suspicion. The opinion reinforces that a high-crime-area label is contextual rather than individualized suspicion and that unusual/evasive conduct must be assessed in the whole factual picture. It does not hold that evasive behavior can never matter.

    Official sources:

    • https://supreme.courts.ca.gov/case/s267522-people-v-flores
    • https://courts.ca.gov/opinion/citings-archive/2024-05-02/s267522

    Penal Code § 148(g) — recording bridge¶

    California Penal Code § 148(g) provides that photographing or recording a public/peace officer, while the officer is in a public place or the recorder is in a place the recorder has a right to be, does not in and of itself:

    • violate § 148(a);
    • create reasonable suspicion to detain; or
    • create probable cause to arrest.

    Primary source:

    https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=PEN&sectionNum=148

    California pedestrian-stop notice rule — Vehicle Code § 2806.5¶

    For this project's present facility-audit scope, the relevant part of Vehicle Code § 2806.5 is its pedestrian-stop rule. Effective January 1, 2024, the statute requires a peace officer making a traffic or pedestrian stop, before engaging in questioning related to a criminal investigation or traffic violation, to state the reason for the stop. The traffic-stop branch exists but is not developed further in this Field Guide at this stage. The officer must also document the reason on any resulting citation or police report.

    The statute has an exception when the officer reasonably believes withholding the reason is necessary to protect life or property from an imminent threat.

    Section 2806.5 is a stop-notice rule. It does not convert a consensual police approach into a detention; first determine whether a traffic or pedestrian stop has actually occurred.

    Primary source:

    https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=VEH&sectionNum=2806.5

    In People v. Valle, A169080 (Cal. Ct. App. Sept. 18, 2024), the published First District Court of Appeal described § 2806.5 as requiring notice to the detainee of the objective reason for a stop before the covered questioning. Valle arose from a traffic stop; the Field Guide retains it only for the statute's interpretation and does not use it to expand the project's traffic-stop scope. The court also explained that the statute does not alter the federal Fourth Amendment analysis governing whether the stop itself is objectively justified. Valle notes that proposed language making a failure to state the reason grounds for a suppression motion was removed before enactment.

    Official California Judicial Branch source:

    https://courts.ca.gov/opinion/published-extended-post/2024-09-18/a169080

    Reason for the stop is not the same as the full reasonable-suspicion record¶

    Section 2806.5 creates a California notice requirement. It does not say that the officer must recite every observation, caller statement, inference, or item of evidence that may later be used to establish reasonable suspicion.

    The Fourth Amendment question remains whether sufficient specific and articulable facts actually existed when the seizure began.

    Useful questions are therefore:

    Am I free to leave?

    Am I being detained?

    What is the reason for the stop?

    What crime do you reasonably suspect me of committing?

    What specific facts are you relying on?

    These questions help preserve the encounter timeline and the officer's contemporaneous explanation. Absent the statutory exception, failure to state the reason before questioning covered by § 2806.5 violates the stop-notice requirement. That does not by itself establish that the detention lacked Fourth Amendment reasonable suspicion; remedies are a separate layer. Refusal to provide every underlying fact on scene likewise does not, by itself, establish that reasonable suspicion was absent.

    3. Reasonable suspicion must exist when the seizure begins¶

    A Terry stop must be justified at inception. Facts learned during a lawful stop may expand the investigation or eventually supply probable cause, but later discoveries do not retroactively create reasonable suspicion for an earlier unlawful seizure.

    Existence and disclosure are separate questions. For a California pedestrian stop within this project's current scope, Vehicle Code § 2806.5 may require the officer to state the reason before covered questioning, but stating a reason cannot manufacture reasonable suspicion that did not already exist when the seizure began.

    A useful timeline is:

    T1: what facts were known when the seizure began?
    T2: what new facts lawfully developed?
    T3: did probable cause later arise?

    Do not project everything eventually learned backward to the beginning of the encounter.

    4. Scope and duration¶

    Under United States v. Sharpe, 470 U.S. 675 (1985), there is no universal stopwatch for Terry detentions. The inquiry includes whether police diligently pursued investigative means likely to confirm or dispel suspicion reasonably quickly under the circumstances.

    Official source:

    https://www.supremecourt.gov/pdfs/USReports/USREPORTS-470_PDFA.pdf

    For traffic stops, Rodriguez v. United States, 575 U.S. 348 (2015) holds that police may not prolong the traffic mission to pursue unrelated investigation absent an independent legal basis.

    Official source:

    https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/boundvolumes/575bv.pdf

    Field rule: a stop lawful at inception can become unlawful if its scope or continued duration is no longer justified.

    Complaint allegations, contradictory evidence, and dissipation¶

    A call for service or complaining witness can supply important facts. It does not turn legal conclusions such as "harassing," "disruptive," "interfering," or "disturbing" into statutory elements.

    When a detention grows out of a facility complaint, preserve two layers separately:

    1. what the complainant actually says happened; and
    2. what objective facts, if any, satisfy the asserted offense or justify the seizure.

    Arpin — an unexamined accusation is not automatically enough¶

    In Arpin v. Santa Clara Valley Transportation Agency, 261 F.3d 912 (9th Cir. 2001), the Ninth Circuit held that officers could not establish probable cause by relying solely on an unexamined accusation by a citizen witness without investigating the basis of the witness's knowledge or interviewing other witnesses.

    That rule is not a requirement that police conduct a full trial at the scene. A sufficiently detailed and reliable victim account, corroboration, officer observations, or further investigation can establish probable cause. John v. Youngquist, 515 F.3d 936 (9th Cir. 2008) distinguished Arpin where the officer probed the allegations and evaluated their reliability rather than merely accepting an unexamined charge.

    Sources:

    • https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/261/912/570569/
    • https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2008/02/04/0556125.pdf

    Probable cause is dynamic¶

    Probable cause is not permanently locked in merely because it existed at an earlier moment.

    In Nicholson v. City of Los Angeles, 935 F.3d 685 (9th Cir. 2019), the Ninth Circuit reiterated that officers may not disregard facts tending to dissipate probable cause. The broader Ninth Circuit rule is that a person may not be arrested, or must be released from arrest, when previously established probable cause has dissipated.

    Official opinion:

    https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2019/08/21/17-56648.pdf

    The same rule applies at the reasonable-suspicion stage. In Thomas v. Dillard, 818 F.3d 864 (9th Cir. 2016), the Ninth Circuit emphasized that subsequent facts can dispel or negate suspicion that initially appeared sufficient. In doing so, it relied on United States v. Thomas, 863 F.2d 622 (9th Cir. 1988), where the initial stop was justified but the officer had no reason to continue detaining the driver after seeing that he did not match the suspects and after the driver gave a plausible explanation and properly identified himself.

    Sources:

    • https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2016/05/05/13-55889.pdf
    • https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F2/863/622/336519/

    A Terry detention therefore must remain tied to an objective investigative basis; the government cannot preserve a detention merely by repeating facts that later information has materially dispelled.

    National-core comparison — immediately available exculpatory evidence¶

    The project-wide Complaint-Driven Police Encounter preserves the broader federal comparison:

    • Kuehl v. Burtis, 173 F.3d 646 (8th Cir. 1999): an officer contemplating arrest is not free to disregard plainly exculpatory evidence merely because inculpatory evidence exists.
    • Kingsland v. City of Miami, 382 F.3d 1220 (11th Cir. 2004): the rule that police need not investigate every claim of innocence is analytically different from turning a blind eye to immediately available exculpatory information.
    • Baptiste v. J.C. Penney Co., 147 F.3d 1252 (10th Cir. 1998): where police viewed video recording the very conduct security characterized as criminal, the video had to be considered independently. An unpublished later Tenth Circuit order, Craft v. White, characterized Baptiste's broader footnote about known/readily accessible video as dictum in a materially different warrant context.
    • Yousefian v. City of Glendale, 779 F.3d 1010 (9th Cir. 2015): binding Ninth Circuit authority states that police may not ignore exculpatory evidence that would negate probable cause, while also illustrating that merely conflicting evidence does not necessarily negate it.

    For California field use, Yousefian and the Ninth Circuit dissipation cases form the applicable federal-circuit layer: they bind federal district courts within the Ninth Circuit, while California state courts are not vertically bound by Ninth Circuit interpretations of federal law. Kuehl, Kingsland, and Baptiste are out-of-circuit persuasive comparative authority.

    This distinction matters because the Field Guide does not assert a categorical Ninth Circuit rule requiring an officer to watch every video a detainee offers. It does preserve the materially narrower question whether police may ignore specifically identified, immediately available objective evidence that directly negates the factual premise on which continued detention or arrest depends.

    Holcomb — objective video can change the probable-cause picture¶

    In United States v. Holcomb, No. 23-469 (9th Cir. July 17, 2026), a published Ninth Circuit decision arising from a search rather than a street detention, the majority held that probable cause to continue the challenged search dissipated after detectives viewed surveillance video that undermined the alleged criminal conduct.

    The case is useful here for a narrow point: objective video that officers actually review can materially alter the continuing probable-cause analysis. It does not establish a categorical rule that an officer must watch every recording a suspect offers before taking any action.

    Official opinion:

    https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2026/07/17/23-469.pdf

    Exculpatory-evidence tender¶

    When contemporaneous video directly addresses the factual accusation, the Field Guide should preserve the offer itself as part of the encounter record.

    Useful language:

    I dispute that allegation. I have continuous video of the entire encounter that directly addresses it, and I am offering to show you that recording.

    If the person believes the detention's factual basis has been dispelled:

    I believe the reasonable suspicion for continuing to detain me has dissipated. I have continuous video that directly contradicts the allegation. Am I free to go?

    These are evidence-preserving statements, not magic words. The officer may still have other facts or a different lawful basis unknown to the detainee.

    If a person chooses to show a recording from a phone, the person can make the scope of consent explicit:

    I am offering only this identified recording. I do not consent to any broader search of my device.

    Record, when possible, whether the officer accepts or declines the offered evidence and what reason is given for continuing the detention or making an arrest.

    If the officer declines to review the identified recording, useful record-building language is:

    For the record, I offered you continuous video that directly addresses the allegation and you declined to review it. Am I free to go?

    The point is to preserve what was offered and what the officer chose to consider, not to argue that the officer is categorically required to watch the entire recording.

    Allegation-to-Elements Check¶

    For facility-originated complaints, document:

    1. the exact factual allegation without labels;
    2. the precise statute, ordinance, regulation, or valid facility rule asserted;
    3. the required elements or factual predicates;
    4. which observed fact is said to satisfy each element;
    5. the complainant's basis of knowledge and any corroboration;
    6. contemporaneous contradictory or exculpatory evidence;
    7. whether police considered, rejected, or declined readily identified evidence;
    8. whether the officer's justification changed during the encounter; and
    9. whether reasonable suspicion or probable cause still existed at each material stage.

    This framework does not presume the complainant is dishonest or the officer is acting improperly. It prevents a subjective characterization from replacing the actual legal test.

    California false-report overlay — do not call every bad 911 allegation a felony¶

    When the complaint itself appears knowingly false, keep that issue separate from whether the auditor committed the alleged offense.

    Penal Code § 148.5 makes it a misdemeanor to report to specified law-enforcement officers/agencies that a felony or misdemeanor has been committed while knowing the report is false.

    Official source:

    https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=PEN&sectionNum=148.5

    Penal Code § 148.3(a) separately makes a knowingly false report that an emergency exists a misdemeanor. Under § 148.3(b), the offense becomes a felony only when the person knows or should know the response is likely to cause death or great bodily injury and death or great bodily injury actually results from the false report.

    Official source:

    https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=PEN&sectionNum=148.3

    Therefore:

    • He is trespassing may be a mistaken legal conclusion rather than a knowingly false factual report.
    • A demonstrably false factual assertion such as I watched him enter, ordered him out repeatedly, and he is still inside presents a different evidentiary question if continuous video shows none of those things occurred.
    • Disagreement, exaggeration, hostility, or a wrong legal opinion does not by itself establish the knowledge element of §§ 148.5 or 148.3.
    • The ordinary false-crime-report offense under § 148.5 is a misdemeanor, not automatically a felony.

    The encounter record should preserve the caller's exact factual statements, the caller's claimed basis of knowledge, the objective video/record evidence, and what police were told. Whether the caller actually violated a false-report statute is a separate charging/proof question.

    5. Identification — request, statutory duty, silence, booking, and false information are different questions¶

    Avoid both slogans:

    You never have to identify in California.

    and

    If you're detained, you have to show ID.

    The correct sequence is:

    1. Is the encounter consensual, a lawful detention, or an arrest?
    2. Is the officer merely asking for identification?
    3. Is there a current California statute requiring specified identifying information in this context?
    4. Is this a driver, citation, booking, specialized-facility, probation/parole, or other special setting?
    5. Is the issue silence/refusal, or false information? Those are not equivalent.

    Hiibel does not itself create a California duty¶

    Hiibel v. Sixth Judicial District Court, 542 U.S. 177 (2004) upheld application of Nevada's own stop-and-identify statute during a valid Terry stop. As authoritatively construed, Nevada required Hiibel to state his name. The Court found that requirement constitutional on those facts and rejected the Fifth Amendment claim because stating his name did not present a reasonable danger of incrimination there.

    Official source:

    https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/boundvolumes/542bv.pdf

    Hiibel establishes that a state may enact a qualifying stop-and-identify requirement. It does not legislate one for California and does not hold that identity can never have Fifth Amendment implications.

    Historical California context — Kolender v. Lawson¶

    In Kolender v. Lawson, 461 U.S. 352 (1983), the Supreme Court invalidated California's former requirement that a stopped person provide credible and reliable identification because the scheme was unconstitutionally vague and gave excessive enforcement discretion. Hiibel itself discusses that history.

    This historical case does not mean all modern California identification requirements are invalid. Current specialized statutes still control their own settings.

    United States v. Landeros, 913 F.3d 862 (9th Cir. 2019)¶

    Published Ninth Circuit authority held that a passenger's refusal to identify himself, where officers lacked reasonable suspicion that he had committed an offense, did not itself create reasonable suspicion and did not justify prolonging the traffic stop to investigate him.

    Official Ninth Circuit opinion:

    https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2019/01/11/17-10217.pdf

    The important distinction is request versus compulsion. An officer may ask without necessarily having authority to impose criminal consequences for refusal.

    California published cases — § 148 is not a generic silence offense¶

    People v. Quiroga, 16 Cal.App.4th 961 (1993)¶

    The published Court of Appeal decision drew several important lines under Penal Code § 148:

    • slow compliance and verbal argument did not automatically amount to § 148 obstruction;
    • refusing to give a name after arrest while being transported did not itself violate § 148 because the arrest had already been effected and booking had not begun;
    • refusing identity during a felony booking interview could violate § 148 because it actually delayed the booking process;
    • the court distinguished misdemeanor/infraction identification contexts, for which California statutes separately address identification and release/custody procedures.

    The current Judicial Council CALCRIM No. 2656 cites Quiroga under Verbal Resistance or Obstruction and specifically describes the nondisclosure proposition as identity following arrest for a felony, not misdemeanor.

    Official Judicial Council source:

    https://courts.ca.gov/advisory-body/criminal-jury-instructions-advisory-committee

    Full published opinion text (reporter reproduction):

    https://law.justia.com/cases/california/court-of-appeal/4th/16/961.html

    In re Chase C., 243 Cal.App.4th 107 (2015)¶

    This published Court of Appeal decision applied Quiroga in a police-investigation setting. The court held that, on the facts before it, the minor's refusal to identify himself while detained/postarrest but before booking did not constitute § 148 obstruction; the opinion described that refusal as protected Fifth Amendment silence. It distinguished the booking stage and false identification.

    The case is also important more generally because it emphasized that refusal to cooperate becomes criminal under § 148 only when it actually obstructs lawful police activity; verbal protest, slow compliance, or refusal cannot simply be relabeled obstruction without the statute's elements.

    Published opinion text:

    https://law.justia.com/cases/california/court-of-appeal/2015/d067787.html

    Limits of Quiroga / Chase C.¶

    Do not turn these cases into:

    The Fifth Amendment always lets every California detainee refuse every identification demand.

    That would conflict with the context-specific structure of Hiibel and California statutes. The narrower California proposition is:

    Silence or refusal to identify is not automatically Penal Code § 148 obstruction. Identify the actual statutory duty and stage of the encounter. Booking and specialized statutory contexts can be different.

    Penal Code § 148.9 — false identity is separate¶

    Penal Code § 148.9 makes it a misdemeanor, under its stated elements, to falsely represent or identify oneself as another or fictitious person to specified officers upon lawful detention or arrest for the purpose of evading process or proper identification.

    Primary source:

    https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=PEN&sectionNum=148.9

    Section 148.9 tells a detainee what not to do. Its text does not itself say every lawfully detained pedestrian must affirmatively produce ID.

    Carrying physical identification is a separate question¶

    The current California sweep has not identified a general statute requiring an ordinary pedestrian merely existing in public to carry a physical identification card.

    That question is separate from whether an officer may ask for a name, whether a specific statute requires identifying information in a specialized context, or whether a person is subject to an arrest/booking/citation procedure.

    A call for service, an officer's desire to "investigate," or an officer's request for identification does not itself create a general carry-ID statute. Facts reported in a call can still matter to reasonable-suspicion analysis, and a specific statutory context can create a real identification duty.

    The project therefore distinguishes:

    carry ID → produce a document → state identifying information → do not provide false identity

    Those are different legal questions.

    Scope of this conclusion: the absence of a general carry-ID statute and general statutory stop-and-identify law does not answer every lawful-detention question. Sweep 014 has now reconciled the principal older published identification-production/search cases with later authority. The remaining California § 148 question—whether refusal to identify during every otherwise lawful Terry detention, without another statutory duty or obstructive conduct, independently supplies arrest authority—is preserved as RESEARCHED-UNRESOLVED rather than guessed.

    Lawful detention: older production cases and the unresolved Vanegas boundary¶

    People v. Loudermilk, 195 Cal.App.3d 996 (1987)¶

    Loudermilk contains broad language allowing an officer conducting a lawful Terry investigation to require a suspect to produce proof of identification if the suspect has it. But the facts were narrow and serious: a reported shooting, a matching suspect description, a weapon concern, a lawful frisk, and a wallet discovered after the suspect said he had no identification.

    The opinion itself cautioned that it was not holding that a suspect may be detained and searched merely for refusing to identify or produce proof.

    Source:

    https://law.justia.com/cases/california/court-of-appeal/3d/195/996.html

    People v. Long, 189 Cal.App.3d 77 (1987)¶

    Long likewise upheld a direction to produce written identification on specific facts involving a lawful detention, suspected drug intoxication, an apparent inconsistency between a denial of possessing identification and a visible wallet, and later safety/evidence concerns when the wallet was produced.

    Source:

    https://law.justia.com/cases/california/court-of-appeal/3d/189/77.html

    Do not rewrite Long as a general pocket-search-for-ID rule.

    People v. Garcia, B187453 (Cal. Ct. App. Dec. 14, 2006)¶

    Garcia supplies the important later limitation. A lawfully detained bicyclist said he had no identification, and the officer conducted a patdown/search solely to find identification. The published Court of Appeal held there was no legal justification for a patdown search for identification. Terry's protective-frisk rule cannot be repurposed into an evidence search for ID.

    Garcia expressly distinguished Long and Loudermilk as fact-specific.

    Source:

    https://law.justia.com/cases/california/court-of-appeal/2006/b187453.html

    Field rule: detention and frisk/search authority are separate. A lawful Terry stop does not, by itself, authorize a patdown for identification.

    Vanegas v. City of Pasadena, 46 F.4th 1159 (9th Cir. 2022)¶

    Vanegas confirms that the broader affirmative-identification question should not be overstated.

    The officers had an independent probable-cause basis under Penal Code § 415(2), so the Ninth Circuit did not need to decide whether Vanegas's refusal to identify during the investigation itself supplied probable cause under § 148(a)(1). On the § 148 theory, the panel held that no controlling authority or robust consensus clearly prohibited the officers' asserted interpretation and therefore granted qualified immunity. The majority expressly left open whether refusal actually supplied § 148 probable cause.

    Official Ninth Circuit opinion:

    https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2022/08/31/21-55478.pdf

    Judge Bumatay's separate concurrence emphasized that he found no California decision categorically holding that failure to identify during a police investigation, without more, supplies § 148 probable cause. Other separate reasoning in the case read the obligation more broadly.

    Qualification rule: the California-law effect of refusing to identify during every otherwise lawful Terry detention is RESEARCHED-UNRESOLVED. Do not turn the qualified-immunity holding into a substantive rule that § 148 always requires ID.

    Citation, arrest, and booking can change the identification analysis¶

    The unresolved Terry-stage question does not erase specific identification consequences after an independently supported offense/arrest.

    • Penal Code § 853.5 supplies identification/signature/fingerprint procedures in the specified infraction citation process.
    • Vehicle Code § 40302 supplies custody/identity rules after arrest for qualifying nonfelony Vehicle Code offenses.
    • In People v. McKay, 27 Cal.4th 601 (2002), the California Supreme Court emphasized that the bicyclist was not arrested merely for failing to carry identification; he had first committed a Vehicle Code infraction, after which § 40302 governed whether satisfactory identification permitted cite-and-release treatment.
    • Quiroga preserves a separate narrow felony-booking rule where withholding identity actually obstructs booking.

    McKay source:

    https://law.justia.com/cases/california/supreme-court/4th/27/601.html

    The sequence is:

    independent offense/detention authority → citation/arrest/booking stage → stage-specific identification rule → consequence of noncompliance

    Do not reverse the sequence into:

    no physical ID → therefore detention/arrest is justified.

    Exclusion notices do not create their own ID power¶

    An employee or officer may want a name to document a facility exclusion. Administrative usefulness is not itself a criminal identification statute.

    When the stated purpose is "we need your ID to trespass you," ask:

    1. Is the contact consensual or a lawful detention?
    2. What independent offense or legal basis supports detention?
    3. Is a citation/arrest/booking statute now applicable?
    4. Is the facility instead imposing a valid administrative access condition for future service or appeal?
    5. Is the person merely refusing/silent, or giving false information?
    6. Is any physical search for identification separately justified?

    See Sweep 014 and Trespass and Removal.

    Drivers — Vehicle Code § 12951¶

    Driving is different. Vehicle Code § 12951 requires a driver to possess the valid license when driving on a highway and present it on demand to a peace officer enforcing the Vehicle Code.

    Primary source:

    https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=VEH&sectionNum=12951

    Infraction-arrest citation — Penal Code § 853.5¶

    Section 853.5 supplies identification/fingerprint procedures when an officer is issuing a written promise to appear after an infraction arrest. It is not a general pre-arrest pedestrian stop-and-identify statute.

    Primary source:

    https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=PEN&sectionNum=853.5

    Vehicle Code § 40302¶

    Section 40302 supplies additional identity/custody rules after arrest for specified nonfelony Vehicle Code violations.

    Primary source:

    https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=VEH&sectionNum=40302

    Current California field rule¶

    This review has not identified a current general California pedestrian stop-and-identify statute equivalent to Nevada's statute in Hiibel. That is not the same as saying identification can never be required. Determine the specific current authority and stage. Silence/refusal is not automatically § 148 obstruction, false identity can independently violate § 148.9, citation/booking rules can impose separate consequences, and the otherwise-lawful-Terry-stop affirmative-ID boundary remains RESEARCHED-UNRESOLVED.

    Useful question:

    What law requires me to provide identification under these circumstances?

    And regardless of whether a duty exists:

    Do not give a false identity.

    6. Stop does not automatically authorize a frisk¶

    For the separate line between protected verbal challenge and Penal Code § 148 resistance/delay/obstruction, see Disturbing the Peace and Disorderly Conduct and Sweep 016. Quiroga and Mackinney protect verbal dispute/momentary slow compliance; changed facts involving repeated conduct and actual delay are treated separately.

    A Terry detention and Terry frisk are analytically distinct. A protective frisk requires a reasonable basis to believe the detained person is armed and dangerous. Its purpose/scope is protective rather than a general evidence search.

    Useful question:

    Are you frisking me because you believe I am armed and dangerous? What facts support that?

    Do not physically resist.

    Plain feel does not make a phone searchable evidence¶

    Under Minnesota v. Dickerson, 508 U.S. 366 (1993), a Terry frisk remains a protective weapons search. Nonthreatening contraband may be seized through lawful touch only when its incriminating character is immediately apparent and the officer does not exceed the protective frisk through further manipulation.

    Source:

    https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/508/366

    A common phone or camera detected during a frisk is not automatically contraband or evidence merely because the officer recognizes what it is. Separate authority is needed for evidentiary seizure and still-separate authority is needed to search stored digital contents.

    California's People v. Garcia, discussed in the identification section above, reinforces the same structural point: Terry cannot be repurposed into a generalized evidence search merely because a particular item would be useful to police.

    7. Arrest — probable cause is a different threshold¶

    An arrest ordinarily requires probable cause to believe the person committed or is committing an offense.

    • reasonable suspicion: supports a limited investigative detention;
    • probable cause: ordinarily supports arrest.

    Devenpeck v. Alford, 543 U.S. 146 (2004)¶

    The Supreme Court held that warrantless-arrest legality is an objective probable-cause inquiry. The offense objectively supported by known facts need not be the same offense the officer announced.

    Official source:

    https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/boundvolumes/543bv.pdf

    Thus:

    Asking What offense? is excellent record-building, but an officer's verbal answer is not necessarily the complete later probable-cause analysis.

    8. Fourth Amendment detention is not the same as Miranda custody¶

    A Terry detainee is not free to leave for Fourth Amendment purposes but is not automatically in custody for Miranda purposes.

    In Berkemer v. McCarty, 468 U.S. 420 (1984), the Supreme Court explained that ordinary traffic stops resemble Terry stops and generally are noncustodial for Miranda even though they significantly restrict movement. Miranda applies when the treatment reaches the degree associated with formal arrest.

    Official source:

    https://www.supremecourt.gov/pdfs/USReports/USREPORTS-468_PDFA.pdf

    Field distinction:

    4A: Am I seized, and what justifies that seizure?

    5A/Miranda: Am I in Miranda custody and being interrogated?

    A person may answer yes to the first and no to the second.

    9. Fifth Amendment privilege and Miranda¶

    The Fifth Amendment protects against compelled testimonial self-incrimination. Malloy v. Hogan, 378 U.S. 1 (1964) applies that privilege against the States through the Fourteenth Amendment. California Constitution article I, section 15 provides a parallel state privilege.

    Miranda is a procedural doctrine protecting the privilege during custodial interrogation. Failure to give Miranda warnings does not automatically make the underlying detention or arrest unlawful.

    Clear invocation is the safer rule¶

    In Berghuis v. Thompkins, 560 U.S. 370 (2010), the Court held in the Miranda context that invocation of the right to remain silent must be unambiguous; prolonged silence did not itself invoke the right on those facts.

    Official source:

    https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/boundvolumes/560bv.pdf

    In Salinas v. Texas, 570 U.S. 178 (2013), a plurality concluded that a person in a voluntary, noncustodial interview could not rely on silence alone on the facts there because he had not expressly invoked the privilege.

    Official source:

    https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/boundvolumes/570bv.pdf

    Because Salinas is plurality-limited and invocation doctrine is context-dependent, the Field Guide should not say silence never invokes the Fifth Amendment. The safer operational advice is:

    If you intend to invoke, say so clearly rather than expecting silence alone to communicate the invocation.

    Useful language:

    I invoke my right to remain silent. I do not want to answer investigative questions.

    No exact incantation is required; clarity is the point.

    Invocation does not automatically terminate a lawful detention or erase a specific identification duty that is otherwise constitutionally enforceable.

    10. Hiibel's Fifth Amendment holding is narrow¶

    Hiibel rejected the Fifth Amendment challenge because on those facts stating the detainee's name did not create a reasonable danger of incrimination. The opinion did not hold that identity can never be testimonial/incriminating.

    For California, ask in order:

    1. Is there a lawful detention?
    2. Is there a current law requiring the particular information?
    3. What exactly is demanded — name, document, address, DOB, fingerprint, etc.?
    4. Does the Fifth Amendment create an additional issue on these facts?

    11. Phones and cameras — seizure, retention, search, and unlocking are separate¶

    Keep separate:

    1. request to show a recording;
    2. consent and its exact scope;
    3. physical seizure or temporary securing;
    4. legal basis for that seizure;
    5. duration/retention while a warrant is sought;
    6. digital-content search;
    7. forensic extraction; and
    8. compelled unlocking.

    Detailed authority record: Sweep 015 — Recording-Device Seizure, Retention, Search, and Compelled Access.

    Riley v. California — digital search is not physical custody¶

    Riley v. California, 573 U.S. 373 (2014) holds that search incident to arrest does not ordinarily authorize police to search digital information on a cellphone merely because the phone was seized from a lawfully arrested person.

    Official source:

    https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/boundvolumes/573BV.pdf

    Riley expressly separates the two acts: officers may sometimes seize and secure a physical phone while seeking a warrant even though they ordinarily may not browse its digital contents without a warrant or another recognized exception.

    Field rule:

    Riley is not a no-seizure rule. It is a strong digital-search rule.

    Initial physical seizure still needs authority¶

    A phone/camera is property protected by the Fourth Amendment. For evidentiary seizure, identify the actual basis:

    • warrant;
    • lawful arrest/property authority;
    • probable cause plus a recognized warrant exception;
    • genuine evidence-preservation exigency;
    • a narrowly justified temporary property detention;
    • valid consent; or
    • a specialized search condition.

    Under Arizona v. Hicks, 480 U.S. 321 (1987), plain-view evidentiary seizure requires probable cause; reasonable suspicion is not enough merely because the object can be seen.

    Source:

    https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/480/321/

    An officer saying the video is evidence does not itself establish the evidentiary nexus or seizure exception.

    Preservation exigency must be specific¶

    Published Ninth Circuit United States v. Camou, 773 F.3d 932 (2014) applies Riley and rejects a generalized claim that digital data is volatile. A genuine imminent remote-wipe or other "now or never" problem can matter, but the government must show the case-specific exigency and keep the action within the scope of that need.

    Source:

    https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/ca9/12-50598/12-50598-2014-12-11.html

    Once officers have secured the phone, the owner's ability personally to delete its contents is ordinarily eliminated. The possibility of remote wiping is not itself a categorical license to browse.

    Continued retention has its own reasonableness limit¶

    A seizure lawful at the beginning can become unreasonable through delay.

    Published Ninth Circuit anchors:

    • United States v. Song Ja Cha, 597 F.3d 995 (2010) — a seizure reasonable at inception can become unreasonable because of duration; officers must pursue the warrant with appropriate diligence.
    • United States v. Dass, 849 F.2d 414 (1988) — probable cause did not permit officers to retain mailed packages for days while warrants were obtained at leisure.
    • United States v. Sullivan, 797 F.3d 623 (2015) — no universal day-count rule; 21-day laptop retention was reasonable on the unusual totality there, including incarceration, parole/search conditions, reduced possessory interest, failure to seek return, later consent, and government interests.

    Sources:

    https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/ca9/09-10147/09-10147-2011-02-25.html

    https://openjurist.org/849/f2d/414

    https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2015/07/29/12-10196.pdf

    For a seized recorder, preserve the time taken, whether return was requested, whether police actually pursued a warrant, when a warrant was submitted/issued, whether investigative need changed, and when the device was returned.

    Recent Ninth Circuit memoranda Carlson v. Chavez, No. 25-3038 (Sept. 9, 2026) and Langham v. Noyd, No. 24-7292 (Dec. 23, 2025) provide unusually direct phone-seizure comparators on speculative exigency and delay, but both are not for publication/nonprecedential and are retained only as factual cross-checks.

    Consent to one recording is not unlimited device consent¶

    Under Florida v. Jimeno, 500 U.S. 248 (1991), consent scope turns on objective reasonableness and can be expressly limited.

    Source:

    https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/500/248

    Useful language when voluntarily showing exculpatory video:

    I am offering only this identified recording. I do not consent to any broader search of my device.

    That preserves the intended scope. It does not guarantee that later factual disputes cannot arise from what the person actually does with the device.

    If physical surrender is ordered:

    I do not consent to seizure or search. If you are ordering me to hand it over, I am complying under protest and not consenting.

    Do not physically fight over the device.

    News-material statutes can add protection without making credentials the constitutional baseline¶

    The federal Privacy Protection Act, 42 U.S.C. § 2000aa, restricts certain government searches/seizures of defined work-product and documentary materials held in connection with public dissemination, subject to important statutory exceptions. Its definitions expressly include photographs and electronically recorded audio/video media.

    Primary sources:

    https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/42/2000aa

    https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/42/2000aa-7

    The PPA turns on its statutory material/dissemination requirements and exceptions, not merely possession of a press credential. It also does not automatically protect every citizen's phone.

    California separately provides in Penal Code § 1524(g) that no warrant shall issue for items described in Evidence Code § 1070. The § 1070 shield is tied to its newsperson/media categories and unpublished-information definition, so this guide does not assume every citizen recorder automatically qualifies.

    Sources:

    https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=PEN&sectionNum=1524.

    https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=EVID&sectionNum=1070.

    These protections are overlays; they do not replace the ordinary Fourth Amendment seizure/search analysis.

    Compelled biometric unlocking — Payne is binding but narrow¶

    In United States v. Payne, 99 F.4th 495 (9th Cir. 2024), officers physically selected and used a California parolee's thumb to unlock an already identified phone. The Ninth Circuit held the compelled thumb use non-testimonial on those facts because it required no cognitive exertion.

    Source:

    https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/ca9/22-50262/22-50262-2024-04-17.html

    Two limits are critical:

    1. Payne's Fourth Amendment search authority came from parole search conditions; do not import that search authority to an ordinary person.
    2. The Ninth Circuit expressly cautioned that its Fifth Amendment result does not cover every biometric situation; a result could differ where the person must independently choose which biometric will unlock the device.

    Published California People v. Ramirez, H049957 (2023) reaches a similar non-testimonial fingerprint result on warrant-based facts.

    Source:

    https://law.justia.com/cases/california/court-of-appeal/2023/h049957.html

    Alphanumeric passcodes remain a specialized unresolved edge¶

    Payne distinguishes a physical biometric from forcing a person to divulge a passcode held in the mind, but it did not decide every compelled-passcode scenario.

    Published Ninth Circuit In re Grand Jury Subpoena Dated April 18, 2003, 383 F.3d 905 (2004) supplies the circuit's act-of-production/foregone-conclusion framework in the document-production context: prior government knowledge of existence, possession, and authenticity matters.

    Source:

    https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/383/905/495139/

    Project status: the ordinary device branch is researched, but the precise compelled alphanumeric-passcode/foregone-conclusion application remains a specialized RESEARCHED-UNRESOLVED edge. Do not use either categorical slogan: a warrant always lets police force the passcode or a passcode can never be compelled.

    12. Practical encounter script — evidence-preserving, not magic words¶

    Initial contact¶

    Am I free to leave?

    If no¶

    Am I being detained?

    What is the reason for the stop?

    What offense do you reasonably suspect me of committing?

    What specific facts are you relying on?

    If the stop rests on a disputed complaint¶

    I dispute that allegation. I have continuous video of the entire encounter that directly addresses it, and I am offering to show you that recording.

    I believe the reasonable suspicion for continuing to detain me has dissipated. Am I free to go?

    If offering a specific recording from a phone:

    I am offering only this identified recording. I do not consent to any broader search of my device.

    If ID is demanded¶

    What legal authority requires me to provide identification under these circumstances?

    Never provide false/fictitious identity.

    If a frisk/search begins¶

    I do not consent to any search.

    Are you frisking me because you believe I am armed and dangerous?

    If substantive questioning begins¶

    I invoke my right to remain silent. I do not want to answer investigative questions.

    If a device is demanded¶

    I do not consent to seizure or search of my device. If you are ordering me to hand it over, I am complying under protest and not consenting.

    Physical nonresistance and legal consent are different things.

    13. Common bad field rules¶

    Bad slogan Better rule
    If I'm not under arrest, I can leave. A Terry detainee may be temporarily not free to leave without being under formal arrest.
    If I'm detained, they have to Mirandize me. Fourth Amendment detention and Miranda custody are different thresholds.
    Hiibel says I have to identify whenever detained. Hiibel upheld Nevada's statute; identify California authority for the actual context.
    The officer has to tell me all of the RAS immediately. For a pedestrian stop within the guide's current scope, California § 2806.5 requires the reason for the stop before covered questioning, subject to its exception. That is different from requiring an on-scene recital of every fact underlying reasonable suspicion.
    California has no stop-and-ID law, so I can lie. Whether affirmative ID is required is separate from § 148.9's false-ID offense.
    Refusing my name is automatically obstruction. California published cases distinguish silence/refusal from actual § 148 obstruction and distinguish booking from earlier stages.
    A legal stop automatically allows a frisk. A frisk requires a separate armed-and-dangerous basis.
    No Miranda warning means the arrest is illegal. Arrest legality is primarily a 4A probable-cause question; Miranda concerns custodial interrogation/statement use.
    Arrest means police can search my phone. Riley generally requires a warrant for digital contents; seizure and search are separate.
