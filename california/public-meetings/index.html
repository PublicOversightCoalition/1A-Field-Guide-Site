    Public Meetings: Brown Act and Bagley-Keene¶

    Research status: The ordinary qualification branch is reviewed for attendance/sign-in, recording, public comment/criticism, individual and group disruption/removal, and the Ninth Circuit limited-public-forum overlay. An exhaustive section-by-section/virtual/special-body/local-rule treatment remains specialized.
    Last verified: September 29, 2026.

    California has separate open-meeting statutes for local legislative bodies and many state bodies. Do not treat the Brown Act and Bagley-Keene as interchangeable.

    Short field rule¶

    At a covered open meeting, California law may protect more than the ability to sit in the room. The Brown Act and Bagley-Keene contain provisions addressing attendance conditions, recording, public comment, criticism, and disruption/removal. For covered meetings, a person ordinarily cannot be required to give a name or complete a sign-in form merely as a condition of attendance, and recording is expressly protected subject to the statutes' disruption standards.

    Brown Act — local legislative bodies¶

    Government Code § 54950 — purpose¶

    Primary source:
    https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=GOV&sectionNum=54950.

    The Brown Act declares that local public bodies exist to conduct the people's business and that their actions and deliberations are to occur openly.

    Use in the guide: § 54950 states legislative purpose. It is not by itself the operative attendance, recording, comment, or removal rule.

    Government Code § 54953.3 — no mandatory sign-in or identification prerequisite¶

    Primary source:
    https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=GOV&sectionNum=54953.3

    Section 54953.3 states that a member of the public may not be required, as a condition of attendance at a meeting of a local legislative body, to register a name, provide other information, complete a questionnaire, or otherwise satisfy a condition precedent to attendance.

    If a sign-in sheet, register, questionnaire, or similar document is posted or circulated, it must clearly state that completion is voluntary and that attendance is permitted regardless of whether a person signs or registers.

    Field rule: At a covered Brown Act meeting, you must sign in before you can attend is not the statutory rule. A voluntary attendance sheet is different from a mandatory condition of entry.

    Limit: This section concerns attendance at covered meetings. It is not a general rule that a government facility may never lawfully request identifying information for some other purpose.

    Government Code § 54953.5 — recording an open meeting¶

    Primary source:
    https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=GOV&sectionNum=54953.5

    Section 54953.5(a) expressly gives a person attending an open and public meeting the right to record the proceedings unless the legislative body makes a reasonable finding that the recording cannot continue without noise, illumination, or obstruction of view that constitutes or would constitute a persistent disruption.

    Section 54953.5(b) separately addresses recordings made by or at the direction of the local agency and their inspection under the California Public Records Act.

    Field rule: A bare statement that recording is prohibited is not the statutory standard at a Brown Act open meeting. The text specifically focuses on persistent disruption from noise, illumination, or obstruction of view.

    Current-law note: The official text reflects amendments effective January 1, 2026.

    Government Code § 54954.3 — public comment and criticism¶

    Primary source:
    https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=GOV&sectionNum=54954.3.

    Section 54954.3 requires opportunities for members of the public to address a local legislative body within the statute's terms and permits reasonable regulations including time limits.

    Section 54954.3(c) states that the legislative body shall not prohibit public criticism of agency policies, procedures, programs, or services, or of acts or omissions of the legislative body. The section also states that it does not create expression privileges beyond those otherwise provided by law.

    Field rule: Criticism of the agency or legislative body is not, merely because it is critical, a statutory basis for prohibiting public comment. Reasonable and lawful meeting regulations can still apply.

    Government Code § 54957.95 — removal of an individual for disruption¶

    Primary source:
    https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=GOV&sectionNum=54957.95.

    The section authorizes the presiding member or designee to remove an individual for disrupting a meeting. Ordinarily, before removal, the person must be warned that the behavior is disrupting the meeting and that failure to cease may result in removal; the person may then be removed if the disruptive behavior does not promptly cease. The warning requirement does not apply to the statute's force/true-threat category.

    The statute defines disrupting as behavior that actually disrupts, disturbs, impedes, or renders infeasible the orderly conduct of the meeting. It includes failure to comply with reasonable and lawful regulations and force or a true threat of force.

    Field rule: The current statute does not define disruption merely as making officials uncomfortable or expressing criticism. Apply the statutory definition and any relevant case law.

    Standing-scenario consequence: A brief verbal or nonverbal audience reaction is not silently assumed to have actually disrupted, disturbed, impeded, or rendered infeasible the orderly conduct of the meeting. If officials tolerate materially comparable applause, cheering, laughter, or gestures expressing approval but warn or remove someone for comparable disapproval, preserve the full sequence. The comparison may matter to viewpoint-neutrality analysis, but relevant differences in timing, duration, volume, invitation by the chair, actual effect on proceedings, and any reasonable and lawful decorum regulation must also be documented.

    Current-law note: The official text reflects amendments effective January 1, 2026.

    Government Code § 54957.9 — clearing a meeting room after group disruption¶

    Primary source:
    https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=GOV&sectionNum=54957.9.

    If a meeting is willfully interrupted by a group or groups so that orderly conduct becomes unfeasible and order cannot be restored by removing the individuals who are willfully interrupting, the legislative body may order the meeting room cleared and continue the session under the section's terms.

    The statute also addresses continued attendance by representatives of the press/news media who were not participating in the disturbance and permits a procedure for readmitting individuals not responsible for the disturbance.

    Field rule: § 54957.9 concerns a specific escalation—group disruption making orderly conduct unfeasible after individual-removal measures cannot restore order. Do not treat it as a general power to clear a room whenever a meeting becomes contentious.

    Bagley-Keene Open Meeting Act — state bodies¶

    Government Code § 11120 — purpose¶

    Primary source:
    https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=GOV&sectionNum=11120.

    Section 11120 states California's policy that public-agency proceedings be conducted openly so the public may remain informed and identifies the article as the Bagley-Keene Open Meeting Act.

    Government Code § 11124 — no mandatory sign-in or identification prerequisite¶

    Primary source:
    https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=GOV&sectionNum=11124.

    Section 11124(a) states that a person may not be required, as a condition of attendance at a meeting of a state body, to register a name, provide other information, complete a questionnaire, or otherwise fulfill a condition precedent to attendance.

    Section 11124(b) requires any attendance list, register, questionnaire, or similar document posted or circulated at the meeting to make clear that completion is voluntary.

    Section 11124(c) addresses online platforms used to attend teleconferenced meetings. A platform may require submission of information to log in, but a person required to submit information must be permitted to use a pseudonym or other anonymous information.

    Field rule: For a covered Bagley-Keene meeting, in-person attendance ordinarily cannot be conditioned on identifying yourself or completing a sign-in form. For an online platform that requires login information, the statute expressly preserves the ability to use pseudonymous or anonymous information.

    Limit: This is a meeting-attendance rule, not a universal prohibition on identification requirements in every state facility or transaction.

    Government Code § 11124.1 — recording and broadcasting open state-body meetings¶

    Primary source:
    https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=GOV&sectionNum=11124.1.

    Section 11124.1(a) gives a person attending an open and public meeting of a state body the right to record using audio/video recorders or still/motion-picture cameras unless the state body reasonably finds that the recording cannot continue without noise, illumination, or obstruction of view constituting or likely to constitute a persistent disruption.

    Section 11124.1(c) also prohibits a state body from prohibiting or otherwise restricting broadcast of an open public meeting absent the specified persistent-disruption finding.

    Field rule: For a covered open state-body meeting, the statute affirmatively protects recording and broadcasting subject to the persistent-disruption limitation.

    Government Code § 11125.7 — public comment and criticism¶

    Primary source:
    https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=GOV&sectionNum=11125.7.

    Section 11125.7 generally requires an opportunity for public comment on agenda items under its terms and permits reasonable regulations, including time limits.

    Section 11125.7(d) states that the state body shall not prohibit public criticism of its policies, programs, or services, or its acts or omissions. As with the Brown Act, the statute says this does not confer expression privileges beyond those otherwise provided by law.

    Brown Act versus Bagley-Keene¶

    Before relying on a meeting rule, identify which body is involved:

    • city councils, county boards, and many local legislative bodies generally implicate the Brown Act;
    • covered state boards/commissions generally implicate Bagley-Keene;
    • courts, federal bodies, school/site-specific entities, advisory groups, and other bodies may have additional or different governing rules.

    Do not cite a Brown Act section to a state body or a Bagley-Keene section to a local body without first establishing coverage.

    First Amendment forum overlay¶

    The open-meeting statutes above are not the entire constitutional analysis. In Norse v. City of Santa Cruz, 629 F.3d 966 (9th Cir. 2010) (en banc), relying on White v. City of Norwalk, the Ninth Circuit held that a city-council meeting opened to public participation is a limited public forum. Reasonable topic, time, and decorum rules can apply, but they must remain viewpoint neutral; the court rejected the idea that all First Amendment protection disappears when the public-comment period ends. It also reiterated that removal under the constitutional meeting cases turns on actual disruption rather than simply disfavored expression.

    Ninth Circuit source:
    https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2010/12/15/07-15814.pdf

    This forum doctrine supplements rather than replaces the Brown Act's current statutory attendance, comment, criticism, recording, warning, and disruption rules. Norse also makes comparator evidence especially important where supportive expression is tolerated and materially comparable critical expression is punished.

    See public-forum-doctrine.md and standing-scenario.md.

    Remaining specialized meeting work¶

    The ordinary CA-F7.1 / CA-F7.2 qualification branch is runnable from the current Brown Act/Bagley-Keene provisions plus the Norse/White constitutional overlay.

    This chapter is not an exhaustive treatise. Remaining specialized work includes:

    • sections outside the ordinary attendance/recording/comment/removal path;
    • unusual or specially constituted bodies;
    • current virtual/teleconference edge cases beyond the ordinary branch;
    • local decorum rules and their authority/constitutional validity;
    • body-specific procedural rules; and
    • additional state cases when a changed fact makes them material.

    A local meeting rule remains evidence to test against governing statute and constitutional doctrine; it is not self-validating.
