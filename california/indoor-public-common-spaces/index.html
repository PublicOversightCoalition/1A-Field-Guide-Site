    Indoor Public Common Spaces¶

    This chapter applies the Field Guide's Constitution First method to indoor public common areas such as:

    • public lobbies;
    • foyers;
    • waiting areas;
    • shared public corridors;
    • public-facing vestibules;
    • general customer-service common space; and
    • other interior areas that members of the public may lawfully enter without individualized permission.

    It does not automatically include:

    • employee-only or secure areas;
    • treatment/exam rooms;
    • evidence rooms;
    • records workspaces;
    • controlled interview rooms;
    • courtrooms governed by separate court rules;
    • classrooms governed by separate rules;
    • dedicated meeting rooms opened for a distinct expressive purpose; or
    • any space whose access is conditioned on individualized permission.

    The exact area still controls.

    Short rule¶

    Public access indoors does not automatically create a traditional public forum. But a nonpublic-forum label does not erase the First Amendment.

    For ordinary government lobbies and similar service/common areas, the federal baseline is often nonpublic forum unless the government has intentionally opened the area as an expressive channel.

    That classification answers only the forum question.

    It does not answer:

    • whether photography or recording is protected;
    • whether a particular privacy rule is valid;
    • whether criticism may be prohibited;
    • whether expressive clothing may be banned;
    • whether casual conversation may be suppressed;
    • whether removal is lawful;
    • whether a policy violation creates criminal trespass; or
    • whether the California Speech Clause produces a broader result.

    1. Start with access, not with the word "lobby"¶

    Ask:

    1. Is this area ordinarily open to the public?
    2. Why is the public allowed in?
    3. Is access limited to transacting government business?
    4. Has the government invited private expressive activity there?
    5. Is the area physically and operationally distinct from nearby restricted space?
    6. Is the challenged activity passive, conversational, documentary, distributive, demonstrative, or physically occupative?

    A public lobby used for transactions can be a nonpublic forum even though anyone may enter during business hours.

    A meeting room inside the same building can be a limited public forum.

    A municipal sidewalk immediately outside can be a traditional public forum.

    Do not transfer one classification across those boundaries.

    2. Federal baseline for ordinary government interiors¶

    Sammartano v. First Judicial District Court, 303 F.3d 959 (9th Cir. 2002), is the project's principal published Ninth Circuit government-building anchor.

    The Ninth Circuit treated a judicial/municipal complex as a nonpublic forum because the building existed to conduct governmental and court business rather than to serve as a place for assembly and debate.

    That does not mean "government interior = NPF" as a categorical rule.

    It means that ordinary publicly accessible government interiors should be analyzed from:

    • the purpose of the space;
    • government intent;
    • access policy;
    • actual practice;
    • physical characteristics; and
    • the expressive channel, if any, the government intentionally opened.

    A common waiting area whose purpose is moving members of the public through a government service is usually a stronger NPF candidate than a room the agency advertises for public meetings, civic discussion, public comment, or community use.

    3. Nonpublic forum does not mean expression-free zone¶

    Sammartano is especially important because the government lost on the restriction issue even after the court accepted the NPF classification.

    The restriction on expressive clothing still had to be:

    1. reasonable in light of the purpose served by the forum; and
    2. viewpoint neutral.

    The Ninth Circuit emphasized that reasonableness in a nonpublic forum requires more than ordinary rational-basis-style assertion. The record must contain evidence supporting the claimed connection between the restriction and the legitimate need.

    That creates a useful indoor-common-space rule:

    The government cannot use "this is a nonpublic forum" as the entire justification for suppressing expression.

    The next questions are always:

    • What expression is being restricted?
    • What legitimate operational need is asserted?
    • What evidence supports the connection?
    • Is the rule broader than the demonstrated problem?
    • Is the rule viewpoint neutral?
    • Are simpler alternatives relevant to whether the restriction is reasonable?

    The government does not have to choose the least restrictive imaginable rule in an NPF, but weak fit and obvious alternatives can matter to reasonableness.

    4. Indoor common-space activity categories¶

    Different conduct creates different constitutional questions.

    Speech / conversation¶

    Ordinary quiet conversation, criticism of government, asking questions, and verbal interaction do not disappear merely because the lobby is an NPF.

    The government may regulate conduct that materially interferes with service delivery, noise limits, safety, queues, or employee access.

    A rule suppressing criticism because staff dislike the viewpoint is a different constitutional problem.

    Expressive clothing¶

    Sammartano is a direct warning against treating an NPF as a blank check to ban messages, symbols, affiliations, or criticism.

    Expressive clothing is low-footprint expression. A restriction needs a real connection to the function of the space and must be viewpoint neutral.

    Leafleting, petitioning, demonstrations, and tabling¶

    These activities use the physical space more intensively.

    In an ordinary service lobby or waiting area, government generally has greater authority to reserve circulation, seating, counters, and queue space for official business.

    That does not allow viewpoint discrimination.

    A public lobby does not become a general demonstration or tabling venue merely because members of the public may enter it.

    Photography, video, and audio recording¶

    Recording must be analyzed separately.

    A nonpublic-forum label does not create a camera ban.

    The sequence is:

    lawful vantage → subject being recorded → protected recording interest → actual restriction → asserted government interest → constitutional test → privacy/confidentiality law → enforcement authority

    The Ninth Circuit recognizes First Amendment protection for at least some acts of recording and information gathering, including recording matters of public interest. The constitutional strength of that protection can depend on the subject, location, and actual restriction.

    See Recording Public Officials and What Can I Actually Do in Each Forum?.

    5. Privacy is a justification to test, not a magic word¶

    Indoor government common spaces can contain legitimate privacy concerns:

    • benefits applications;
    • medical information;
    • financial information;
    • addresses and telephone numbers;
    • victim/witness information;
    • computer screens;
    • private interviews;
    • intake forms;
    • personally identifying information.

    Those concerns can support narrower rules.

    But "privacy" is not self-proving.

    A Constitution First restriction audit should identify:

    1. What information is actually exposed?
    2. Who is legally responsible for protecting it?
    3. What law makes it confidential?
    4. Is the information visible/audible from ordinary lawful public space?
    5. What safeguard has the facility implemented?
    6. What expressive activity is being restricted?
    7. Is the restriction tied to the actual privacy risk?
    8. Is the rule viewpoint neutral?
    9. Is a blanket ban justified, or is the problem limited to a narrower area, subject, screen, counter, or conversation?

    6. HIPAA does not turn an ordinary visitor into the facility's compliance officer¶

    HIPAA is frequently invoked too loosely in camera disputes.

    The HIPAA Privacy Rule applies to covered entities and, in relevant respects, business associates.

    An ordinary member of the public does not become a HIPAA covered entity or business associate merely by entering a public waiting area, lobby, or foyer.

    Accordingly:

    A facility cannot simply say "HIPAA" and thereby transfer its own HIPAA compliance obligations onto an unrelated visitor.

    If a government health provider is a HIPAA covered entity, the covered entity is responsible for implementing the safeguards required by the Privacy Rule.

    HHS expressly describes the obligation as one of reasonable administrative, technical, and physical safeguards. The Privacy Rule does not require elimination of every possibility of incidental exposure.

    Examples recognized by HHS include:

    • speaking quietly in public or semi-public areas;
    • limiting the amount of information exposed;
    • controlling screen access;
    • limiting unnecessary access;
    • positioning records appropriately;
    • protecting files and systems.

    The fact that a visitor can incidentally see or overhear information does not automatically mean the visitor has violated HIPAA.

    7. HIPAA also does not mean everything visible is fair game¶

    The opposite overstatement is also wrong.

    The fact that a person is lawfully standing in a public-access area does not automatically establish a right to deliberately record or publish every piece of information visible or audible from that location.

    Other law may matter, including:

    • California Penal Code § 632 and confidential communications;
    • specialized confidentiality statutes;
    • court rules;
    • victim/witness protections;
    • privacy tort law;
    • harassment/stalking law;
    • valid narrowly supported facility restrictions; and
    • rules protecting secure or restricted areas.

    The Field Guide therefore separates:

    Who bears the HIPAA compliance duty?
    from
    Whether another law independently restricts the recorder's conduct.

    Those are different questions.

    For the broader California visual-privacy analysis—including limited-audience privacy, windows/glass, Civil Code § 1708.8, Penal Code § 647(j), exposed screens/documents, and the visual/audio distinction—see Lawful Vantage, Public View, and California Privacy Boundaries.

    8. Public waiting areas and HIPAA¶

    HHS guidance is particularly useful here.

    HHS states that covered health care providers may not invite or allow media into treatment or other areas where PHI will be accessible without patient authorization.

    But HHS also states that the HIPAA Privacy Rule does not require providers to prevent media from entering areas otherwise generally accessible to the public, which may include public waiting areas and entrance/exit areas.

    That is an important allocation rule.

    It means HIPAA itself does not transform every public waiting room into a legally closed camera-free zone.

    If the facility wants to impose a recording restriction there, the restriction still needs its own legal and constitutional analysis.

    9. Incidental disclosure is primarily a safeguard question for the covered entity¶

    HHS recognizes that some incidental exposure can occur even when a covered entity is complying with HIPAA.

    A visitor may overhear a conversation, see a patient name, or glimpse information in a public-facing environment.

    HIPAA generally asks whether the covered entity used reasonable safeguards and complied with the minimum-necessary standard where applicable.

    Therefore the Field Guide should not use either of these shortcuts:

    "A visitor saw PHI, therefore the visitor violated HIPAA."

    or

    "A visitor saw PHI, therefore the facility necessarily violated HIPAA."

    The correct question is whether the covered entity used reasonable safeguards under the circumstances and whether the visitor's own conduct implicates some independent law.

    10. "HIPAA says you cannot record" — field analysis¶

    If a government employee invokes HIPAA to prohibit recording in a public-access common area, preserve the statement and separate the questions.

    Useful follow-up questions include:

    • Are you saying HIPAA directly regulates me as a member of the public?
    • What specific recording policy are you enforcing?
    • Where is that policy published?
    • What area does it cover?
    • What specific privacy interest is exposed here?
    • Is the concern a computer screen, a conversation, a patient, a form, or the entire room?
    • What statute, regulation, or policy gives the agency authority to impose that restriction?
    • Is the restriction against recording itself, or against capturing identified confidential information?
    • Does the same rule apply to agency photography, security cameras, contractors, or authorized media?
    • What authority supports removal if I do not comply?

    These are evidence-preservation questions, not magic words.

    11. Facility design matters¶

    A facility cannot necessarily justify a broad recording ban merely because it has designed a public lobby in a way that unnecessarily exposes confidential information.

    The project should investigate whether:

    • monitors face the public;
    • intake conversations occur at audibly open counters;
    • records are left visible;
    • sign-in sheets disclose more information than necessary;
    • private conversations are conducted in circulation areas;
    • staff rely on visitors averting their eyes rather than implementing safeguards;
    • narrower privacy zones or screens already exist;
    • the agency itself records the area continuously.

    These facts can matter to whether the government's asserted privacy justification is reasonable and whether a blanket restriction actually fits the demonstrated need.

    This does not mean the Constitution requires every agency to rebuild its lobby, nor does it mean a recorder may intentionally defeat legitimate safeguards.

    It means the government should not be allowed to make its own poor information-security design invisible by automatically shifting the burden to protected expression.

    12. Indoor common-space classification workflow¶

    For every lobby, foyer, waiting area, or general shared interior space:

    1. Map the exact publicly accessible area.
    2. Document the purpose of public access.
    3. Determine whether an expressive channel was intentionally opened.
    4. Assign the federal forum classification.
    5. Analyze California separately.
    6. Identify the protected activity actually at issue.
    7. Locate the claimed restriction.
    8. Identify its legal source.
    9. Identify the asserted operational/privacy/security interest.
    10. Demand evidentiary fit under the governing constitutional standard.
    11. Separate HIPAA/entity duties from visitor duties.
    12. Identify any independent law governing the recorder.
    13. Analyze removal/trespass only after the restriction itself is tested.

    13. Working presumptions for the Registry¶

    These are research presumptions, not categorical legal rules.

    Ordinary government lobby / foyer / waiting area¶

    Federal: usually NPF candidate, often HIGH or MEDIUM confidence once purpose/access facts are established.

    Why: public access is generally provided to transact government business, not for unrestricted expressive activity.

    Do not infer: no speech rights, no recording rights, automatic camera ban, automatic removal authority.

    Intentionally opened meeting/comment/community space¶

    Federal: often LPF when government intentionally opens the channel to defined speakers, topics, groups, or civic uses.

    Ordinary public sidewalk immediately outside¶

    Federal: ordinarily TPF if it is a genuine municipal sidewalk rather than a purpose-built facility access route.

    Restricted employee/secure interior¶

    Access: NOT PUBLICLY ACCESSIBLE unless facts establish otherwise.

    15. Project rule¶

    Indoor public access is not permission from government to exercise a right, and it is not surrender of the right either.

    The job is to identify the precise indoor forum, identify the protected activity, identify the actual restriction, and make the government carry the constitutional burden that belongs to it.

    And where the government invokes privacy:

    Protecting legally confidential information is primarily the duty of the entity that possesses and discloses it. A member of the public does not become the government's HIPAA compliance agent merely by standing lawfully in public common space.

    That principle does not erase independent privacy laws that may apply directly to the recorder.
