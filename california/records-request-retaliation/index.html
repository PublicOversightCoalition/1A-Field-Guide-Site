    Records Requests and Retaliation¶

    Public-records activity can reveal the evidentiary basis for a government restriction. It can also become part of a later retaliation dispute.

    The Field Guide does not presume that an unfavorable response after a CPRA or FOIA request is retaliation. It documents the chronology and tests the legal elements.

    First distinction: statutory access is not the same as a freestanding constitutional right of access¶

    FOIA and the California Public Records Act create statutory rights of access to covered government records.

    The Supreme Court has not recognized a general First Amendment right to obtain all government records. The records statute therefore supplies the disclosure right in the first instance.

    That does not mean government is free to punish constitutionally protected speech, criticism, petitioning, recording, or other protected activity merely because a records request is also involved.

    Ninth Circuit retaliation framework¶

    A typical First Amendment retaliation claim against a government official asks whether:

    1. the plaintiff engaged in constitutionally protected activity;
    2. the government took an adverse action that would chill a person of ordinary firmness from continuing that activity; and
    3. there was a sufficient causal relationship between the protected activity and the adverse action.

    The precise nature of the retaliatory act is not always dispositive. Threats, coercive measures, investigations, discipline, exclusion, or other exercises of government power can matter when they are used because of protected activity.

    Key Ninth Circuit authorities include Capp v. County of San Diego, 940 F.3d 1046 (9th Cir. 2019), and O'Brien v. Welty, 818 F.3d 920 (9th Cir. 2016).

    Public-records requests themselves: do not oversimplify¶

    O'Brien involved a student who had publicly criticized university officials, posted political views, and made several public-records requests. The Ninth Circuit held that the complaint adequately alleged protected First Amendment activity and allowed a retaliation claim to proceed.

    Some later district-court decisions have read O'Brien as support for treating a public-records request as protected speech in a retaliation analysis. Other district courts have stated more narrowly that there is no general constitutional right to request or receive government records.

    Project rule: do not rest a retaliation claim on the slogan "every records request is independently protected by the First Amendment." Identify the complete protected activity: criticism, petitioning, journalism/information gathering, recording, public advocacy, publication, or another protected act, and then analyze how the records request fits the chronology.

    The statutory right to make the request remains important even where the constitutional theory is disputed.

    Retaliatory arrest has additional doctrine¶

    If the alleged retaliation is an arrest, ordinary retaliation doctrine is not the whole analysis.

    Nieves v. Bartlett, 587 U.S. 391 (2019), generally makes probable cause important to a retaliatory-arrest claim, subject to the Court's recognized exception involving objective evidence that otherwise similarly situated people not engaged in the same protected speech were not arrested.

    In Gonzalez v. Trevino, 602 U.S. 653 (2024), the Supreme Court rejected an overly cramped approach to that exception. The objective evidence need not take the form of a nearly identical comparator case; the relevant question remains whether objective evidence supports the claim that otherwise similarly situated people not engaged in the protected speech ordinarily were not arrested for the conduct at issue.

    Official opinion:

    https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/23pdf/22-1025_1a72.pdf

    Lozman v. City of Riviera Beach, 585 U.S. 87 (2018), addressed a distinct retaliatory-arrest theory involving an alleged official municipal policy of retaliation.

    For facility encounters, keep the Fourth Amendment and First Amendment questions separate. Evidence that reasonable suspicion or probable cause never existed—or later dissipated—belongs in the seizure analysis. Evidence that an otherwise available enforcement power was used because of protected recording, criticism, petitioning, or information gathering belongs in the retaliation analysis. See Police Encounters.

    Do not convert ordinary retaliation cases into a claim that probable cause is irrelevant to every arrest.

    What to preserve when retaliation is suspected¶

    Preserve contemporaneously:

    • the exact request and timestamp;
    • acknowledgment/tracking number;
    • every response and amendment;
    • agency statements about the requester;
    • new restrictions imposed after the request;
    • orders to leave or cease recording;
    • calls to law enforcement;
    • trespass/exclusion notices;
    • unusual identification demands;
    • threats of criminal or administrative consequences;
    • evidence of materially similar requesters or speakers treated differently;
    • policy changes or newly created signage;
    • emails or public statements showing motive;
    • timing between protected activity and adverse action;
    • subsequent records requests for the incident generated by the response.

    Temporal proximity can be evidence, but timing alone does not automatically prove retaliatory motive.

    Retaliation follow-up request package¶

    When an adverse government action follows a records request, consider a second, tightly scoped request for:

    • communications mentioning the requester, email address, request number, organization, channel name, or known alias;
    • internal routing notes for the request;
    • records of meetings/calls discussing the requester;
    • records directing staff how to respond to the requester;
    • security or law-enforcement contacts concerning the requester;
    • incident reports/CAD/bodycam related to the interaction;
    • exclusion/trespass documentation;
    • policy/signage changes made after the request;
    • complaints about the requester's conduct;
    • training or guidance cited as the reason for the response;
    • logs showing who accessed or changed the request record;
    • retention schedules for those records.

    Do not assume every requested item is disclosable; privacy, privilege, security, law-enforcement, and other exemptions may apply. Request segregable nonexempt material and the legal basis for any withholding.

    A denial is not automatically retaliation¶

    A lawful exemption, fee dispute, reasonable clarification request, ordinary queue delay, or good-faith disagreement over scope does not become unconstitutional retaliation merely because the requester is critical of the agency.

    The analysis becomes stronger when the record shows an adverse governmental action beyond ordinary records administration and a concrete connection to protected activity.

    Field Guide posture¶

    The records chapter and restriction-audit chapter should be used together:

    Request the rule → request the evidence behind the rule → compare enforcement → preserve any adverse response → test retaliation separately.

    That is more useful than assuming either good faith or bad faith in advance.
