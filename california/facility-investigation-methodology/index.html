    Facility Research and Field Verification¶

    Field rule¶

    Research the facility before testing the policy.

    The project-methodology status describes this page's workflow function. Legal propositions referenced here remain subject to the verification status in the substantive chapters, authority index, and sweep ledgers.

    A strong facility investigation should establish what the law says, what the agency says its policy is, how the policy was adopted, how employees and police have applied it, and what actually happens during a peaceful field visit.

    The goal is not to manufacture a confrontation. It is to document and test a defined legal or policy question under the project's standing scenario.

    1. Start with a research question¶

    Do not begin with "let's see whether they kick us out." Begin with a proposition that can be tested.

    Examples:

    • Does this facility actually have a written no-recording policy?
    • What area does the policy cover?
    • What statute, regulation, order, or other authority does the agency claim supports it?
    • Is a visitor sign-in or identification rule written down?
    • Has the agency previously instructed staff to call police when a person records from a place they may lawfully be?
    • What offense have officers previously relied on when enforcing the policy?
    • Are favorable and unfavorable expressive reactions treated differently at a public meeting?
    • Has a policy changed after a complaint, claim, lawsuit, or earlier audit?

    The research question belongs in the facility file before the visit.

    2. Build the authority chain¶

    A sign or employee instruction is not the end of the analysis. Trace the claimed authority through each layer.

    Layer Research question
    Constitution / controlling cases What federal and California constitutional rules govern the specific area and activity?
    Statute / regulation / court rule / ordinance What enacted law actually authorizes or limits the conduct?
    Agency policy What does the agency's written rule say, who issued it, and when was it adopted or revised?
    Facility implementation What signs, screening procedures, visitor instructions, or employee practices implement the policy?
    Enforcement practice What have security staff, supervisors, or police actually done when the rule was invoked?
    Exclusion / removal authority Who is authorized to restrict access or order departure from this specific area, and what law, policy, property-management authority, or other rule supports that action?
    Police / criminal authority If police intervention occurs, what particular offense or detention/arrest basis is asserted, and what facts support that basis or satisfy the offense elements?
    Remedy / follow-up What administrative, records, complaint, civil, or judicial process applies if the policy or enforcement appears unlawful?

    The project should never collapse these into one proposition such as "there is a sign, therefore it is illegal to record" or "the employee wants the person gone, therefore police have arrest authority."

    2A. Verify the authority and delegation chain¶

    When a material facility rule is located, do not stop at its text. Build the authority record far enough to answer who could adopt it and who may enforce it.

    For each material restriction, record when available:

    Authority field Research target
    Rule-maker Exact governing body, agency, department, official, or other actor that adopted the rule
    Entity type State agency, general-law city, charter city, county, charter county, special district, board/commission, joint-powers entity, court, or other entity
    Enabling authority Constitution, statute, charter, enabling act, ordinance, regulation, resolution, agreement, or necessarily implied power
    Adoption instrument Regulation, ordinance, resolution, order, board policy, department policy, or other identified form
    Adoption date/version Current effective version and any material superseded versions
    Required procedure State APA where applicable, or the local/statutory/charter adoption procedure governing the entity
    Internal delegation Governing body/entity → department → manager/official/employee/security actor
    State-law conflict Any duplication, contradiction, occupied-field, charter-home-rule, or specialized-preemption question
    Federal conflict Any express, field, or conflict-preemption issue under the relevant federal scheme
    Enforcement bridge Separate authority for removal, exclusion, citation, detention, arrest, or criminal prosecution
    Confidence VERIFIED, PARTIALLY-VERIFIED, or UNRESOLVED

    A missing local delegation document is not proof that no authority exists. Mark the chain unresolved and use targeted public-source research or CPRA to obtain the ordinance, resolution, charter provision, enabling law, delegation, contract, or other source needed to resolve it.

    The statewide framework is in Sweep 013 — Delegated Authority and Preemption.

    3. Build a pre-visit facility research packet¶

    Before field verification, gather what is reasonably available.

    Facility and access facts¶

    Record:

    • agency and facility name;
    • ownership or controlling agency where relevant;
    • exact areas open to the public;
    • public hours;
    • entrances and security checkpoints;
    • appointment, badge, sign-in, or identification conditions;
    • public counters, waiting rooms, meeting rooms, hallways, secured doors, employee workspaces, and exterior areas;
    • posted signs and their exact wording;
    • available floor plans, public maps, meeting-room diagrams, or visitor instructions.

    Do not assign one forum label to the entire address merely because the building has a public lobby.

    Property and boundary evidence — standard facility pass¶

    Property research is now a standard part of the facility SOP wherever practical, not something reserved only for locations that already look complicated.

    Every facility should receive a baseline property pass. A deeper boundary pass is required whenever ownership, control, right-of-way, easements, shared parking, plazas, approaches, or other boundary facts could materially affect access, forum, removal, or trespass analysis.

    This is especially important for sidewalks, plazas, forecourts, parking lots, shared civic campuses, leased facilities, joint-use buildings, transit property, large corporate/government campuses, and approaches that may cross from public right-of-way onto differently controlled property.

    Baseline property pass¶

    Where official sources are reasonably available:

    1. Search the facility address in county assessor, parcel, or official GIS sources.
    2. Record the parcel/APN or equivalent identifier.
    3. Record the current record owner if an official source supports it.
    4. Identify the operational agency/controller if different from the owner.
    5. Capture the official parcel/GIS source, layer name, and retrieval date.
    6. Identify immediately adjacent parcels when they affect entrances, sidewalks, plazas, parking, campus circulation, or likely field positions.
    7. Note any obvious public-right-of-way, easement, shared-control, or lease question.
    8. Assign a boundary confidence of VERIFIED, PARTIALLY-VERIFIED, or UNRESOLVED.

    A facility does not need a boundary dispute before this baseline pass is useful. The result may simply establish that no material property ambiguity has been identified.

    Deeper boundary pass when material¶

    When boundaries could change the legal analysis, continue the chain:

    1. Address → parcel/APN. Use assessor, county parcel, or official city/county GIS sources.
    2. APN → assessor parcel map / parcel-book reference. Record the map-book/page or equivalent reference where available.
    3. Parcel map → recorded maps and surveys. Look for subdivision maps, parcel maps, records of survey, corner records, deeds, or other recorded instruments needed to understand the geometry.
    4. Identify adjacent parcels. A parking lot, plaza, walkway, or campus may occupy a different parcel from the facility building.
    5. Resolve public right-of-way separately. Use official engineering, public-works, street, dedication, right-of-way, or survey records for sidewalks, street margins, medians, alleys, and approaches where relevant.
    6. Check easements and access rights. Look for public-access easements, dedications, reciprocal-access agreements, or other recorded interests when the activity depends on them. Do not assume that the absence of an easement from one map proves none exists.
    7. Resolve operational control separately from ownership. Review leases, licenses, MOUs, joint-use agreements, operating agreements, parking agreements, maintenance agreements, or similar records when another agency or entity may control the space.
    8. Overlay legal evidence with the physical site. Match verified lines and control facts to the actual entrance, sidewalk, forecourt, parking stalls, benches, landscaping, driveways, or other specific areas being analyzed.
    9. Preserve and grade the evidence. Record what each source proves, what it does not prove, and the remaining confidence level.
    10. Use public-records requests when necessary. If the public source record cannot resolve a material ownership/control/right-of-way question, request the deed, map, agreement, easement, dedication, or other underlying record rather than guessing.

    Useful official sources can include:

    • deeds and recorded parcel or subdivision maps;
    • county assessor or parcel records;
    • city/county GIS parcel layers;
    • official right-of-way, engineering, survey, or public-works maps;
    • city-owned or county-owned property layers;
    • recorded easements and dedication records;
    • leases, licenses, easements, joint-use agreements, MOUs, or operating agreements;
    • parking or maintenance agreements where they identify control of a specific area.

    Keep four questions separate:

    1. Who owns the land?
    2. Who controls or operates the particular area?
    3. What access rights, easements, or public-right-of-way interests apply?
    4. What constitutional forum classification follows for the particular activity?

    The answer to one does not automatically answer the others. A parcel may be government-owned but leased or operationally controlled by another entity; a sidewalk may lie within or next to a government parcel yet function as public right-of-way; a privately owned parcel may carry a public-access easement; and a publicly accessible parking lot does not become a traditional public forum merely because the public may park there.

    For each material boundary source, record the source URL, source type, parcel/APN or other identifier, layer or map name, date retrieved, what proposition the source supports, and any limitation. Use a boundary-confidence field such as VERIFIED, PARTIALLY-VERIFIED, or UNRESOLVED.

    When an interactive GIS viewer materially supports the facility analysis, preserve an export, PDF, or screenshot when practical in addition to the live link. Dynamic viewers can later change layers, symbology, parcel selection, or URLs. A preserved view should be treated as evidence of what the project reviewed on that date, not as a substitute for a deed, survey, recorded map, or other source needed to resolve a disputed legal boundary.

    For facilities where boundary evidence is material, keep a small facility-specific evidence manifest linking the live source and identifying any preserved copy. A practical project layout is docs/research/evidence/<facility-slug>/README.md, with preserved exports or screenshots stored alongside it when repository/file tooling permits. The manifest should say what each item proves and, just as importantly, what it does not prove.

    Consumer map imagery may help explain physical layout, but it should not be the sole source for a legal property-line conclusion.

    Policy and authority records¶

    Look for or request:

    • photography, video, audio, media, visitor-conduct, security, and public-access policies;
    • administrative regulations, general orders, manuals, directives, bulletins, FAQs, and staff instructions;
    • the ordinance, statute, regulation, resolution, order, or other authority cited for the policy;
    • adoption records, revision history, effective dates, and superseded versions;
    • training materials addressing cameras, auditors, media, disruptive visitors, removal, trespass, public comment, or records requests;
    • signage specifications or records showing who authorized a posted restriction;
    • security contracts and post orders where public and disclosable;
    • retention policies for surveillance, body-camera, dispatch, or incident records.

    The fact that a record is requested does not guarantee disclosure. Exemptions, privileges, privacy rules, and specialized statutes must still be analyzed.

    Prior-incident records¶

    Where relevant and lawfully obtainable, collect:

    • prior complaints about the same policy;
    • incident reports;
    • calls for service and CAD/dispatch records;
    • body-camera or other official recordings;
    • written trespass notices or exclusion orders;
    • citations or arrest records identifying the offense relied upon;
    • claims, lawsuits, settlements, published judicial decisions, or administrative rulings;
    • correspondence showing how the agency interpreted the policy;
    • later policy revisions following an incident.

    Preserve chronology. A policy adopted after an incident cannot be silently treated as the policy that governed the earlier event.

    Historical enforcement and chilling review¶

    Every mature pilot-facility sweep should ask not only what the rule says now, but whether government has previously warned, removed, excluded, cited, arrested, prosecuted, or otherwise burdened people for the same or materially similar activity.

    This review is documentary first. It is not a request to recreate an old confrontation.

    Potential sources include:

    • prior warnings, cease orders, removal directives, suspensions, bans, and trespass notices;
    • police calls for service, CAD/dispatch entries, incident reports, citations, arrests, and charging records;
    • criminal prosecutions and dispositions;
    • administrative complaints, internal reviews, claims, civil-rights suits, settlements, injunctions, and published opinions;
    • board, council, commission, or library minutes discussing expressive activity or enforcement;
    • superseded policies, training, directives, and signage;
    • records showing why a policy was revised or withdrawn; and
    • evidence that a person stopped otherwise protected or potentially protected activity because an official threatened enforcement.

    Useful search terms should be tailored to the facility but can include:

    leafleting, handbill, petition, solicitation, filming, photography, recording, First Amendment, free speech, trespass, removal, suspension, patron conduct, disturbance, harassment, protest, and demonstration.

    Chilling / compliance event¶

    An arrest or prosecution is not required before an enforcement event matters.

    A chilling / compliance event occurs when reliable evidence shows that a government warning, threat, rule, or enforcement posture caused a person to stop, relocate, refrain from, or materially alter protected or potentially protected activity.

    The project should record the event without automatically declaring it unconstitutional. The legal question remains whether the restriction was valid under the law controlling at that time.

    Prigmore v. City of Redding, 211 Cal.App.4th 1322 (2012), is a useful California library example: the record included warnings under the City's challenged leafleting policy and evidence that some people stopped leafleting because they feared arrest. The Court of Appeal later sustained preliminary injunctive relief against most of the challenged restrictions while treating the parking-lot vehicle-leafleting restriction differently. The lesson for this methodology is that chilling can be constitutionally material even when the event never becomes a completed prosecution.

    Date-sensitive historical assessment¶

    Historical enforcement must be assessed against the law, policy, area, and known facts that existed when the event occurred. Do not apply a later case or policy retroactively without analyzing whether it merely clarified existing law or actually changed the governing rule.

    For each historical event, use one of these working assessments:

    • ADJUDICATED_UNLAWFUL — a court or competent tribunal actually held the relevant challenged policy or enforcement unlawful on the issue being recorded;
    • APPARENTLY_INCONSISTENT_WITH_THEN_CONTROLLING_LAW — the governing authority already existed and the documented facts appear inconsistent with it, but no adjudication of this event is claimed;
    • CONSTITUTIONALLY_SUSPECT_NEEDS_RECORDS — available facts raise a concrete constitutional question, but records or factual development are still needed;
    • LATER_LAW_CHANGED_OR_CLARIFIED — later authority materially changed or clarified the analysis and the project should not silently project the modern rule backward;
    • LAWFUL_OR_DISTINGUISHABLE — the documented conduct, area, restriction, or legal posture materially differs from the protected scenario or is supported by identified authority; or
    • UNRESOLVED — the evidence or law does not support a more specific assessment.

    These are project research classifications. Except for ADJUDICATED_UNLAWFUL, they are not judicial findings.

    4. Use CPRA as a research tool, not as a slogan¶

    California Constitution article I, section 3(b) recognizes a right of access to information concerning the conduct of the people's business and provides that the writings of public officials and agencies shall be open to public scrutiny, subject to lawful limitations.

    Government Code § 7922.525 states that public records are open to inspection during agency office hours and that every person has a right to inspect public records except as otherwise provided. The existing CPRA chapter contains the project's detailed rules on requests, searches, exemptions, personal devices, retention, and preservation.

    Useful pre-visit requests can seek the actual policy and its provenance, not merely ask the agency whether recording is "allowed."

    See Public Records and Evidence Preservation.

    Primary sources:

    • California Constitution art. I, § 3(b): https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=CONS&article=I&sectionNum=SEC.+3.
    • Government Code § 7922.525: https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=GOV&sectionNum=7922.525

    5. Classify policy questions before the visit¶

    Facility files should use research flags rather than prematurely declaring an agency unlawful.

    Suggested flags:

    • SUPPORTED-BY-IDENTIFIED-AUTHORITY — a current source has been identified that appears to support the rule;
    • AUTHORITY-UNRESOLVED — the policy exists, but the project has not yet identified adequate authority for or against it;
    • POTENTIAL-FACIAL-CONFLICT — the written rule itself appears to conflict with controlling law and requires final legal review;
    • POTENTIAL-AS-APPLIED-CONFLICT — the rule may be lawful in some applications but prior enforcement raises a constitutional/statutory issue;
    • POTENTIAL-VIEWPOINT-COMPARATOR — comparable favorable and unfavorable expression appears to have been treated differently;
    • POLICY-TO-CRIMINAL-AUTHORITY-GAP — a policy violation appears to have been treated as sufficient basis for detention, citation, arrest, or criminal trespass without the required offense elements or legal bridge being identified;
    • SUPERSEDED-OR-REVISED — the policy has changed and historical versions must not be treated as current.

    These are research flags, not court holdings.

    6. Plan a peaceful field verification¶

    The visit should test the defined question while preserving the standing scenario.

    Before entering, decide:

    • what specific area is being observed;
    • what activity is being tested;
    • what policy is expected to be invoked;
    • what conduct is deliberately being avoided;
    • what factual questions would distinguish a lawful restriction from an unsupported one;
    • whether the visit will include a records request, observation only, consensual conversation, public comment, or some combination;
    • what evidence should be preserved if police are called.

    A useful investigation is reproducible. Another researcher should be able to understand what was tested and why.

    7. Consensual conversation can state the investigative purpose¶

    This methodology applies to an ordinary member of the public. It does not depend on profession, organizational affiliation, or carrying any project or role credential.

    If an official voluntarily asks what the person is doing, a truthful response can be as simple as:

    I am gathering information about this facility's recording and public-access policies and how they are enforced.

    Where prior research has identified a concrete issue:

    I am investigating whether this facility's policy or enforcement practice conflicts with California or federal law.

    Those statements explain the purpose of the activity. They do not claim special access or create a new legal privilege.

    8. Document policy enforcement as evidence¶

    During the visit, preserve:

    • the exact area and time;
    • normal public hours;
    • signs and where they are posted;
    • the identity/role of the official when reasonably available;
    • the exact rule or policy cited;
    • whether a written copy is offered or refused;
    • the legal authority the official says supports the rule;
    • the specific conduct the official says is prohibited;
    • whether similarly situated people are treated differently;
    • warnings and opportunities to comply;
    • whether the government describes the issue as policy, trespass, obstruction, disturbance, safety, privacy, security, or something else;
    • whether the reason changes after police arrive.

    Statements are evidence of what an official said. They are not, by themselves, proof that the official's legal interpretation is correct.

    9. If police arrive, document the policy-to-law transition¶

    A central research question is whether law enforcement identifies an independent legal basis for intervention or simply treats the employee's preference as sufficient.

    Useful factual questions include:

    • Am I free to leave?
    • Am I being detained?
    • What is the reason for the stop?
    • What specific offense do you reasonably suspect?
    • Which statute, ordinance, regulation, or other legal authority are you relying on?
    • What conduct are you saying satisfies its elements?
    • Is the issue a facility policy violation, or are you saying the policy violation itself constitutes a crime?
    • Who has authority to order removal from this particular area?
    • Is the order limited to this area or the entire property?

    The guide does not assume that every policy is invalid or that officers may never assist with a lawful facility restriction. It requires the authority chain to be identified.

    California has a separate stop-notice rule relevant to pedestrian detentions at facilities. Vehicle Code § 2806.5 requires a peace officer making a traffic or pedestrian stop, before questioning related to a criminal investigation or traffic violation, to state the reason for the stop, unless the officer reasonably believes withholding the reason is necessary to protect life or property from an imminent threat. The Field Guide retains the pedestrian-stop application here; traffic-stop doctrine is outside the current facility-audit focus. The statutory notice requirement is not the same as a requirement to disclose every fact underlying reasonable suspicion.

    Primary source: https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=VEH&sectionNum=2806.5

    10. Post-visit investigation¶

    The field visit usually creates a second records phase.

    Depending on the event, request or preserve:

    • CAD/dispatch logs;
    • body-camera and other official recordings;
    • incident reports;
    • employee emails or messages concerning the encounter where potentially public and disclosable;
    • supervisor instructions;
    • the policy/version actually relied upon;
    • records showing policy changes after the encounter;
    • retention schedules;
    • complaints, use-of-force records, citations, or exclusion records where applicable and lawfully available.

    Compare the contemporaneous records with what officials said on scene. Preserve contradictions without overstating what they prove.

    11. Member-of-public baseline¶

    The Field Guide does not depend on professional status or credentials.

    A person using this methodology is analyzed as a member of the public unless a genuinely role-specific law applies. The baseline is intended to work for a person carrying only a phone. The legal analysis starts with the rights, duties, and restrictions that apply to the person in the specific place and activity.

    No project ID or other self-created credential is required by this methodology. Likewise, the existence or absence of such a credential does not determine whether the person's activity is protected.

    Identification demands during police encounters are analyzed separately in police-encounters.md. The project does not treat an officer's desire to investigate, the fact that someone "called the police," or a request for identification as interchangeable with an actual legal duty or authority concerning identification.

    12. Facility-registry integration¶

    Every mature facility entry should eventually contain both legal classification and evidence history.

    Minimum fields:

    • facility and controlling agency;
    • specific area;
    • access status and public hours;
    • federal forum classification;
    • California Speech Clause classification;
    • activity analyzed;
    • current written policy;
    • policy source/provenance;
    • claimed legal authority;
    • signage/implementation;
    • prior incidents;
    • prior police/security enforcement;
    • exclusion/removal authority previously invoked;
    • offense/detention/arrest authority previously invoked;
    • comparator evidence;
    • current research flag(s);
    • field-verification date/result;
    • post-visit records obtained;
    • unresolved questions;
    • last verified.

    The resulting record should let a reader trace:

    controlling law → enacted authority → written policy → implementation → prior enforcement → field verification → post-incident records.

    That chain is the core investigative product.

    Quality rule¶

    The project should seek facilities with documented legal or policy questions, not invent misconduct to create a test.

    A strong investigation is one where the standing scenario remains intact and the disputed government action can therefore be evaluated without guessing whether unrelated obstruction, threats, noise, restricted-area entry, or other misconduct occurred.
