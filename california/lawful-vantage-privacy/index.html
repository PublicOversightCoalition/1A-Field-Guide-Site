    Lawful Vantage, Public View, and California Privacy Boundaries¶

    This chapter is the California overlay for national-core scenario NC-VANTAGE-01 — Lawful-Vantage Observation and Recording / Matters Exposed to Public View.

    It answers a recurring question:

    A person is somewhere they may lawfully be and can see a person, vehicle, building interior, document, screen, or other subject from there. What changes when the person looks, photographs, records video, captures audio, uses zoom or another device, crosses a boundary, or manipulates/accesses something?

    Short field rule¶

    Start with the exact vantage and the exact method. Ordinary visual observation, photography, or video of something genuinely exposed to ordinary public view from a place the observer may lawfully occupy is materially different from entering private/restricted property, defeating a barrier, using a device to obtain private activity that ordinary lawful viewing could not obtain, secretly viewing a legally protected private zone, recording a confidential communication, or accessing a computer/system.

    Two shortcuts are rejected:

    Visible does not mean "all privacy is gone."

    and

    Objection does not mean "recording is automatically unlawful."

    California law is context-specific. Ask who could ordinarily see or hear the subject, from where, through what shielding, by what technology, and under what independent privacy/property/audio rule.

    1. Establish the vantage before analyzing the camera¶

    Document:

    1. exact observer location;
    2. ownership/control/access status of that location;
    3. public hours or other right-to-remain conditions;
    4. whether the observer crossed a line, gate, doorway, fence, landscaping boundary, secured area, or other limit;
    5. whether the observer touched, opened, moved, reached through, repositioned, or defeated anything;
    6. whether the subject is public, private, or mixed;
    7. whether the view was ordinarily available to the general public or only to a limited audience;
    8. whether optical/electronic equipment materially changed what could be perceived; and
    9. whether audio was captured.

    A subject does not acquire control over adjacent property merely because the subject can be seen from there. Conversely, a person does not acquire a right to remain on private property merely because the property is open to some visitors.

    2. Public place observation versus intrusion¶

    California's intrusion tort supplies an important baseline.

    In Shulman v. Group W Productions, Inc., 18 Cal.4th 200 (1998), the California Supreme Court explained that intrusion requires an intentional intrusion into a private place, conversation, or matter in a manner highly offensive to a reasonable person. Ordinary observation, photography, or recording in a public place generally does not supply the private-place element merely because the subject would rather not be observed.

    That principle is useful, but it is not an all-purpose permission slip. The same case recognizes privacy in genuinely secluded places/conversations and rejects the idea that newsgathering creates a blanket privilege to violate generally applicable privacy law.

    Source: https://scocal.stanford.edu/opinion/shulman-v-group-w-productions-inc-31911/

    Field rule:

    If the subject is genuinely exposed to ordinary public observation from the observer's lawful location, that fact materially weakens an intrusion claim. If the observer penetrates a zone of seclusion or uses a different method to obtain otherwise private matter, run the privacy branches below.

    3. Visibility is not binary: general-public exposure versus limited audience¶

    Sanders v. American Broadcasting Companies, Inc., 20 Cal.4th 907 (1999) is critical because it rejects a simplistic rule that being visible or audible to someone eliminates all privacy.

    The California Supreme Court held that a worker in an office not generally open to unfettered public access could have a limited expectation against covert television recording even though coworkers could see or hear the same interactions.

    Source: https://law.justia.com/cases/california/supreme-court/4th/20/907.html

    Hernandez v. Hillsides, Inc., 47 Cal.4th 272 (2009) likewise recognized a reasonable visual-privacy expectation against hidden video monitoring in an enclosed shared office. The court separately concluded that the intrusion was not sufficiently offensive on the particular facts, illustrating that privacy expectation and ultimate liability are separate questions.

    Source: https://law.justia.com/cases/california/supreme-court/2009/s147552/

    Field distinction¶

    Ask:

    • Is this subject visible to anyone walking down an ordinary public sidewalk?
    • Or only to coworkers, tenants, invited customers, patients, staff, or another bounded group?
    • Is the observer using the same ordinary vantage those people use?
    • Did the subject employ doors, blinds, tint, partitions, walls, access controls, or positioning to limit the audience?
    • Is the recording persistent/covert in a way materially different from ordinary human observation?

    Do not replace those questions with:

    "Someone could see it, therefore everyone may record it."

    4. California constitutional privacy is a separate layer¶

    California Constitution article I, section 1 expressly protects privacy.

    Hill v. National Collegiate Athletic Assn., 7 Cal.4th 1 (1994) identifies the core state constitutional framework: a legally protected privacy interest, a reasonable expectation of privacy in the circumstances, and a serious invasion. Countervailing interests may affect the ultimate result.

    Source: https://law.justia.com/cases/california/supreme-court/4th/7/1.html

    This is another reason the Field Guide does not use visibility as a binary switch.

    Important limitation: the existence of a privacy interest does not by itself establish that a particular camera restriction, exclusion, arrest, or government policy is lawful. Government restrictions must still be tested under the relevant First Amendment/forum and other authority.

    California Constitution: https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displayText.xhtml?lawCode=CONS&article=I

    5. Windows, windshields, glass doors, and transparent barriers¶

    A transparent barrier is a fact, not a magic legal category.

    Record:

    • where the observer is standing;
    • whether that vantage is ordinarily available to the public;
    • whether the observer moved onto private/restricted property;
    • whether curtains, blinds, tint, privacy film, covers, fencing, orientation, or other shielding exists;
    • whether the observer opened, touched, moved, reached through, pressed against, or otherwise manipulated anything;
    • whether the observer used ordinary eyesight/camera capture or a device that materially revealed something ordinary lawful viewing could not;
    • the type of room/space being viewed; and
    • whether the observer had an intent made relevant by a particular statute.

    Penal Code § 647(i)¶

    Section 647(i) addresses peeking through a door/window while loitering, prowling, or wandering upon the private property of another without visible or lawful business with the owner or occupant.

    That private-property predicate matters. Do not paraphrase § 647(i) as though it criminalizes every look through every window from a public sidewalk.

    Source: https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=PEN&sectionNum=647

    Penal Code § 647(j)(1)¶

    Section 647(j)(1) separately reaches viewing by specified instrumentalities—including cameras, mobile phones, binoculars, and similar devices—into:

    • a bedroom;
    • bathroom;
    • changing/fitting/dressing room;
    • tanning booth; or
    • another area in which the occupant has a reasonable expectation of privacy,

    when done with the statutory intent to invade privacy.

    This means:

    Glass does not create a universal "window exception" to a privacy-zone statute.

    Apply the actual statutory elements.

    6. Fourth Amendment window cases are analogy only¶

    California search cases can help identify relevant vantage facts, but they regulate government searches and are not a civilian photography license.

    People v. Berutko, 71 Cal.2d 84 (1969)¶

    Police were lawfully present in a common area and observed through an aperture in the resident's window coverings. The court treated the lawful/common vantage and what was exposed from that vantage as important to the Fourth Amendment analysis.

    Source: https://law.justia.com/cases/california/supreme-court/2d/71/84.html

    Lorenzana v. Superior Court, 9 Cal.3d 626 (1973)¶

    The converse matters too: police use of a private side-yard vantage not open/common to the public changes the constitutional analysis.

    Source: https://law.justia.com/cases/california/supreme-court/3d/9/626.html

    People v. Camacho, 23 Cal.4th 824 (2000)¶

    The officers' presence in an area of private property where the public was not invited mattered even though they could see through an uncovered window.

    Source: https://law.justia.com/cases/california/supreme-court/4th/23/824.html

    Field Guide rule:

    These cases may support the factual question "Was this an ordinary lawful vantage?" They do not establish "anything police could constitutionally see may be photographed by any private person."

    7. Physical intrusion and device-assisted constructive intrusion¶

    Civil Code § 1708.8(a) — physical invasion¶

    Section 1708.8(a) creates civil liability when a person knowingly enters another's land/airspace without permission or otherwise trespasses in order to capture a visual image, sound recording, or other physical impression of the plaintiff engaged in private, personal, or familial activity, when the statutory offensiveness requirement is met.

    Civil Code § 1708.8(b) — constructive invasion¶

    Subdivision (b) does not require physical trespass. It addresses use of a device to offensively attempt to capture private/personal/familial activity where the image/sound/impression could not have been achieved without trespass unless the device were used.

    Source: https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=CIV&sectionNum=1708.8

    This gives the Field Guide a concrete California test for some technology-enhanced observation.

    Do not translate that statute into:

    "Using zoom is illegal."

    Instead ask the statutory question:

    Did the device permit capture of private activity that could not otherwise have been achieved without trespass?

    The answer is fact-specific.

    8. Ordinary camera capture versus persistent/covert surveillance¶

    California privacy cases recognize that recording technology can sometimes be more intrusive than ordinary human observation.

    In Hernandez, the court discussed the privacy significance of persistent hidden video monitoring while still requiring the separate offensiveness/seriousness analysis.

    Accordingly, preserve:

    • camera placement;
    • duration;
    • concealment;
    • proximity;
    • focus;
    • repeated targeting;
    • optical enhancement;
    • whether the view was general-public or limited; and
    • whether a specific statute adds an intent requirement.

    The project does not label normal handheld recording in a public place "surveillance" merely because it is continuous. The point is to preserve legally relevant facts rather than assume the answer.

    9. Documents, computer screens, identifiers, and other exposed information¶

    A visible screen/document problem should be broken into separate propositions.

    A. The custodian displayed or failed to shield the information¶

    Document what was visible from the ordinary lawful vantage and whether shielding/access controls existed.

    B. The observer looked at or photographed what was ordinarily visible¶

    Analyze the applicable privacy/confidentiality law. Ask whom that law regulates and what acquisition/use conduct it restricts.

    A label such as:

    "confidential"

    does not itself establish the observer's liability.

    But visibility also does not automatically establish that all privacy/confidentiality interests disappeared.

    C. The observer accesses the device/system/data¶

    That is a different factual event.

    Penal Code § 502 regulates specified unauthorized access to computers, systems, networks, programs, services, and data.

    Source: https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=PEN&sectionNum=502

    Field rule:

    Seeing a display left visible from your lawful position is analytically different from touching the computer, navigating it, logging in, opening files, accessing an account/network, defeating access controls, or otherwise obtaining stored data through the system.

    This chapter does not state that photographing a screen visible from a lawful position is itself § 502 "access."

    10. HIPAA and other custodian-side duties do not answer the observer question by themselves¶

    Existing Indoor Public Common Spaces research shows why proposition separation matters.

    HIPAA may impose safeguard duties on a covered entity. That does not automatically mean an unrelated visitor becomes the covered entity's compliance agent. Conversely, the custodian's obligation to safeguard information does not establish that the observer is free from every independently applicable privacy law.

    Use:

    custodian duty → observer conduct → independent observer law

    as three separate propositions.

    For medical, student, victim/witness, court, financial, or other specially protected data, identify the particular statute/regulation before drawing a conclusion.

    11. Audio is a separate branch¶

    Visual recording and audio recording are not interchangeable in California.

    Penal Code § 632¶

    Section 632 regulates intentional nonconsensual electronic amplification/recording of a confidential communication.

    The statute's definition excludes communications made:

    • in public gatherings;
    • in open legislative, judicial, executive, or administrative proceedings open to the public; or
    • in circumstances where the parties may reasonably expect the communication may be overheard or recorded.

    Source: https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=PEN&sectionNum=632

    Flanagan v. Flanagan, 27 Cal.4th 766 (2002)¶

    The California Supreme Court held that § 632 confidentiality turns on an objectively reasonable expectation that the conversation is not being overheard or recorded, rather than merely an expectation that its contents will not later be repeated.

    Source: https://law.justia.com/cases/california/supreme-court/4th/27/766.html

    Field rule:

    A visually unobjectionable camera angle can still create a separate California audio question.

    Penal Code § 632.7 and other communications provisions may apply to specialized telephone/electronic contexts and should be checked separately when those facts are supplied.

    12. Public-interest recording remains a separate First Amendment layer¶

    This privacy chapter does not replace the existing recording doctrine.

    The Ninth Circuit's published authorities include:

    • Fordyce v. City of Seattle, 55 F.3d 436 (9th Cir. 1995), involving filming matters of public interest/police activity; and
    • Askins v. U.S. Department of Homeland Security, 899 F.3d 1035 (9th Cir. 2018), emphasizing exact location, forum, restriction, and government justification in a photography dispute.

    California Penal Code § 148(g) also provides that, within its statutory location conditions, photographing or making audio/video recordings of a public/peace officer does not by itself establish Penal Code § 148(a), reasonable suspicion, or probable cause.

    See Recording Public Officials and Police.

    Those authorities do not erase California privacy law. Privacy law likewise does not erase First Amendment scrutiny of a government camera restriction.

    13. Private property does not use one statewide slogan¶

    Two California Supreme Court decisions illustrate why the observer's right to remain must be established rather than assumed.

    Robins v. Pruneyard Shopping Center, 23 Cal.3d 899 (1979)¶

    California recognized state-constitutional speech/petition protection on the facts of a large privately owned shopping center open to the public.

    Source: https://scocal.stanford.edu/opinion/robins-v-pruneyard-shopping-center-30510/

    Golden Gateway Center v. Golden Gateway Tenants Assn., 26 Cal.4th 1013 (2001)¶

    The judgment rejected the claimed state-constitutional leafleting right in a private residential portion of a complex not freely/openly accessible to the public. The three-justice lead opinion would have adopted a broad state-action limitation for article I, section 2(a); Chief Justice George concurred in the judgment on a narrower ground and expressly did not join that broad state-action discussion. The Field Guide therefore uses the case for its narrower private-property/access boundary, not as a majority holding establishing a universal state-action rule.

    Source: https://scocal.stanford.edu/opinion/golden-gateway-center-v-golden-gateway-tenants-assn-32146/

    Field rule:

    Do not infer either that every private business may remove every speaker for any reason or that a business open to customers has become public property. Identify the actual property/access right, any California private-property speech doctrine, the observer's location, and the conduct at issue.

    The existing trespass/property chapter supplies the separate statutory layer.

    14. "Stop filming," "move away," or "leave"¶

    An objection does not finish the analysis.

    Record:

    1. who made the demand;
    2. what property that person/entity actually controls;
    3. the observer's exact location;
    4. the activity the demand targets;
    5. the specific privacy/property/policy authority cited;
    6. whether the observer has crossed or manipulated anything;
    7. whether any independent misconduct occurred; and
    8. whether police/security later translated the objection into "trespass" or "illegal recording."

    If government conditions continued access on ending recording/speech/observation, also run NC-COMPLAINT-01.M — conditional access / derivative trespass.

    That California exclusion issue is maintained separately in GAP-02; closing GAP-01 does not prejudge whether a particular "stop recording or leave" order is lawful.

    15. Operational decision tree¶

    For ordinary California lawful-vantage disputes:

    Where exactly am I?
        ↓
Do I have a present right to remain at this vantage?
        ↓
Is the subject exposed to general public view,
or only to a limited/controlled audience?
        ↓
Did I cross, touch, open, move, reach through,
manipulate, defeat shielding, or access a system?
        ↓
Could ordinary lawful viewing perceive the matter,
or did a device materially obtain otherwise inaccessible private activity?
        ↓
Is this a §647(j)-type protected private zone / privacy intent issue?
        ↓
Is audio being captured?
        ↓
Does §632 or another communication rule apply?
        ↓
Is sensitive data involved?
        ↓
Which law regulates the custodian, the observer,
acquisition, use, or publication?
        ↓
Has someone demanded stop / move / leave?
        ↓
What independent authority supports that demand?

    16. Changed-fact and specialized checks¶

    This ordinary branch does not claim completeness for every specialized privacy regime.

    Open a dedicated check when material facts involve:

    • courtrooms or court-specific rules;
    • K-12/school or child-specific rules;
    • sexual/intimate-image recording or distribution;
    • medical/mental-health records or treatment spaces;
    • victim/witness protection;
    • trade secrets/proprietary data;
    • biometric/facial-recognition collection;
    • special surveillance equipment;
    • contractual confidentiality duties;
    • publication/private-facts liability distinct from acquisition;
    • federal property; or
    • another statute specifically regulating the subject/data/location.

    Those specialized facts do not keep the ordinary lawful-vantage branch in perpetual Gap status. They are changed-fact triggers.

    17. Project rule¶

    The California overlay uses this shorthand:

    Lawful vantage is the starting question, not the final answer. Visibility is evidence, not a universal waiver of privacy. Objection is evidence, not a universal camera ban.

    Then identify the actual California privacy/property/audio/access rule.
