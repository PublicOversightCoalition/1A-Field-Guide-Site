    Sweep 004 — Police Encounters: Fourth Amendment + Self-Incrimination¶

    Date reviewed: September 21, 2026
    Status: Core encounter doctrine reviewed in part; substantial edge-case/remedies work remains pending.

    Defined scope¶

    Sweep 004 builds the encounter spine that commonly follows peaceful First Amendment activity:

    1. consensual police contact;
    2. Fourth Amendment seizure/detention;
    3. Terry reasonable suspicion;
    4. scope and duration;
    5. identification requests and California-specific identification law, including the distinction between carrying ID, stating identity, and producing possessed identification;
    6. protective frisks;
    7. probable-cause arrest;
    8. Fifth Amendment / Miranda questioning;
    9. physical seizure versus digital search of phones/cameras;
    10. field language that preserves facts without treating slogans as law.

    This is a targeted operational/doctrinal sweep, not a complete Fourth Amendment, Fifth Amendment, California criminal-procedure, device-search, or remedies sweep.

    Constitutional architecture¶

    Authority Status Narrow proposition

    U.S. Const. amend. XIV DOCTRINE-REVIEWED-IN-PART Incorporation against California state/local actors; independent 14A doctrine remains broader.
    Cal. Const. art. I, § 13 PRIMARY-TEXT-VERIFIED State search/seizure protection; independent state doctrine pending.
    Cal. Const. art. I, § 15 PRIMARY-TEXT-VERIFIED State self-incrimination/due-process text; independent state doctrine pending.

    Incorporation cases¶

    Mapp v. Ohio, 367 U.S. 643 (1961)¶

    Binding: U.S. Supreme Court.
    Retained: Fourth Amendment protection/exclusionary layer applies against the States through the Fourteenth Amendment. The full exclusionary-rule doctrine is outside this sweep.

    Official source: https://www.supremecourt.gov/pdfs/USReports/USREPORTS-367_PDFA.pdf

    Malloy v. Hogan, 378 U.S. 1 (1964)¶

    Binding: U.S. Supreme Court.
    Retained: the Fourteenth Amendment secures the Fifth Amendment privilege against self-incrimination against state infringement.

    Official source: https://www.supremecourt.gov/pdfs/USReports/USREPORTS-378_PDFA.pdf

    Core Fourth Amendment cases¶

    Terry v. Ohio, 392 U.S. 1 (1968)¶

    DOCTRINE-REVIEWED: investigative detention may rest on reasonable suspicion supported by specific/articulable facts; stop and protective frisk are distinct; frisk requires an armed-and-dangerous safety basis; stop must be justified at inception and appropriately limited in scope.

    Official source: https://www.supremecourt.gov/pdfs/USReports/USREPORTS-392_PDFA.pdf

    United States v. Sharpe, 470 U.S. 675 (1985)¶

    DOCTRINE-REVIEWED-IN-PART: no rigid universal stopwatch; duration analysis includes police diligence and circumstances.

    Official source: https://www.supremecourt.gov/pdfs/USReports/USREPORTS-470_PDFA.pdf

    Devenpeck v. Alford, 543 U.S. 146 (2004)¶

    DOCTRINE-REVIEWED: warrantless-arrest probable cause is objective; the offense supported by known facts need not be the offense announced by the arresting officer.

    Official source: https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/boundvolumes/543bv.pdf

    Rodriguez v. United States, 575 U.S. 348 (2015)¶

    SPECIALIZED / DOCTRINE-REVIEWED-IN-PART: traffic stop may not be prolonged beyond its traffic mission to pursue unrelated investigation without an independent legal basis.

    Official source: https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/boundvolumes/575bv.pdf

    California seizure/reasonable-suspicion cases¶

    People v. Tacardon, 14 Cal.5th 235 (2022)¶

    Binding: California Supreme Court.
    DOCTRINE-REVIEWED: consensual encounter versus detention uses totality of circumstances; directed police attention/spotlight did not automatically create a seizure on the facts there.

    Official sources: - https://supreme.courts.ca.gov/case/s264219-people-v-tacardon - https://www.courts.ca.gov/opinions/archive/S264219.PDF

    People v. Flores, 15 Cal.5th 1032 (2024)¶

    Binding: California Supreme Court.
    DOCTRINE-REVIEWED: reasonable suspicion requires particularized/objective grounds under the totality; high-crime-area context is not by itself individualized suspicion; facts there were insufficient. Evasive/nervous conduct remains potentially relevant in context rather than categorically irrelevant.

    Official sources: - https://supreme.courts.ca.gov/case/s267522-people-v-flores - https://courts.ca.gov/opinion/citings-archive/2024-05-02/s267522

    First/Fourth Amendment statutory bridge¶

    Penal Code § 148(g)¶

    Recording a public/peace officer in a public place or from a place the recorder has a right to be does not in and of itself establish §148 obstruction, reasonable suspicion, or probable cause.

    Primary source: https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=PEN&sectionNum=148

    Status: PRIMARY-TEXT-VERIFIED.

    Vehicle Code § 2806.5 — pedestrian-stop notice relevant to this project¶

    Effective January 1, 2024, a peace officer making a traffic or pedestrian stop must, before questioning related to a criminal investigation or traffic violation, state the reason for the stop. The officer must document the reason on any resulting citation or police report. The statute contains an exception when the officer reasonably believes withholding the reason is necessary to protect life or property from an imminent threat.

    Primary source: https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=VEH&sectionNum=2806.5

    Status: PRIMARY-TEXT-VERIFIED.

    People v. Valle, A169080 (Cal. Ct. App. Sept. 18, 2024)¶

    Binding level: published California Court of Appeal, First District, Division One.
    DOCTRINE-REVIEWED-IN-PART: the opinion recognizes § 2806.5's California stop-notice requirement and treats it as distinct from the federal Fourth Amendment question whether the stop was objectively justified. Valle itself arose from a traffic stop; the project retains it only for interpretation of a statute that also expressly covers pedestrian stops. The statute requires notice of the reason for the stop before the covered questioning; it does not convert the on-scene encounter into a requirement that the officer recite every fact later relied upon to establish reasonable suspicion. Valle also notes that proposed suppression language was removed before enactment and rejects treating the statute as altering federal Fourth Amendment admissibility analysis.

    Official California Judicial Branch source: https://courts.ca.gov/opinion/published-extended-post/2024-09-18/a169080

    Operational distinction: reasonable suspicion must actually exist when the seizure begins. Section 2806.5 may require the officer to state the reason for the stop before covered questioning, but later facts and later explanations cannot retroactively create reasonable suspicion for an earlier seizure.

    California identification statutory layer¶

    Penal Code § 148.9¶

    False representation/identification as another or fictitious person to specified officers upon qualifying lawful detention/arrest, with the statutory evasive purpose.

    Primary source: https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=PEN&sectionNum=148.9

    Retained distinction: false identity is an independent offense question; §148.9 does not itself state a universal affirmative duty for every detained pedestrian to identify.

    Penal Code § 853.5¶

    Specified infraction-arrest/citation process addressing satisfactory identification and fingerprint/thumbprint procedure.

    Primary source: https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=PEN&sectionNum=853.5

    Vehicle Code § 12951¶

    Driver-specific license possession/presentation requirement.

    Primary source: https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=VEH&sectionNum=12951

    Vehicle Code § 40302¶

    Specified custody/magistrate rules after arrest for nonfelony Vehicle Code violations, including identity circumstances.

    Primary source: https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=VEH&sectionNum=40302

    Stop-and-identify and California §148 case layer¶

    Hiibel v. Sixth Judicial District Court, 542 U.S. 177 (2004)¶

    Binding: U.S. Supreme Court.
    DOCTRINE-REVIEWED: Nevada enacted a specific stop-and-identify statute; the Court upheld its name requirement during a valid Terry stop on the facts there. Hiibel does not itself create a California identification duty. Its Fifth Amendment holding was also fact-specific: stating Hiibel's name did not present a reasonable danger of incrimination there.

    Official source: https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/boundvolumes/542bv.pdf

    Kolender v. Lawson, 461 U.S. 352 (1983)¶

    Binding: U.S. Supreme Court.
    CONTEXT / DOCTRINE-REVIEWED: California's former credible and reliable identification scheme was invalidated as unconstitutionally vague. This history does not invalidate all modern California context-specific identification requirements.

    Official discussion/source: https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/boundvolumes/542bv.pdf (Hiibel's discussion of Kolender); official U.S. Reports citation 461 U.S. 352.

    United States v. Landeros, 913 F.3d 862 (9th Cir. 2019)¶

    Binding: published Ninth Circuit federal authority in California.
    DOCTRINE-REVIEWED: where officers lacked reasonable suspicion that a passenger had committed an offense, refusal to identify did not itself furnish reasonable suspicion and did not justify prolonging the traffic stop.

    Official source: https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2019/01/11/17-10217.pdf

    People v. Quiroga, 16 Cal.App.4th 961 (1993)¶

    Binding level: published California Court of Appeal.
    DOCTRINE-REVIEWED-IN-PART:

    • slow compliance/verbal argument did not automatically constitute Penal Code §148 obstruction;
    • refusal to state a name after arrest while being transported did not itself violate §148 because arrest had already been effected and booking had not begun;
    • refusal to disclose identity during a felony booking interview could violate §148 by obstructing/delaying booking;
    • the opinion distinguished misdemeanor/infraction contexts governed by other identification/release statutes.

    The Judicial Council's current CALCRIM authority notes cite Quiroga under Verbal Resistance or Obstruction and describe the nondisclosure proposition as following arrest for a felony, not misdemeanor.

    Official Judicial Council CALCRIM source: https://courts.ca.gov/advisory-body/criminal-jury-instructions-advisory-committee
    Published opinion reproduction: https://law.justia.com/cases/california/court-of-appeal/4th/16/961.html

    In re Chase C., 243 Cal.App.4th 107 (2015)¶

    Binding level: published California Court of Appeal.
    DOCTRINE-REVIEWED-IN-PART: on the facts there, refusal to identify while detained/postarrest but before booking did not constitute §148 obstruction; the court characterized that refusal as protected Fifth Amendment silence and distinguished booking-stage refusal and false identification. The opinion also emphasized that refusal to cooperate becomes criminal under §148 only when it actually obstructs lawful police activity.

    Published opinion reproduction: https://law.justia.com/cases/california/court-of-appeal/2015/d067787.html

    Bounded California conclusion¶

    This targeted sweep has not identified a current general California pedestrian carry-ID statute or a general statutory stop-and-identify law comparable to Nevada's statute in Hiibel.

    Do not inflate that into either of these claims:

    • I can never be required to identify or produce possessed identification in California.
    • If I am lawfully detained, refusing my name automatically violates §148.

    The first proposition is too broad because older published California cases, including People v. Long and People v. Loudermilk, address production/search for identification during otherwise lawful detentions under fact-specific Fourth Amendment reasoning. Loudermilk itself emphasized the limits of its holding, and later cases including People v. Garcia address limits on identification searches. That line requires a dedicated reconciliation before the Field Guide states a categorical rule about compelled production of possessed identification during a lawful detention.

    The better current rule is:

    No general carry-ID or statutory pedestrian stop-and-identify law has been identified. Separately analyze whether a specific context or case-law rule permits an officer to require particular identifying information or possessed proof of identity. Silence/refusal is not automatically §148 obstruction; booking and specialized statutory contexts can be different; false identity is separately regulated by §148.9.

    Fifth Amendment / Miranda layer¶

    Berkemer v. McCarty, 468 U.S. 420 (1984)¶

    DOCTRINE-REVIEWED: a person can be seized/not free to leave under the Fourth Amendment without automatically being in Miranda custody; ordinary traffic/Terry-type stops generally are not custodial solely because freedom of movement is curtailed.

    Official source: https://www.supremecourt.gov/pdfs/USReports/USREPORTS-468_PDFA.pdf

    Berghuis v. Thompkins, 560 U.S. 370 (2010)¶

    DOCTRINE-REVIEWED: in Miranda custody, invocation of the right to remain silent must be unambiguous; prolonged silence did not constitute invocation on the facts there.

    Official source: https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/boundvolumes/560bv.pdf

    Salinas v. Texas, 570 U.S. 178 (2013)¶

    DOCTRINE-REVIEWED-IN-PART / plurality-limited: in a voluntary noncustodial interview, the plurality rejected the Fifth Amendment claim where Salinas became silent but did not expressly invoke. Do not state a categorical majority rule that silence never invokes the privilege in any setting.

    Official source: https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/boundvolumes/570bv.pdf

    Operational consequence: if intending to invoke, clear words are safer than relying on silence alone:

    I invoke my right to remain silent. I do not want to answer investigative questions.

    This is not magic phrasing.

    Phone/device doctrine¶

    Riley v. California, 573 U.S. 373 (2014)¶

    DOCTRINE-REVIEWED: search incident to arrest does not ordinarily authorize a warrantless search of digital information on a seized cellphone; police generally need a warrant for digital contents subject to recognized exceptions. Physical seizure/retention and digital-content search remain analytically distinct.

    Official source: https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/boundvolumes/573BV.pdf

    Still RESEARCH: physical seizure/retention short of arrest; device warrant particularity; exigency details; compelled passcodes/biometrics/encryption; testimonial-act/foregone-conclusion doctrine; deletion/interference with recordings.

    Practical distinctions established by Sweep 004¶

    1. Police contact ≠ detention. Mere approach/questioning can remain consensual.
    2. Detention ≠ arrest. Terry permits a limited seizure on reasonable suspicion.
    3. Detention ≠ frisk. A frisk needs its own armed-and-dangerous basis.
    4. Detention ≠ Miranda custody. Fourth and Fifth Amendment thresholds differ.
    5. Reasonable suspicion ≠ probable cause. Arrest ordinarily requires the higher threshold.
    6. Recording alone ≠ obstruction/RS/PC. Penal Code §148(g) says so expressly within its scope.
    7. Requesting ID ≠ proof of a general carry-ID or stop-and-identify law. Identify the actual statute, context, and applicable case law before treating the request as compulsory.
    8. Refusal/silence ≠ automatic §148 obstruction. Quiroga and Chase C. add California-specific limits; booking can be different.
    9. Refusing ID ≠ license to give false ID. §148.9 is separate.
    10. Hiibel ≠ nationwide ID mandate. It upheld Nevada's statute during a valid Terry stop.
    11. Reason for stop ≠ full RAS recital. For the pedestrian-stop setting relevant to this project, Vehicle Code §2806.5 requires the reason for the stop before covered questioning, subject to its exception; that is distinct from disclosing every fact underlying reasonable suspicion.
    12. Officer's announced offense ≠ entire arrest analysis. Devenpeck makes probable cause objective.
    13. Device seizure ≠ digital search. Riley sharply distinguishes digital contents.
    14. No Miranda warning ≠ illegal arrest. Arrest legality and interrogation safeguards are separate.
    15. Silence ≠ safest invocation strategy. Clear invocation avoids relying on inference.

    California-specific¶

    • independent article I §§13 and 15 doctrine;
    • specialized California ID duties;
    • reconcile older identification-production/search cases, including People v. Long, People v. Loudermilk, People v. Garcia, and later applications before stating a categorical rule about compelled production of possessed identification during a lawful detention;
    • arrest/citation rules beyond the provisions reviewed here;
    • state cases on detention becoming arrest;
    • probation/parole/search-condition encounters where relevant;
    • campus/courthouse/facility ID rules.
