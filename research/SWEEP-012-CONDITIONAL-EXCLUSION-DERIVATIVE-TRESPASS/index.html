    Sweep 012 — California Conditional Access, Removal, and Derivative Trespass¶

    Issue: #66
    Package: GAP-02
    Research date: September 28, 2026
    Status: Defined GAP-02 slice reviewed; affected qualification rows must be re-evaluated individually.

    Scope¶

    This sweep addresses the recurring sequence:

    lawful presence → protected/arguably protected activity → "stop the activity or leave" → refusal/continued activity → exclusion/removal → trespass enforcement

    The affected qualification rows are:

    • CA-F3.4
    • CA-F4.1
    • CA-F4.6
    • NC-COMPLAINT-01.M
    • NC-VANTAGE-01.G

    The sweep does not assume that:

    • every public-facing government interior is a public forum;
    • every camera/speech restriction is invalid;
    • government must wait for actual disruption before enforcing every valid restriction;
    • every order to leave public property is lawful;
    • refusal to comply with an invalid order can never implicate another law; or
    • a later arrest is automatically unconstitutional merely because protected activity preceded it.

    The purpose is to prevent the later word trespass from erasing the upstream legal questions.

    1. Current California primary text¶

    Penal Code § 602.1(b) — public agency open to the public¶

    Current official text requires, for the public-agency branch:

    1. intentional interference with lawful agency business;
    2. by obstructing or intimidating people attempting to carry on agency business or people there to transact business;
    3. refusal to leave after a qualifying request by the office manager/supervisor or a peace officer acting at that person's request.

    Official source: https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=PEN&sectionNum=602.1.

    Subdivision (d)(2) expressly states that § 602.1 does not apply to a person on the premises who is engaging in activities protected by the California Constitution or United States Constitution.

    Subdivision (e) separately states that § 602.1 does not supersede other law.

    Project consequence¶

    Do not rewrite § 602.1(b) as:

    government employee asks you to leave + you remain = misdemeanor.

    The obstruction/intimidation/interference predicate is independent from refusal.

    The constitutional exception also requires the Field Guide to resolve the protected-activity question rather than treating the request to leave as self-validating.

    But § 602.1(e) means the exception does not immunize conduct from some other independently applicable statute.

    Penal Code § 602 — recurring public-facility branches¶

    Official source: https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=PEN&sectionNum=602.

    § 602(o)¶

    The current statute applies to refusing/failing to leave property not open to the general public after a qualifying request.

    Field consequence:

    Do not use § 602(o) as the automatic refusal-to-leave statute for an ordinary public-facing government lobby that is presently open to the general public.

    A different area of the same facility can have a different access status.

    § 602(q)¶

    The current statute applies to a public building of a public agency during hours when the building is regularly closed to the public, after a qualifying request, when surrounding circumstances would indicate no apparent lawful business.

    Field consequence:

    Regular closing time can independently change the legal posture even when the person's earlier presence was lawful.

    § 602(m)¶

    Existing Sweep 002 authority establishes that entering and occupying requires more than transient/minutes-long presence; In re Catalano and Edgerly remain the controlling project anchors for that narrow proposition.

    Specialized branches¶

    Other § 602 subdivisions apply to secured, posted, screened, private, closed, hospital, transit, airport, or other specialized property facts. They must be tested by their own elements.

    2. Published Ninth Circuit: refusal alone does not manufacture § 602.1 interference¶

    Dubner v. City & County of San Francisco, 266 F.3d 959 (9th Cir. 2001)¶

    Dubner involved an arrest near the Moscone Convention Center. The published Ninth Circuit opinion analyzed § 602.1's common intentional-interference/refusal structure.

    The court held that even assuming Dubner refused to leave, there was no evidence she intentionally interfered with the business. She was taking photographs and conversing while positioned away from the doors. The court expressly rejected the government's argument that intent to interfere could simply be inferred from refusal to leave.

    Source: https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/266/959/582256/

    Project rule from Dubner¶

    Refusal is not a substitute for the interference predicate.

    Because the public-agency branch uses the same intentional-interference-by-obstruction-or-intimidation structure, this is an important published Ninth Circuit anchor for element separation.

    Do not misdescribe Dubner as a published holding about every application of § 602.1(b); its facts involved the Moscone Center/convention setting. Its direct value here is the statutory-element separation.

    3. Direct public-agency comparator: Mackey v. Meyer¶

    Mackey v. Meyer, No. 15-55186 (9th Cir. Jan. 11, 2017)¶

    Status: unpublished memorandum; not precedent except as allowed by Ninth Circuit Rule 36-3.

    Mackey was reading scripture outside a California DMV. A security guard asked him to leave; he refused; DMV personnel requested removal; dispatch told CHP that a preacher was harassing customers and refusing to leave.

    The Ninth Circuit specifically analyzed Penal Code § 602.1(b). It stated the two-part structure as intentional interference plus refusal to leave, with intentional interference requiring obstruction or intimidation.

    The officer arrived and saw Mackey reading aloud somewhat apart from the line, with no obstruction or intimidation. The panel held there was no probable cause for § 602.1(b) and that the officer was not entitled to qualified immunity on the Fourth Amendment claim.

    Official Ninth Circuit memorandum: https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/memoranda/2017/01/11/15-55186.pdf

    Project use¶

    Mackey is an unusually close factual comparator for a public agency but remains nonprecedential.

    It strongly illustrates the operational question:

    What did the officer actually know about obstruction/intimidation, beyond the labels harassing, refusing to leave, or they want him removed?

    It should not be promoted to binding authority.

    4. California constitutional line: an unsupported exclusion cannot bootstrap itself¶

    In re Hoffman, 67 Cal.2d 845 (1967)¶

    The California Supreme Court considered peaceful antiwar leafleting at Los Angeles Union Station. An officer told participants to stop distributing leaflets/talking to people or leave; they refused and were arrested.

    The court held that the primary uses of property can be protected through rules against actual interference, but First Amendment activity could not be prohibited solely because the property was not maintained primarily as a speech forum on those facts.

    The court relied on Brown v. Louisiana for a particularly important proposition: where peaceful protesters in a public library had not disrupted the facility, the librarian/officer lacked a constitutionally sufficient basis to order them out merely for their protected protest, and the refusal to leave could not itself constitutionally support the breach-of-peace conviction there.

    Source: https://law.justia.com/cases/california/supreme-court/2d/67/845.html

    Brown v. Louisiana, 383 U.S. 131 (1966)¶

    The U.S. Supreme Court reversed convictions arising from a peaceful, orderly library protest. The plurality stressed that regulation of public facilities must be reasonable and nondiscriminatory and may not be used as a pretext to punish constitutional activity.

    Official U.S. Reports source: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-383/pdf/USREPORTS-383-131.pdf

    Narrow project rule¶

    An otherwise unsupported exclusion order does not automatically become constitutionally sufficient merely because the person refuses it.

    This is not a universal right to remain after every order. It is a rule against treating the downstream refusal as if it answers whether the upstream exclusion was lawful.

    5. Government can enforce valid restrictions before actual disruption¶

    The opposite overstatement is also wrong.

    Sammartano v. First Judicial District Court, 303 F.3d 959 (9th Cir. 2002)¶

    The Ninth Circuit treated the Nevada judicial/municipal complex before it as a nonpublic forum.

    Speech restrictions there still had to be:

    1. reasonable in light of the forum's purpose; and
    2. viewpoint neutral.

    The court emphasized that nonpublic-forum reasonableness requires more than ordinary rational-basis review and that the government must have evidentiary support connecting the restriction to a legitimate need.

    Official Ninth Circuit opinion: https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2002/08/26/0116685.pdf

    Project consequence¶

    A person does not acquire immunity from a valid rule merely because no actual disruption has occurred yet.

    A reasonable, viewpoint-neutral rule designed to preserve the function of a nonpublic forum can regulate activity prospectively.

    But the rule must still survive the applicable constitutional test.

    Simpson v. Municipal Court, 14 Cal.App.3d 591 (1971)¶

    Simpson involved peaceful, nonobstructive picketing inside the California State Capitol.

    The court upheld a specific statutory ban on picketing inside the Capitol because it was a narrow restriction directed at a particular expressive method in a location whose deliberative function supplied a substantial governmental interest. Other channels of communication remained open.

    Source: https://law.justia.com/cases/california/court-of-appeal/3d/14/591.html

    Project consequence¶

    Absence of actual disruption is relevant but not universally dispositive.

    The legal question is whether the restriction is valid under the applicable constitutional/forum rule—not simply whether misconduct had already occurred.

    People v. Fogelson, 21 Cal.3d 158 (1978)¶

    The California Supreme Court reiterated that government may reasonably regulate protected activity to prevent fraud, harassment, or interference with operations, but regulation affecting First Amendment activity requires precision and narrowly drawn standards related to permissible interests.

    Source: https://law.justia.com/cases/california/supreme-court/3d/21/158.html

    6. Neutral closure and access changes are separate from expressive exclusion¶

    Parrish v. Municipal Court, 258 Cal.App.2d 497 (1968)¶

    The case involved protesters remaining in a county welfare building after it had regularly closed for the day.

    The court treated the predecessor of current § 602(q) as a closing-hours rule. The fact that presence had previously been lawful did not freeze the access status indefinitely after regular public closing.

    Source: https://law.justia.com/cases/california/court-of-appeal/2d/258/497.html

    Project rule¶

    When facts change, rerun the access analysis.

    Examples:

    • regular public hours end;
    • a public room closes for a scheduled use;
    • a security perimeter is lawfully established;
    • the person enters a restricted subarea;
    • a valid neutral facility rule becomes applicable; or
    • independent obstruction/disruption arises.

    Do not treat those situations as equivalent to an exclusion imposed solely because an official dislikes recording or criticism.

    7. Recording-specific First Amendment layer¶

    Askins v. U.S. Department of Homeland Security, 899 F.3d 1035 (9th Cir. 2018)¶

    The Ninth Circuit recognized First Amendment protection for photographing/recording matters of public interest and emphasized that the result depends on the nature of the location.

    The plaintiffs sought to photograph only matters exposed to public view from exterior/outdoor areas they alleged were public streets/sidewalks. The court did not create a right to photograph unrestrictedly throughout government interiors.

    Official Ninth Circuit opinion: https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2018/08/14/16-55719.pdf

    Project consequence¶

    When the conditional order is:

    stop recording or leave

    identify separately:

    • the exact area/forum;
    • the subject/activity being recorded;
    • the actual restriction;
    • the government's justification;
    • the restriction's fit/reasonableness;
    • viewpoint/content neutrality;
    • any independent privacy/security rule; and
    • the separate removal/trespass statute.

    8. Close local comparator: Wilson v. County of Contra Costa¶

    Wilson v. County of Contra Costa, No. 14-cv-03491-SI (N.D. Cal. May 6, 2015)¶

    Status: federal district court; persuasive only; pleading-stage ruling.

    Wilson alleged that he recorded officers performing duties in the public lobby of the Contra Costa County Sheriff's Office and that an officer arrested him after noticing the recording.

    The court held that, taking the allegations as true at the motion-to-dismiss stage, he stated a First Amendment retaliation claim against the arresting officer based on recording police officers in the public lobby.

    The same opinion separately analyzed § 602.1(b) allegations.

    Source: https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/district-courts/california/candce/3%3A2014cv03491/279647/103/

    Project use¶

    Wilson is a useful local fact-pattern comparator, not controlling merits precedent.

    It reinforces why the chronology must be preserved:

    recording observed → adverse action → claimed trespass/removal basis

    9. Retaliation is a separate question from restriction validity¶

    Existing project authority includes O'Brien v. Welty, 818 F.3d 920 (9th Cir. 2016).

    A government action that could be lawful for a proper reason may still present a retaliation question if substantially motivated by protected expression, subject to the applicable retaliation doctrine and causation/defense rules.

    Official Ninth Circuit opinion: https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2016/04/07/13-16279.pdf

    For arrest-specific retaliation, the separate Nieves/Gonzalez probable-cause framework remains in the retaliation chapter.

    Project consequence¶

    Do not collapse these propositions:

    • government could lawfully restrict this activity under X rule;
    • government actually restricted this person for that lawful reason; and
    • the later arrest/exclusion was retaliatory.

    They require separate proof.

    10. The California five-step derivative-trespass test¶

    When a person begins lawfully present in a government area and later receives an activity-based leave order, run these questions in sequence.

    Step 1 — What exactly triggered the order?¶

    Preserve chronology:

    • activity before employee contact;
    • first employee demand;
    • whether the demand targeted recording/speech/observation;
    • whether a conditional phrase was used, e.g. stop recording or leave;
    • any independent misconduct identified before the order;
    • later-changing facts.

    Do not infer motive beyond the evidence.

    Step 2 — Was the underlying activity restriction valid?¶

    Apply:

    • exact forum/access classification;
    • activity-specific recording/speech law;
    • content/viewpoint analysis;
    • reasonableness/tailoring standard;
    • privacy/security/operational evidence;
    • California Speech Clause where independently applicable;
    • delegated authority/local rule if material.

    A public-facing interior can be nonpublic. That does not make every restriction valid.

    Step 3 — What legal authority supports exclusion/removal?¶

    Identify the actual authority:

    • § 602.1(b);
    • a specific § 602 subdivision;
    • another statute/regulation/order;
    • a lawful facility access rule;
    • local delegated authority;
    • specialized security/court/school law.

    Do not substitute the employee's word trespass for this step.

    Step 4 — Did the alleged offense's independent elements arise?¶

    For § 602.1(b), ask specifically:

    • intentional interference?
    • obstruction or intimidation?
    • qualifying request to leave?
    • refusal?
    • constitutional-activity exception?

    Under Dubner's published common structure and the nonprecedential Mackey public-agency comparator, refusal does not retroactively create obstruction/intimidation.

    For other statutes, use their own elements.

    Step 5 — Did later facts independently change the result?¶

    Examples:

    • building closed;
    • person moved into restricted area;
    • person actually blocked service;
    • unreasonable noise arose;
    • lawful security rule was violated;
    • person reentered after a separately valid exclusion;
    • other offense elements arose.

    A later independent offense should not be erased merely because the encounter began with protected activity.

    Likewise, a later label should not erase the legality question surrounding the earlier exclusion.

    11. Field Guide rule for "stop recording or leave"¶

    For the standing peaceful scenario:

    Treat "stop recording or leave" as two government acts: a recording restriction and a conditional exclusion. Test the recording restriction first, then identify the legal authority and elements supporting exclusion. A later refusal to leave does not by itself prove the restriction was lawful or supply missing § 602.1 obstruction/intimidation elements.

    The converse is equally important:

    If the restriction is independently lawful under the applicable forum/activity law, government need not always wait for actual disruption before conditioning continued access on compliance.

    This is the GAP-02 balance.

    12. Arrest / probable-cause posture¶

    An officer arriving after a call should preserve:

    • exact alleged conduct;
    • complainant's factual basis;
    • exact location/access status;
    • what restriction was allegedly violated;
    • who issued the leave order and with what authority;
    • whether the relevant offense requires obstruction/intimidation or another element;
    • what the officer personally observes;
    • available contrary/exculpatory evidence; and
    • later-changing facts.

    The phrase:

    the agency wants him trespassed

    is not itself an offense element.

    Published Dubner and nonprecedential Mackey show why the actual § 602.1 predicates matter.

    13. Qualification disposition¶

    After Sweep 012, the California overlay can run the ordinary conditional-access/derivative-trespass branch without assuming either that every exclusion is valid or that every peaceful person has an unlimited right to remain.

    Recommended dispositions:

    Row Pre-sweep Sweep 012 result Reason
    CA-F3.4 Gap Supported Policy/order is now separated from constitutional validity, exclusion authority, and criminal elements; valid prospective restrictions are also accounted for.
    CA-F4.1 Gap Supported California/federal authority now supplies the ordinary test for when government may restrict/revoke access based on a valid facility/activity rule, with local/facility authority preserved as a dependency.
    CA-F4.6 Gap Supported Brown/Hoffman, neutral closure doctrine, forum rules, and element-level trespass analysis distinguish invalid derivative exclusion from later independent changed facts.
    NC-COMPLAINT-01.M Gap Supported The complete upstream-restriction → exclusion → offense-elements → later-facts chain is now runnable in California.
    NC-VANTAGE-01.G Gap Supported Stop/move/leave demands can now be tested against property/forum/activity authority rather than treated as self-validating.

    Supported does not mean every facility exclusion has the same answer. It means the ordinary branch has sufficient authority and a defensible field test. Facility-specific rules, local delegated authority, specialized premises, prospective long-term bans, and identification duties remain separate packages where already mapped.

    14. Boundaries preserved for other packages¶

    Sweep 012 does not close:

    • GAP-03 delegated authority/preemption;
    • GAP-04 prospective no-entry notices, long-term bans, public-agency § 602(o)/§ 602.1 edge cases, and identification duties;
    • GAP-05 device seizure/search edges;
    • GAP-06 disorder/noise/fighting-words/obstruction doctrine;
    • GAP-07 emergency/dispersal/perimeter cases;
    • GAP-08 law-enforcement records.

    If a removal depends on one of those unresolved propositions, apply that open package rather than pretending Sweep 012 supplied it.
