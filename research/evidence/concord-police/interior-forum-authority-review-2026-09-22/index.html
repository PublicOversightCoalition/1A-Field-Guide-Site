    Concord Police Headquarters — Interior Forum / Recording Authority Review¶

    Reviewed: 2026-09-22
    Question: What forum framework best fits the ordinary public-facing Concord Police lobby, and what does that classification establish about recording?

    Current bottom line¶

    No published Ninth Circuit or California appellate decision specifically adjudicating an ordinary patron's right to film inside a California police-station lobby was located in this review.

    The current federal record nevertheless strongly supports treating the ordinary police service lobby / front-counter channel as a purpose-limited governmental interior rather than a traditional or designated public forum.

    Working federal posture:

    FEDERAL LIMITED/NONPUBLIC-FORUM STANDARD STRONGLY SUPPORTED / NO PUBLISHED NINTH-CIRCUIT POLICE-LOBBY FILMING HOLDING LOCATED

    For the practical restriction standard relevant here, both a limited public forum and a nonpublic forum generally permit restrictions that are reasonable in light of the forum's purpose and viewpoint neutral. The project should not overstate the exact label where the controlling authority does not require that distinction to answer the specific restriction question.

    But forum classification is only one layer. A newly located Northern District of California case involving recording officers in the public lobby of the Contra Costa County Sheriff's Office materially strengthens the activity-specific recording side of the analysis. Wilson v. County of Contra Costa, No. 14-cv-03491-SI (N.D. Cal. May 6, 2015), allowed a First Amendment retaliation claim based on recording officers in that public sheriff's lobby to proceed at the pleading stage and declined to grant qualified immunity on the facts alleged.

    California Constitution article I, § 2(a) remains separately unresolved for this particular interior.

    Controlling federal / Ninth Circuit framework¶

    Cornelius / Perry¶

    The forum must be defined by the access sought. Government ownership and public entry for official business do not by themselves create a traditional or designated public forum. Government may reserve property for its intended use, subject to the constitutional rules applicable to the forum it has actually created.

    Sammartano v. First Judicial District Court, 303 F.3d 959 (9th Cir. 2002)¶

    Sammartano remains the strongest published Ninth Circuit government-building comparator located for the threshold classification issue.

    The Ninth Circuit treated the judicial/municipal complex there as a nonpublic forum even though members of the public entered to conduct public business. The decision reinforces that:

    • public access for governmental transactions is not itself forum creation;
    • purpose, location, governmental intent, and actual use matter;
    • restrictions in a nonpublic forum must remain reasonable in light of the forum purpose and viewpoint neutral;
    • asserted safety/order interests still require a real relationship to the restriction rather than functioning as a categorical constitutional exemption.

    A police headquarters has materially stronger privacy, witness/victim, and operational-security concerns than the ordinary administrative parts of the Sammartano complex, while also having a clearly opened front-counter service function. Those differences belong in the reasonableness/application analysis rather than being used to skip forum analysis.

    Askins v. U.S. Department of Homeland Security, 899 F.3d 1035 (9th Cir. 2018)¶

    Askins recognizes First Amendment protection for photography/recording of matters of public interest and emphasizes that the legality of a restriction depends on the nature of the location and the actual governmental rule/justification.

    Its actual dispute concerned public/outdoor areas around ports of entry. It does not establish a right to record in every government interior.

    Serrano v. Sanders, No. 23-35437 (9th Cir. May 16, 2024)¶

    The Ninth Circuit stated that the clearly established right to record law-enforcement officers performing official duties in public places necessarily includes peaceful observation from a non-obstructive position.

    Important limit: Serrano is an unpublished memorandum disposition and is not precedent except as allowed by Ninth Circuit Rule 36-3. It involved a public scene rather than a police lobby.

    Closest local activity comparator — Wilson v. County of Contra Costa¶

    Wilson v. County of Contra Costa, No. 14-cv-03491-SI (N.D. Cal. May 6, 2015)¶

    Wilson is unusually important because it arose in the same county and involved the public lobby of the Contra Costa County Sheriff's Office.

    The amended complaint alleged that the plaintiff entered the 24-hour sheriff's-office lobby for police/public-service purposes, used an audio recorder that was in plain view, recorded officers performing their duties in the public lobby, and was seized/arrested when an officer noticed the recording.

    At the Rule 12(b)(6) stage, Judge Susan Illston held that the plaintiff had stated a First Amendment retaliation claim against the officer. The court relied on Fordyce's First Amendment right to film matters of public interest and treated the allegation that officers were being recorded while performing duties in the public lobby as sufficient protected-activity pleading for the retaliation claim.

    The court also declined to grant qualified immunity on the pleaded facts.

    Penal Code § 632 in Wilson¶

    Defendants argued that the audio recording was unlawful because it captured a confidential communication without consent. The court rejected that theory at the pleading stage because the amended complaint alleged that various members of the public were present in the area and could hear the conversations. On those allegations, defendants had not established an objectively reasonable expectation that the conversation was not being overheard or recorded.

    This provides a highly useful California-specific caution:

    police lobby + audio recording ≠ automatically confidential communication under §632

    but it does not establish the opposite categorical rule. A separated interview, victim report, acoustically isolated counter, or conversation surrounded by privacy measures may present materially different facts.

    Penal Code § 602.1(b) in Wilson¶

    The court also examined § 602.1(b). It did not hold that a public sheriff's lobby can never support a trespass/interference arrest. Instead, on the pleaded facts, the plaintiff alleged that officers physically prevented him from leaving and that he had difficulty standing/walking, so the court found the asserted probable-cause theory insufficient at that stage. The court expressly recognized that later evidence could potentially establish intentional interference and refusal.

    Procedural limitation¶

    Wilson is not a binding or final merits precedent:

    • it is a federal district-court ruling;
    • the key decision was at the motion-to-dismiss stage;
    • it did not conduct a formal public-forum classification of the sheriff's lobby;
    • it did not adjudicate a neutral, preexisting lobby-wide anti-recording rule;
    • the case later ended after an order to show cause concerning failure to prosecute, rather than a final trial/summary-judgment merits decision on the surviving recording claim.

    Approved use:

    HIGHLY-RELEVANT-LOCAL-DISTRICT-COURT-COMPARATOR / PLEADING-STAGE / NONBINDING / NO-FINAL-MERITS-ADJUDICATION

    Methodological consequence of Wilson¶

    Wilson demonstrates why the project should not demand that one case answer both:

    1. what forum is this? and
    2. is this particular recording activity protected/restrictable?

    For police/sheriff lobbies, a two-anchor method is stronger:

    • forum/access anchor — Cornelius, Sammartano, and facility-purpose/access evidence;
    • activity anchor — Fordyce, Penal Code § 148(g), agency recording policy, and close local cases such as Wilson.

    California statutory bridge — Penal Code § 148(g)¶

    Penal Code § 148(g) provides that photographing or audio/video recording a public or peace officer, while the officer is in a public place or the recorder is in a place the recorder has the right to be, does not by itself constitute resisting/delaying/obstructing under § 148(a), and by itself does not create reasonable suspicion or probable cause.

    For Sweep 010 this is significant because the statute is not written solely around traditional public forums. The alternative recorder is in a place ... [they have] the right to be makes lawful presence a central factual/legal question even in an interior.

    But § 148(g) does not itself create a right of access, convert a lobby into a public forum, or invalidate every otherwise lawful recording restriction.

    Other police-station-specific persuasive authorities¶

    These authorities are useful because they confront police facilities more directly, but they do not control the Ninth Circuit/California result.

    People v. DeRossett, 237 Ill. App. 3d 315 (1992)¶

    An Illinois appellate court treated an Urbana police-station lobby as a nonpublic forum. The defendant's conduct, however, involved an extended disruptive complaint and refusal to leave after being given a complaint form and other options. The case supports purpose-limited treatment of a police lobby but is a poor analogue for quiet, noninterfering recording standing alone.

    Use:

    POLICE-LOBBY FORUM/PURPOSE COMPARATOR / OUT-OF-STATE / DISRUPTIVE-CONDUCT FACTS

    First Defense Legal Aid v. City of Chicago, 319 F.3d 967 (7th Cir. 2003)¶

    The Seventh Circuit stated that the interior of a police station is not a public forum while rejecting a claimed First Amendment access right for attorneys seeking witnesses/suspects inside police facilities.

    Limit: the actual dispute involved access to witnesses/suspects and interrogation/internal station areas, not an ordinary visitor filming officers from a front public lobby.

    Use:

    GENERAL POLICE-INTERIOR PURPOSE/ACCESS CONTEXT — PERSUASIVE / NOT A LOBBY-FILMING HOLDING

    Hoffman v. Delgado, No. 23-13213 (11th Cir. Jan. 3, 2025)¶

    In an unpublished Eleventh Circuit decision involving a police-department lobby and a city anti-recording ordinance, the court analyzed the lobby under the limited-public-forum standard because the parties proceeded on that characterization. The panel expressly observed that there were good arguments the lobby was actually a nonpublic forum.

    The court upheld the challenged recording restriction as reasonable and viewpoint neutral on the pleaded facts, describing police lobbies as places generally used by people with legitimate public business to address public-safety needs rather than as places opened for discussion of all topics.

    Limits:

    • unpublished/nonprecedential in the Eleventh Circuit;
    • not controlling here;
    • involved an actual anti-recording ordinance that Concord has not been shown to have;
    • therefore useful for police-lobby purpose/reasonableness comparison, not a direct answer to Concord Policy 423.

    Brandt v. Crone, No. 21-1093 (10th Cir. Mar. 28, 2022)¶

    The Tenth Circuit confronted speech curtailed in a sheriff's-department lobby and held that the sheriff was entitled to qualified immunity because no Tenth Circuit/Supreme Court case or clear weight of authority had resolved the unique character of a sheriff's lobby and the First Amendment limits on responding to perceived disruption there.

    Use:

    SHERIFF-LOBBY UNCERTAINTY/QUALIFIED-IMMUNITY COMPARATOR / TENTH-CIRCUIT ONLY

    It does not answer the California/Ninth Circuit question and predates the current Concord-specific combination of § 148(g) and Policy 423.

    Reyes v. City of New York litigation¶

    The NYPD litigation is useful but must be separated into its components.

    The federal district court initially found the plaintiff unlikely to succeed on his First Amendment theory at the preliminary-injunction stage but likely to succeed under New York state/city right-to-record statutes.

    In 2025, the Second Circuit reserved decision and certified the New York statutory question rather than deciding the federal First Amendment merits.

    In June 2026, the New York Court of Appeals held that New York's state and city right-to-record statutes do not extend into publicly accessible police-station lobbies. Its reasoning emphasized victim, witness, confidential-informant, undercover-officer, investigative, and safety concerns.

    In August 2026, after receiving that answer, the Second Circuit vacated the preliminary injunction that had rested on the statutory theory and remanded. It expressly stated that it was expressing no view on the still-pending constitutional claim.

    Limits:

    • the New York Court of Appeals holding construes New York statutes, not the First Amendment and not California law;
    • the Second Circuit's August 2026 disposition does not resolve the First Amendment merits;
    • the privacy/security considerations are useful factual reasoning, not controlling California doctrine;
    • Concord's Policy 423 and California Penal Code § 148(g) have materially different text and must be analyzed independently.

    Massimino v. Benoit, No. 25-1104 (2d Cir. Aug. 17, 2026)¶

    The Second Circuit held that the First Amendment protects recording the publicly visible exterior of a police station from a public sidewalk. The court expressly distinguished that lawful exterior vantage from a claimed right of physical access inside the station.

    The opinion is therefore especially useful for the exterior/interior boundary:

    public sidewalk + publicly visible police-station exterior ≠ right to enter and record inside police station

    It also cautions that even exterior recording can remain subject to properly tailored restrictions tied to concrete privacy or safety interests.

    The Concord-specific wrinkle — Policy 423¶

    Concord is not presently an ordinary anti-recording-policy case.

    CPD Policy 423 affirmatively states that the Department recognizes lawful public recording of officers performing official duties and directs members not to prohibit or intentionally interfere with such lawful recording. It permits recording from a public place or private property where the individual has the legal right to be present, subject to interference/safety limits.

    Therefore the analysis cannot jump from:

    police lobby is limited/nonpublic

    to:

    recording is prohibited.

    Instead use:

    defined lobby subarea + lawful presence + officer/public activity being recorded + Policy 423 + Penal Code § 148(g) + Wilson + any actual narrower lobby rule + privacy/security facts + forum restriction standard

    A nonpublic-forum classification expands the government's regulatory authority; it does not manufacture a restriction that the agency has not adopted.

    Visitor/victim/witness privacy¶

    Police-station-specific cases identify legitimate concerns that are substantially more acute than at many administrative buildings. Those interests include protecting:

    • victims reporting sensitive crimes;
    • witnesses and confidential informants;
    • undercover personnel;
    • juveniles;
    • confidential records and computer displays;
    • investigative/tactical information;
    • confidential or sensitive conversations.

    Those interests can matter to whether a particular restriction is reasonable. They do not establish that every image or recording made while lawfully present in the lobby is categorically prohibited.

    The project should preserve the subject/activity distinction:

    recording an officer performing a public-facing official interaction ≠ deliberately capturing a victim's private report ≠ filming protected records/screens ≠ filming a secure operational area

    Wilson adds a useful local audio-privacy comparator: where conversations in a sheriff's lobby were alleged to be audible to various members of the public, the court did not accept § 632 confidentiality as a basis for dismissing the recording claim. That result remains fact- and posture-specific.

    California Speech Clause¶

    No sufficiently close published California appellate authority was located that classifies an ordinary police-station public lobby under California Constitution article I, § 2(a).

    Current state-law posture:

    CALIFORNIA SPEECH-CLAUSE CLASSIFICATION UNRESOLVED / NO CLOSE PUBLISHED CALIFORNIA POLICE-LOBBY CASE LOCATED

    Do not copy the federal limited/nonpublic result automatically into the California column.
