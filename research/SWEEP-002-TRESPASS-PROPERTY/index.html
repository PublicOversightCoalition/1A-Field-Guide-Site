    Sweep 002 — California Trespass and Property-Access Family¶

    Date reviewed: September 17, 2026
    Status: Primary-text sweep complete for the defined slice; first controlling-case pass completed for selected core propositions.

    Scope¶

    This sweep reviewed the California Penal Code trespass/property-access family from § 601 through § 603, with complete accounting for:

    • every subdivision of current Penal Code § 602, subdivisions (a) through (y);
    • §§ 602.1 through 602.13;
    • immediately surrounding §§ 601 and 603.

    This is a slice-level completeness claim only. It is not a completed sweep of all Penal Code Title 14, school/campus statutes in §§ 626 et seq., local ordinances, agency regulations, or all controlling case law.

    Key primary-text discoveries¶

    1. Within the defined §§ 601-603 slice, there is no generic asked to leave = trespass provision for an ordinary public-agency interior open to the public. Potentially relevant provisions contain additional predicates, and other laws/regulations/local rules may still independently apply.
    2. § 602(o) says not open to the general public. This matters when refusal-to-leave language is invoked while the person is in an area currently open to everyone.
    3. § 602(q) expressly addresses public buildings but only when regularly closed to the public. Public hours are therefore a potentially dispositive statutory fact for that subsection.
    4. § 602(m) says entering and occupying. The case-law review confirms that occupying is a substantial additional element rather than surplusage.
    5. § 602(y) is a screening-evasion provision. It concerns screening/inspection of the person and accessible property and should not be conflated with ordinary filming after lawful entry through security.
    6. Visitor sign-in, badge, appointment, purpose-of-visit, and ID requirements are separate access-policy questions. § 602(y) does not itself convert refusal to sign a visitor log or produce identification into criminal trespass.
    7. § 602.1(c) is auditor-relevant and was missing from the earlier chapter. It specifically requires a knowing material misrepresentation of law to people there to transact business plus refusal after the qualifying request.
    8. § 602.6 contains a specialized identification/account-for-presence element in a narrow closed-fair setting. Its existence reinforces the need to identify a specific California legal basis before treating every public-building encounter as carrying a universal identification requirement.
    9. § 602.11 expressly says physically does not include speech. This is a useful facility/protest distinction for later chapters.
    10. Public access and forum classification are separate. A specific area may be open to members of the public for agency business without being a traditional or designated public forum for general expressive activity.

    Controlling-case review findings¶

    § 602(m) — occupation is nontransient and continuous¶

    • In re Catalano, 29 Cal.3d 1 (1981) — binding California Supreme Court authority. The court states that the entering-and-occupying subdivision requires occupation of the property, a nontransient, continuous type of possession, citing People v. Wilkinson. The subdivision was later renumbered to current § 602(m) without substantive change to this language.
    • Edgerly v. City & County of San Francisco, 599 F.3d 946 (9th Cir. 2010) — published/binding Ninth Circuit authority for federal probable-cause analysis. A matter of minutes on the property did not supply probable cause for the entering-and-occupying subdivision. The court nevertheless found probable cause under § 602.8(a), illustrating why each potentially applicable subsection must be checked independently.
    • CALCRIM No. 2931 — the Judicial Council instruction requires proof that the defendant occupied some part of the property continuously until removed.

    Narrow doctrine-reviewed proposition: transient or momentary presence does not satisfy § 602(m)'s occupation requirement.

    § 602.1 — refusal and intentional interference are distinct¶

    • Dubner v. City & County of San Francisco, 266 F.3d 959 (9th Cir. 2001) — published Ninth Circuit decision. The court treated § 602.1 as requiring intentional interference plus refusal to leave and rejected the contention that intent to interfere could be inferred merely from refusal. The photographer/legal observer's documented conduct did not supply facts of deliberate obstruction or intimidation.
    • The wording quoted in Dubner was the business-establishment version of § 602.1, not today's public-agency subdivision (b). The case is therefore used as a binding probable-cause guidepost on the common § 602.1 structure, not as a complete published construction of every element of current subdivision (b).
    • Mackey v. Meyer, 675 F. App'x 705 (9th Cir. 2017) applied Dubner specifically to § 602.1(b) at a DMV and found no probable cause on the video-supported facts, but it is unpublished and is not treated as binding precedent.

    § 602(o) — publication-status caution¶

    The 2026 Court of Appeal opinion People v. Hsiung discusses § 602(o)'s constitutional savings language, but that analysis appears in Part III, which the court expressly excluded from publication. The California Supreme Court later denied review and a depublication request, but that does not convert the excluded Part III analysis into published precedential authority. The Field Guide therefore does not rely on that discussion as controlling law.

    Case-law work still required¶

    Before remaining propositions are promoted to DOCTRINE-REVIEWED, research must address at least:

    • application of § 602(m) specifically to public/government interiors beyond its settled occupation requirement;
    • California appellate construction of § 602(o)'s not open to the general public language in public-property settings;
    • § 602.1(b) obstruction/intimidation cases involving public-agency interiors;
    • § 602.1(d)(2) constitutional-activity exception;
    • public-library and government-building forum doctrine;
    • area-specific forum classification rather than facility-wide labels;
    • visitor sign-in, badge, appointment, purpose-of-visit, and identification conditions in public-facing government offices;
    • exclusions based on facility policy;
    • reasonable suspicion/probable cause based on employee requests for removal;
    • retaliatory exclusion for protected recording/speech;
    • longer-term facility bans and due process;
    • local ordinances and agency regulations.
