    Sweep 014 — Public-Agency Exclusion, Prospective Trespass, and Identification¶

    Qualification targets:

    • CA-F4.3 — public-property/public-agency distinctions;
    • CA-F4.5 — never-entered / entered-and-left / currently-remains / prospective-no-entry notice;
    • CA-F4.7 — suspension/exclusion process where material;
    • CA-F5.8 — identification request versus legal duty;
    • NC-COMPLAINT-01.D — prospective trespass notice and identification.

    This sweep addresses a recurring public-facility sequence:

    lawful public access → allegation such as "disruptive" or "harassing" → immediate leave demand → prospective exclusion/no-return notice → later reentry → claimed trespass → demand for name or physical identification

    The sweep does not assume the allegation is true merely because an employee, caller, security worker, or officer uses a legal-sounding label. It also does not assume that public ownership creates an unlimited right to remain.

    The operational sequence is:

    exact area/access status → actual conduct and rule → immediate-removal authority → prospective-exclusion authority → scope/duration/process → later-reentry statute → officer knowledge/reliance → identification stage and source of duty

    Three important outcomes are preserved rather than forced into categorical rules:

    1. California's ordinary public-agency/current-access trespass branches can now be separated and analyzed.
    2. The First Amendment supplies binding limits on some broad prospective exclusions, but the effect of an individualized prior ban on Penal Code § 602(o) remains materially unsettled in the California authorities reviewed.
    3. California does not have a current general pedestrian carry-ID statute equivalent to Nevada's Hiibel statute, but the effect of refusing to identify during an otherwise lawful detention remains an unresolved California § 148 boundary after Vanegas and the older California cases.

    1. Current California trespass statutes do not create one generic "trespassed" status¶

    Primary source:

    https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=PEN&sectionNum=602.

    The current Penal Code uses different factual predicates for different kinds of trespass.

    For the ordinary public-facility problem, the most important distinctions are:

    • § 602(o): refusal/failure to leave property not open to the general public after the specified request;
    • § 602(q): refusal/failure to leave a public building of a public agency during hours when the building is regularly closed to the public, after the specified request and under the objective lawful-business condition;
    • § 602.1(b): intentional interference with public-agency business by obstruction or intimidation plus refusal after a qualifying request;
    • § 602(t): a specialized prospective reentry/refusal provision for private property after a qualifying prior conviction and notice;
    • specialized secured/posted/screened-area provisions that must be tested on their own facts.

    The phrase "you are trespassed" does not identify which of these statutes applies.

    Field rule¶

    A leave order, an administrative exclusion, and a criminal-trespass offense are separate legal objects. Identify each one.

    Sweep 012 already established the derivative-trespass sequence for an immediate "stop the activity or leave" demand. Sweep 014 adds the later prospective-exclusion and reentry branches.

    2. Public ownership and "open to the general public" are different questions¶

    Section 602(o) turns on whether the exact land, real property, or structure is not open to the general public. Ownership by a public entity does not answer that question.

    James v. City of Long Beach, 18 F. Supp. 2d 1078 (C.D. Cal. 1998)¶

    Status: federal district court; persuasive only.

    James involved a city-owned baseball facility during a game. Access required a ticket. The district court concluded that the facility was not open to the general public for the relevant § 602(o)-predecessor analysis because entry was restricted to ticket-holders.

    Source: https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/district-courts/FSupp2/18/1078/2449844/

    Project consequence:

    Public property can be access-restricted. Ask whether anyone may presently enter the exact area under ordinary conditions or whether entry requires permission, a ticket, authorization, screening, appointment, or another condition.

    The converse matters too. A public-facing lobby or ordinary public service floor that anyone may enter during business hours is not converted into § 602(o) property merely because the government owns it.

    Section 602(q) separately covers regularly closed public buildings. Section 602.1(b) separately addresses a public agency open to the public.

    This supplies the ordinary public-property/public-agency distinction required by CA-F4.3 without pretending every area of a public building has the same access status.

    3. "Disruptive," "harassing," and similar labels are allegations, not elements¶

    For the standing peaceful scenario, preserve the conduct underneath the characterization.

    Examples of factual questions:

    • What precisely was said or done before the first leave order?
    • Was service actually obstructed?
    • Was an employee or member of the public intimidated?
    • Was a doorway, queue, counter, aisle, or workstation blocked?
    • Was there unreasonable noise?
    • Was another person followed, threatened, crowded, or repeatedly targeted?
    • Was a valid facility rule actually violated?
    • Did the official identify conduct, or only apply a conclusion such as "harassing customers"?
    • Did the alleged disruption precede the exclusion, or did conflict arise only after the person was told to stop recording or leave?

    Penal Code § 602.1(b) requires intentional interference with public-agency business by obstruction or intimidation plus refusal after a qualifying request. Sweep 012's published Dubner anchor and nonprecedential Mackey DMV comparator remain controlling project guardrails against treating refusal or generalized labels as substitutes for missing interference facts.

    Current statutory source: https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=PEN&sectionNum=602.1

    Project rule:

    Translate the allegation into observable facts and the actual offense or policy elements before relying on it to support removal, exclusion, detention, or arrest.

    If the allegation is knowingly false, retaliatory, or contradicted by objective evidence, those issues remain separate retaliation, false-report, and police-investigation branches. Sweep 014 does not infer motive from the label alone.

    4. Immediate removal and prospective exclusion are not the same government act¶

    A same-day order to leave can be limited to ending a present violation or restoring facility operations. A prospective exclusion changes future access even after the immediate facts have ended.

    For every prospective exclusion, record:

    • issuing authority;
    • legal source and delegation;
    • exact property/areas covered;
    • start and end dates;
    • whether it is one branch, one room, one building, a campus, or systemwide;
    • conduct relied on;
    • evidence supporting that conduct;
    • whether future risk is independently supported;
    • permitted access for unrelated government business;
    • exceptions or appointment procedures;
    • written notice;
    • opportunity to respond;
    • appeal/review procedure;
    • effect of an appeal;
    • reentry consequences;
    • actual criminal statute claimed to apply later.

    Sweep 013 supplies the authority/delegation chain. Sweep 014 adds the constitutional breadth/duration and later-reentry analysis.

    5. Binding Ninth Circuit: Reza limits broad prospective exclusions from a government forum¶

    Reza v. Pearce, 806 F.3d 497 (9th Cir. 2015)¶

    Status: Ninth Circuit, published/binding federal authority in California.

    Reza arose from an Arizona Senate building, which the Ninth Circuit treated as a limited public forum for the relevant activity. Officials attributed disruption during a legislative hearing to Reza. Two days later, the Senate President barred him from the building. Reza later attempted to enter for a scheduled meeting with another senator and was arrested for trespass.

    The amended published opinion found material factual disputes about whether Reza actually disrupted the proceeding and whether there was a legitimate future-disruption basis. On the plaintiff-favorable summary-judgment facts, a complete building-wide and initially indefinite ban was unreasonable. The court emphasized the breadth of the exclusion, its impact on unrelated future hearings and access to elected representatives, the absence of sufficient evidence that the asserted safety risk was real, and the availability of narrower restrictions.

    Official Ninth Circuit source: https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2015/11/19/13-15154.pdf

    Narrow project rule from Reza¶

    A prior incident does not automatically justify a broad future exclusion from a government forum. Preserve whether the alleged misconduct actually occurred, what future risk the record supports, how broadly the ban reaches, how long it lasts, and what otherwise lawful government access it forecloses.

    This is not a categorical rule that government can never suspend someone after disruption. The constitutional test remains forum- and activity-specific, and valid safety/operational restrictions can be enforced.

    Officer-reliance distinction¶

    Reza separately granted qualified immunity to officers who later enforced the facially valid exclusion order under Arizona's criminal-trespass law.

    That result must not be imported as though Arizona's trespass statute were California law. Its project value is the analytical separation:

    • underlying exclusion validity;
    • facial order and issuing authority;
    • later officer knowledge;
    • the actual jurisdiction's reentry/trespass statute; and
    • qualified-immunity / reasonable-reliance questions.

    A potentially unconstitutional exclusion and an officer's later civil liability are not necessarily the same question.

    6. Persuasive prospective-exclusion authorities¶

    Walsh v. Enge, 154 F. Supp. 3d 1113 (D. Or. 2015)¶

    Status: District of Oregon; persuasive only.

    Walsh applied Ninth Circuit First Amendment doctrine to a Portland City Hall exclusion following disruption of a city council meeting. It treated lengthy prospective exclusion from future meetings and City Hall access as constitutionally problematic rather than assuming that one disruption creates indefinite future exclusion power.

    Project use: comparator for breadth, duration, future forum access, and alternatives. Do not treat it as binding California authority.

    Catron v. City of St. Petersburg, 658 F.3d 1260 (11th Cir. 2011)¶

    Status: Eleventh Circuit; persuasive only.

    Catron involved trespass warnings from public parks. The Eleventh Circuit recognized that the plaintiffs had sufficiently alleged a procedural-due-process problem where the warning scheme provided no way to contest the warning or its scope. The opinion did not create an unlimited right to occupy public property and arose outside the Ninth Circuit.

    Project use: due-process comparator for meaningful review of a government-imposed future access restriction.

    7. Procedural due process: identify the protected interest before prescribing procedure¶

    Mathews v. Eldridge, 424 U.S. 319 (1976)¶

    Status: U.S. Supreme Court.

    Procedural due process first requires a protected liberty or property interest. If government is depriving such an interest, the required procedure is context-sensitive and ordinarily considers:

    1. the private interest affected;
    2. the risk of erroneous deprivation and probable value of additional safeguards; and
    3. the government's interest and burdens of additional procedure.

    Source: https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/424/319

    Field Guide consequence¶

    Do not write:

    Every exclusion from every public building requires a formal hearing before it takes effect.

    Instead ask:

    • What liberty, property, speech, petition, service-access, or other protected interest is actually burdened?
    • Is the restriction immediate/emergency, one day, weeks, months, indefinite, or permanent?
    • Is it one room or every facility in a system?
    • How serious is the risk from allowing continued access during review?
    • What facts are disputed?
    • Was there notice of the conduct and rule?
    • Could the person give their side?
    • Is there prompt review or appeal?
    • Does the ban remain in force during appeal?
    • Can necessary government business still be conducted by appointment or another channel?

    Reza supplies binding First Amendment limits in the government-forum setting. Mathews supplies the general procedural framework. Catron and library cases remain persuasive comparators for the amount of process in particular public-access settings.

    This authority is sufficient to run CA-F4.7's ordinary process branch while preserving facility-specific interests and procedures.

    8. A prior no-return notice does not have one settled California § 602(o) effect¶

    This is the most important unresolved trespass boundary in Sweep 014.

    Current § 602(o) expressly applies to property not open to the general public. It also contains owner-to-police request provisions, including limited-duration standing requests in specified circumstances. Those request provisions concern authorization for police assistance; they do not expressly create a universal individualized no-return offense for an otherwise public-facing government building.

    By contrast, current § 602(t) expressly creates a prospective reentry/refusal offense for specified private property after a qualifying prior conviction and notice, with defined time limits tied to the conviction.

    Current source: https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=PEN&sectionNum=602.

    That textual contrast is important but does not itself resolve how § 602(o) applies after an individualized ban.

    9. Blankenhorn: important but not a clean prospective-public-property holding¶

    Blankenhorn v. City of Orange, 485 F.3d 463 (9th Cir. 2007)¶

    Status: Ninth Circuit, published.

    Blankenhorn involved a privately owned shopping center that was generally open to the public. The plaintiff had received a prior "Notice Forbidding Trespass."

    In the probable-cause analysis, the majority said the notice arguably rendered the shopping center not open to the public with respect to Blankenhorn. The opinion emphasized that the question was not whether Blankenhorn ultimately was guilty of trespass but whether officers reasonably could think probable cause existed.

    The case therefore matters, but it should not be rewritten as:

    A prior trespass notice conclusively converts every otherwise public-facing property into property "not open to the general public" under § 602(o).

    It involved private commercial property, a probable-cause/qualified-immunity posture, and conditional language.

    Judge Berzon's partial dissent read the predecessor of § 602(o) differently, reasoning that the statutory request requirement called for a contemporaneous request and that a prior individualized notice did not itself satisfy it.

    Source: https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/485/463/577982/

    The majority/dissent disagreement is one reason this project preserves the later-notice branch as unresolved rather than converting Blankenhorn into a categorical rule.

    10. California federal district courts expose the unresolved § 602(o) boundary¶

    No published California appellate decision located in this sweep squarely resolves whether an individualized prospective ban, by itself, makes a public-facing government area that is otherwise open to the general public "not open to the general public" for purposes of current § 602(o).

    Federal district decisions applying California law point in different directions.

    Han v. City of Los Angeles¶

    Status: C.D. Cal.; persuasive only.

    In pretrial treatment of an otherwise public-facing private marketplace, the court examined Blankenhorn's conditional "arguably" language and rejected using a prior ban notice to transform the statutory phrase "not open to the general public" into individualized status for that trial. The court emphasized that Blankenhorn focused on what reasonable officers could have believed rather than conclusively deciding the statutory elements.

    Source: https://docs.justia.com/cases/federal/district-courts/california/cacdce/2%3A2014cv08582/603656/95

    Garcia v. City of Santa Clara¶

    Status: N.D. Cal.; persuasive only; later Ninth Circuit disposition did not create a published California statutory construction resolving the general issue.

    The district-court litigation treated Blankenhorn as supporting the proposition that a specific person can, at least arguably, be excluded from property otherwise open to the public. Other federal district decisions have used similar reasoning.

    Source: https://docs.justia.com/cases/federal/district-courts/california/candce/3%3A2010cv02424/228199/163

    Qualification consequence¶

    The split is not resolved by choosing the interpretation the project prefers.

    Field rule:

    If later criminal enforcement depends on a prior individualized ban converting otherwise generally open property into § 602(o) property, flag the statutory effect as RESEARCHED-UNRESOLVED unless a more specific statute, ordinance, valid restricted-area rule, closing condition, or other controlling authority independently resolves the facts.

    The prior notice still matters as evidence of notice, access instructions, intent, and officer knowledge. It simply does not eliminate the need to identify the criminal statute that actually governs later reentry.

    11. Reentry posture: rerun the law at the time of return¶

    When a person comes back after an earlier exclusion, do not analyze only the earlier encounter.

    Record:

    • current date/time;
    • whether the exclusion has expired;
    • exact property/area covered;
    • whether public hours are open;
    • whether the person has legitimate government business;
    • whether an appeal/review changed the order;
    • whether there is an appointment or express authorization;
    • whether a specific restricted-area or specialized statute applies;
    • what current statute is claimed to prohibit entry/remain;
    • whether a fresh request to leave occurred;
    • whether the person leaves when currently requested;
    • what officers know about the order's source, scope, and validity.

    A valid prior administrative exclusion can have legal consequences. But the phrase "you were trespassed before" is not a substitute for the elements of the California offense being asserted now.

    12. Identification: request, name, physical ID, search, citation, and booking are separate¶

    The Field Guide must keep at least six different propositions separate:

    1. officer may ask for identification;
    2. person must verbally state a name;
    3. person must carry a physical ID document;
    4. person must produce a document they possess;
    5. officer may search the person or belongings for ID;
    6. arrest/citation/booking rules create specific identification consequences.

    The answer to one does not decide the others.

    13. No current general pedestrian carry-ID statute located¶

    The current California corpus and this targeted sweep have not identified a general statute requiring an ordinary pedestrian merely present in public to carry a government identification card.

    Hiibel v. Sixth Judicial District Court, 542 U.S. 177 (2004), upheld Nevada's own stop-and-identify statute during a lawful Terry stop. Hiibel did not enact such a statute for California.

    Kolender v. Lawson, 461 U.S. 352 (1983), invalidated California's former vague identification scheme. That historical decision does not invalidate every current specialized identification statute.

    Project rule:

    Do not cite Hiibel as though it creates a California stop-and-identify statute. Find the California source applicable to the actual encounter.

    14. Landeros: refusal cannot create its own reasonable suspicion¶

    United States v. Landeros, 913 F.3d 862 (9th Cir. 2019)¶

    Status: Ninth Circuit, published.

    Landeros held that where officers lacked reasonable suspicion that a passenger had committed an offense, the passenger's refusal to identify himself did not itself create reasonable suspicion and did not justify prolonging the traffic stop to investigate him.

    Official source: https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2019/01/11/17-10217.pdf

    Field rule:

    An officer may ask for identification during a consensual encounter. Refusal does not manufacture the reasonable suspicion needed to detain a person who was not otherwise lawfully detainable.

    Landeros does not resolve every California-law consequence of refusal during an otherwise valid, particularized Terry detention.

    15. Vanegas confirms that the lawful-detention identification boundary remains open¶

    Vanegas v. City of Pasadena, 46 F.4th 1159 (9th Cir. 2022)¶

    Status: Ninth Circuit, published.

    Vanegas involved an investigation of reported conduct outside a courthouse. The officers had an independent probable-cause basis under Penal Code § 415(2), so the panel did not need to decide whether refusal to identify during the investigation itself supplied probable cause under § 148(a)(1).

    On the § 148 theory, the panel held that no controlling authority or robust consensus made the officers' asserted mistake clearly unconstitutional and granted qualified immunity. The majority expressly left open whether the failure to identify actually supplied § 148 probable cause.

    Official source: https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2022/08/31/21-55478.pdf

    Judge Bumatay's separate concurrence emphasized that he could find no California decision categorically holding that failure to identify during a police investigation, without more, supplies § 148 probable cause. Other separate reasoning in Vanegas read the law more broadly.

    Project consequence:

    The California-law duty to affirmatively identify oneself during every otherwise lawful Terry detention is not treated as settled by Vanegas.

    This is a genuine RESEARCHED-UNRESOLVED boundary, not merely missing research.

    16. California § 148 cases narrow the slogans but do not erase the unresolved detention issue¶

    People v. Quiroga, 16 Cal.App.4th 961 (1993)¶

    Status: published California Court of Appeal.

    Quiroga held, among other things:

    • verbal argument and slow compliance are not automatically § 148 obstruction;
    • refusing to provide a name after arrest while being transported did not itself violate § 148 where booking had not begun and no police duty was shown to have been delayed;
    • in the narrow felony-booking context, withholding identity can obstruct the booking process;
    • misdemeanor/infraction identification contexts are governed by their own statutory procedures.

    Source: https://law.justia.com/cases/california/court-of-appeal/4th/16/961.html

    In re Chase C., 243 Cal.App.4th 107 (2015)¶

    Status: published California Court of Appeal.

    Chase C. applied the actual-obstruction requirement in a police-investigation setting and held on its facts that refusal to identify before booking did not constitute § 148 obstruction. It reinforces that noncooperation cannot simply be relabeled obstruction without an obstructed lawful duty.

    Source: https://law.justia.com/cases/california/court-of-appeal/2015/d067787.html

    These cases strongly reject a generic "silence equals § 148" rule. They do not squarely overrule the older identification-production cases discussed below.

    17. Older production cases must be read with their facts and later Garcia limitation¶

    People v. Loudermilk, 195 Cal.App.3d 996 (1987)¶

    Status: published California Court of Appeal.

    Loudermilk contains broad language that, as part of a lawful Terry investigation, police may require a suspect to produce proof of identification if the suspect has it.

    But the case involved materially specific facts: officers were investigating a reported shooting, the detained person matched the description, a weapon was implicated, a lawful weapons frisk occurred, and the suspect said he had no identification after a wallet was discovered.

    The opinion itself cautioned that it was not holding that a suspect may be detained and searched merely because the suspect refused to identify or produce proof.

    Source: https://law.justia.com/cases/california/court-of-appeal/3d/195/996.html

    People v. Long, 189 Cal.App.3d 77 (1987)¶

    Status: published California Court of Appeal.

    Long likewise upheld a direction to produce written identification on particular facts involving a lawful detention, suspected drug intoxication, apparent inconsistency between the person's denial of identification and a visible wallet, and later safety/evidence concerns when the wallet was produced.

    Source: https://law.justia.com/cases/california/court-of-appeal/3d/189/77.html

    Do not convert Long into a rule authorizing officers to reach into any Terry detainee's pocket for identification.

    People v. Garcia, B187453 (Cal. Ct. App. Dec. 14, 2006)¶

    Status: published California Court of Appeal.

    Garcia directly rejected a generalized "patdown search for identification." The officer had lawfully detained a bicyclist who said he had no identification and searched him solely to locate identification. The court held that Terry's protective-frisk doctrine cannot be turned into an evidence search for ID.

    Garcia expressly distinguished Long and Loudermilk as fact-specific.

    Source: https://law.justia.com/cases/california/court-of-appeal/2006/b187453.html

    Project rule:

    A lawful detention is not, by itself, a license to conduct a Terry patdown for identification. Search authority requires its own justification.

    The separate question whether a lawfully detained person can be ordered to state or produce identity in every investigative detention remains unresolved after Vanegas and the older cases.

    18. False identity is independently regulated¶

    Current Penal Code § 148.9 makes specified false/fictitious identification conduct a misdemeanor upon lawful detention or arrest when the statutory purpose/elements are satisfied.

    Primary source: https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=PEN&sectionNum=148.9

    The statute tells a detainee what not to do. Its text is not itself a universal affirmative carry-ID statute.

    Field rule:

    Silence/refusal and false identity are different legal acts. Do not infer permission to lie from uncertainty about an affirmative identification duty.

    19. Citation, traffic, arrest, and booking stages can create specific identification consequences¶

    Penal Code § 853.5 — infraction citation procedure¶

    Current § 853.5 supplies identification, signature, and fingerprint procedures after an infraction arrest in the citation/release process.

    Primary source: https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=PEN&sectionNum=853.5

    The statute is not a general pre-offense pedestrian stop-and-identify law.

    Vehicle Code § 40302 / People v. McKay, 27 Cal.4th 601 (2002)¶

    McKay concerned a bicyclist already stopped for a Vehicle Code violation. The California Supreme Court emphasized that he was not arrested merely because he failed to carry identification; the predicate was the underlying Vehicle Code offense, after which the citation/custody statute required satisfactory evidence of identity for release.

    Source: https://law.justia.com/cases/california/supreme-court/4th/27/601.html

    Felony booking¶

    Quiroga recognizes a narrow booking-stage § 148 consequence where withholding identity actually impedes felony booking.

    Project sequence:

    underlying offense/detention authority → citation/arrest/booking stage → stage-specific identification statute or duty → consequence of noncompliance

    Do not reverse that sequence into:

    no ID → therefore detention/arrest is justified.

    20. An exclusion notice does not itself create a new identification statute¶

    A public employee or officer may want a person's name to document an exclusion. That administrative convenience does not by itself answer whether California law compels the person to provide it.

    For an exclusion encounter, ask separately:

    • Is the contact consensual or a lawful detention?
    • What suspected offense or other legal basis supports detention?
    • Is the person being arrested or cited for an independently supported offense?
    • Does a citation/release or booking statute apply?
    • Does the facility's valid rule condition future voluntary access on an identification process?
    • If so, is the question administrative access or criminal compulsion?
    • Is the person merely silent/refusing, or affirmatively giving false information?
    • Is any physical search for identification separately lawful?

    Field Guide rule:

    "We need your ID so we can trespass you" is not itself the source of an identification duty. Identify the detention/arrest/citation/access authority that supplies the duty or consequence.

    A person may still face a practical administrative consequence if a valid facility rule conditions a future service or appeal process on enough information to identify the affected person. That is analytically distinct from arresting someone merely for refusing an unsupported ID demand.

    21. Public-agency exclusion / reentry decision tree¶

    When government says a person is "trespassed" from public property, run this sequence.

    Step 1 — Current access status¶

    Is the exact area:

    • presently open to the general public;
    • closed for the day;
    • ticketed/appointment-only;
    • restricted to authorized people;
    • subject to valid screening;
    • temporarily closed for a lawful use; or
    • governed by a specialized statute/regulation?

    Step 2 — Conduct before the restriction¶

    Record actual facts before labels.

    For "disruptive" or "harassing":

    • identify the conduct;
    • identify the rule/offense;
    • test its elements;
    • preserve contrary objective evidence.

    Step 3 — Immediate order¶

    Who ordered departure, under what authority, and was the underlying rule valid?

    Use Sweeps 012 and 013.

    Step 4 — Prospective exclusion¶

    If the order extends beyond the current encounter:

    • identify issuing authority/delegation;
    • exact scope;
    • duration;
    • evidence/future-risk rationale;
    • viewpoint neutrality;
    • impact on unrelated government access;
    • notice and review procedure.

    Use Reza and, where procedural due process is implicated, Mathews.

    Step 5 — Later return¶

    Do not assume the old order itself equals a current criminal offense.

    Identify:

    • current access facts;
    • order still in force?;
    • exact California offense claimed;
    • whether a fresh request occurred;
    • whether a specialized no-entry law applies;
    • whether § 602(o)'s "not open to the general public" predicate is actually satisfied.

    If the criminal theory depends only on an individualized prior ban changing otherwise generally open property into § 602(o) property, preserve the unresolved Blankenhorn/Han/Garcia boundary.

    Step 6 — Police reliance¶

    Separate:

    • legality of the underlying exclusion;
    • what the officer reasonably knew;
    • facial validity/source/scope of the order;
    • independent offense elements;
    • qualified-immunity issues.

    Reza illustrates why these can produce different answers.

    Step 7 — Identification¶

    Separate:

    • request;
    • lawful detention;
    • verbal name;
    • physical document;
    • search for ID;
    • false identity;
    • citation/release;
    • booking.

    Do not invent a general ID duty from the exclusion notice itself.

    22. Field rule for recurring "you're being trespassed" encounters¶

    For the standing peaceful scenario:

    A public employee or officer saying "you're trespassed" does not identify a California crime. Determine whether the present area is open to the general public, what conduct preceded the order, what authority supports immediate removal, whether a separate prospective exclusion was validly issued, and what statute applies if the person later returns.

    For allegation-driven exclusions:

    "Disruptive," "harassing," and "interfering" are factual conclusions to test, not magic words that satisfy statutory or constitutional predicates.

    For prospective bans:

    A prior incident can support a future restriction when the actual facts and governing law justify it, but breadth, duration, unrelated future access, viewpoint neutrality, and evidence of future risk matter. Reza does not permit a broad ban to bootstrap itself from an unsupported disruption allegation.

    For identification:

    California has specialized identification duties and consequences, but this sweep does not identify a general pedestrian carry-ID statute. The legal effect of refusing to identify during every otherwise lawful Terry detention remains unresolved; detention alone also does not authorize a patdown search for ID.
