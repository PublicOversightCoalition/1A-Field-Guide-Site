    Sweep 018 — Law-Enforcement Records and Specialized Evidence Disclosure¶

    Qualification target:

    • CA-F9.4 — access to policy, incident, dispatch/body-camera/CCTV, peace-officer, and analogous evidence subject to applicable exemptions.

    This sweep completes the ordinary California post-incident records branch needed after a public-facility or police encounter.

    The operational sequence is:

    record/custodian → investigatory-record status → mandatory information carveout → critical-incident audio/video override → peace-officer-record override → privacy/safety/redaction → electronic format/cost → retention/preservation separately

    The central distinction is:

    A record may be a public record without being fully disclosable, and an investigatory-record exemption does not erase later statutes that affirmatively require disclosure of specified information, recordings, or officer records.

    This sweep uses the current recodified CPRA numbering. Older cases refer to former Government Code § 6254(f). Current Government Code § 7923.630 expressly preserves the pre-recodification construction of that law; the 2021 recodification was not intended to change its substance.

    1. Start with the custodian and the actual record¶

    After an encounter, potentially useful records can be held by different agencies:

    • incident/offense reports;
    • arrest/citation records;
    • 911 recordings;
    • CAD/dispatch event records;
    • radio traffic;
    • body-worn-camera video/audio;
    • dash-camera or other agency video;
    • police photographs;
    • government-facility CCTV;
    • use-of-force records;
    • officer complaint/investigation records;
    • exclusion/trespass notices;
    • facility emails or communications;
    • personal-device public-business recordings;
    • retention/deletion logs.

    A police department may possess a copy of a facility's CCTV because it was collected for an investigation while the original facility also retains its own copy for routine security/operations. Those copies can present different custody, purpose, retention, and exemption facts.

    Field rule¶

    Request the record from the agency reasonably likely to hold it, and identify duplicate custodians when appropriate. Do not assume that a police department, host agency, county, city, or facility necessarily holds every item.

    The ordinary CPRA public-record definition, personal-device doctrine, request procedure, and preservation framework remain in public-records-evidence-preservation.md / Sweep 003.

    2. Current law-enforcement investigatory-record exemption — Government Code § 7923.600¶

    Current primary source:

    https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=GOV&sectionNum=7923.600.

    Section 7923.600 preserves a broad CPRA exemption for specified:

    • complaint/investigation records;
    • intelligence and security-procedure records;
    • investigatory or security files compiled by state/local police; and
    • investigatory/security files compiled by another state/local agency for correctional, law-enforcement, or licensing purposes.

    This is the recodified successor to former § 6254(f).

    Government Code § 7923.630 — recodification continuity¶

    Section 7923.630 expressly records that this article was derived from former § 6254(f) and that dividing it into multiple sections was not intended to alter its construction or relationships.

    Current source:

    https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=GOV&sectionNum=7923.630.

    Project consequence:

    Pre-recodification California Supreme Court cases such as Williams and Haynie remain material to current §§ 7923.600–7923.630. Cite the current statute and identify the former number only when explaining the older opinion.

    3. Williams: the investigatory-file exemption is broad and does not expire merely because the case ends¶

    Williams v. Superior Court (Freedom Newspapers, Inc.), 5 Cal.4th 337 (1993)¶

    Status: California Supreme Court; controlling.

    Williams construed former § 6254(f), now the § 7923.600 article.

    The court rejected a case-by-case FOIA-style balancing test for ordinary law-enforcement investigatory files. The Legislature instead chose a broad record/file exemption while affirmatively requiring disclosure of specified information derived from those records.

    Important propositions:

    • qualifying law-enforcement investigatory files are broadly exempt absent a later specific disclosure statute;
    • the exemption does not automatically terminate when the investigation concludes;
    • otherwise nonexempt material can acquire investigatory-file status when compiled as part of a concrete and definite law-enforcement investigation; and
    • the mandatory-information provisions must still be honored.

    Source:

    https://law.justia.com/cases/california/supreme-court/4th/5/337.html

    Do not overread Williams¶

    Later legislation now affirmatively requires disclosure of some materials that would once have fallen within the broad investigatory exemption, especially:

    • critical-incident audio/video under Government Code § 7923.625; and
    • specified peace-officer records under Penal Code § 832.7(b).

    Those later statutes must be applied before declaring a record categorically unavailable.

    4. Haynie: actual investigations are exempt, but not everything police do is an investigation¶

    Haynie v. Superior Court, 26 Cal.4th 1061 (2001)¶

    Status: California Supreme Court; controlling.

    Haynie involved a police stop prompted by a report of possible armed suspects. The requester sought recordings/statements surrounding the stop.

    The Supreme Court held that records of an actual investigation undertaken to determine whether a violation of law may occur or has occurred fall within the law-enforcement investigatory exemption.

    But the court expressly cautioned that the exemption does not shield everything law enforcement officers do. Inquiries made for crime prevention/public safety that are unrelated to determining whether a civil or criminal violation may occur/has occurred are not automatically "records of investigations."

    Source:

    https://law.justia.com/cases/california/supreme-court/4th/26/1061.html

    Field rule¶

    Ask why the record was created. A targeted inquiry into suspected law violation can be an exempt investigation even if no arrest or charge results. Routine/public-safety data collection is not automatically investigatory merely because police created it.

    5. ACLU / Castañares: mass/routine collection and assistance footage are not automatically investigatory¶

    American Civil Liberties Union Foundation of Southern California v. Superior Court, 3 Cal.5th 1032 (2017)¶

    Status: California Supreme Court.

    The court rejected the proposition that bulk automated-license-plate-reader data was automatically a record of investigation merely because law enforcement collected it. The investigatory exemption requires a sufficiently targeted relationship to suspected law violations; the catchall exemption remained a separate question.

    Source:

    https://law.justia.com/cases/california/supreme-court/2017/s227106.html

    Castañares v. Superior Court, 98 Cal.App.5th 295 (2023)¶

    Status: published California Court of Appeal.

    Castañares applied the same distinction to police drone footage and described three useful categories:

    1. footage placed in an investigatory file — exempt under § 7923.600;
    2. footage created while investigating whether a law violation occurred, even without a formal file — ordinarily exempt under Haynie; and
    3. footage created to determine what kind of noncriminal public assistance is needed — not automatically investigatory.

    Source:

    https://law.justia.com/cases/california/court-of-appeal/2023/d082048.html

    CCTV / routine video consequence¶

    Routine facility CCTV is not automatically a law-enforcement investigatory record merely because police later become interested in it.

    If police obtain a copy for an actual investigation, the police-held copy can acquire investigatory-file status. The original facility-held copy must be analyzed based on that custodian's own purpose, exemptions, privacy/security interests, and retention rules.

    Do not promise that requesting the same file from another custodian necessarily defeats a valid exemption; analyze each agency and source separately.

    6. Mandatory arrest information — Government Code § 7923.610¶

    Current source:

    https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=GOV&sectionNum=7923.610.

    Section 7923.610 requires state/local law-enforcement agencies to make specified arrestee information public, subject to item-specific safety/investigation limitations.

    The required information includes categories such as:

    • name/occupation;
    • physical description and identifying data specified by statute;
    • time/date of arrest and booking;
    • location;
    • factual circumstances;
    • bail;
    • release/holding information; and
    • charges/warrants/parole/probation holds.

    Critical distinction¶

    The statute mandates specified information. It does not necessarily compel production of the entire underlying arrest report.

    An agency cannot answer "investigatory file" and ignore the mandatory-information provision. Conversely, the existence of mandatory arrest information does not erase all exemptions applicable to the report itself.

    7. Mandatory complaint/request/response information — § 7923.615¶

    Current source:

    https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=GOV&sectionNum=7923.615.

    Subject to statutory safety/investigation and victim protections, law enforcement must make public specified information regarding complaints or requests for assistance, including:

    • time, substance, and location of complaints/requests;
    • time and nature of the response;
    • recorded time/date/location of occurrence and report;
    • specified victim information;
    • factual circumstances; and
    • general descriptions of injury, property, or weapons.

    911 / CAD / dispatch consequence¶

    Section 7923.615 is extremely useful after a complaint-driven encounter because it requires specified information about the call/request and response.

    It does not automatically mean:

    "The agency must give me the complete unredacted 911 audio, CAD event, or radio recording."

    If the actual audio/CAD/radio record is part of an exempt investigation, Williams/Haynie can still govern the record while § 7923.615 requires disclosure of the enumerated information.

    Request both:

    1. the actual record; and
    2. in the alternative, all information required by § 7923.615 if the agency invokes § 7923.600.

    That prevents an agency from treating an investigatory-record exemption as though it eliminates the mandatory public-information floor.

    8. Victim/authorized-requester information — § 7923.605¶

    Current source:

    https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=GOV&sectionNum=7923.605.

    Section 7923.605 creates a separate disclosure branch for specified victims, authorized representatives, insurers, and persons suffering bodily injury/property damage/loss from listed incidents.

    The branch can include:

    • names/addresses;
    • property descriptions;
    • date/time/location;
    • diagrams;
    • party/witness statements;

    subject to statutory safety/investigation limitations.

    It does not require release of officer analysis/conclusions merely because a requester falls within the branch.

    Classification: specialized requester-status branch. Use when the requester actually qualifies.

    9. Critical-incident audio/video — Government Code § 7923.625¶

    Current official source:

    https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=GOV&sectionNum=7923.625.

    This is the main specialized CPRA disclosure statute for law-enforcement audio/video recordings that depict a defined critical incident.

    A critical incident is a recording that depicts:

    1. an incident involving discharge of a firearm at a person by a peace/custodial officer; or
    2. an incident in which officer force against a person resulted in death or great bodily injury.

    The statute applies to qualifying recordings regardless of whether the medium is a body-worn camera, dash camera, or another audio/video source within the provision.

    Active-investigation delay¶

    The statute does not allow indefinite use of the generic investigatory-file exemption.

    In simplified operational terms:

    • during the initial statutory period, disclosure can be delayed if release would substantially interfere with an active criminal/administrative investigation;
    • continued delay after that period requires the agency to continue demonstrating substantial interference;
    • after one year, continued delay requires the heightened clear-and-convincing-evidence showing;
    • the agency must periodically reassess/notify as the statute requires; and
    • once the basis for delay resolves, disclosure must occur promptly.

    The current text controls the exact notice/reassessment deadlines; do not rely on memory when drafting a demand or litigating a delay.

    Privacy redaction¶

    The statute permits privacy-protective blurring/distortion when the privacy interest outweighs disclosure, but the redaction cannot interfere with full, complete, and accurate comprehension and the agency may not otherwise edit the recording.

    If privacy cannot adequately be protected by redaction, the statute contains a narrower public-withholding branch plus access provisions for specified subjects/authorized representatives/heirs, subject to active-investigation delay.

    Not every BWC recording is a § 7923.625 recording¶

    Subdivision (f) expressly leaves other audio/video records to other applicable law.

    Therefore:

    BWC footage of an ordinary detention, trespass complaint, or non-GBI force encounter is not automatically subject to § 7923.625 merely because a body camera captured it.

    It may still be disclosable under another statute or CPRA rule; it can also fall within § 7923.600's investigatory exemption.

    10. Sacramento Television Stations — current interpretation of critical-incident recordings¶

    Sacramento Television Stations Inc. v. Superior Court, 112 Cal.App.5th 306 (2025)¶

    Status: published California Court of Appeal.

    The court addressed how much audio/video must be released when recordings relate to a critical incident.

    Important propositions:

    • § 7923.625 was enacted as a specific limitation on the older broad investigatory-record exemption for defined critical-incident recordings;
    • the statutory command can require disclosure sufficient to depict the relevant incident/context rather than permitting an agency to release only an artificially narrow fragment;
    • a claimed active-investigation delay still requires the evidentiary showing the statute specifies.

    Source:

    https://law.justia.com/cases/california/court-of-appeal/2025/c102316.html

    Project use:

    For critical-incident recordings, begin with § 7923.625 itself rather than assuming Williams/Haynie categorically controls the video.

    11. BWC retention — Penal Code § 832.18 is not the disclosure statute¶

    Current source:

    https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=PEN&sectionNum=832.18.

    Penal Code § 832.18 is primarily a body-worn-camera policy/best-practices and retention provision.

    Its retention framework includes:

    • nonevidentiary BWC data should ordinarily be retained for a minimum period identified by the statute before routine deletion/recycling;
    • evidentiary categories specified by the statute should be retained for a longer minimum period; and
    • criminal-prosecution or other legal circumstances can require additional retention.

    The current statute should be checked for the exact period/category before relying on a retention deadline.

    Field rule¶

    Do not cite § 832.18 as though it automatically makes BWC footage public. Disclosure comes from the CPRA and specific disclosure statutes; § 832.18 principally informs policy/retention.

    This is consistent with the California Supreme Court's current 2026 retention discussion in City of Gilroy v. Superior Court.

    Source:

    https://law.justia.com/cases/california/supreme-court/2026/s282937.html

    12. Peace-officer personnel/misconduct records — Penal Code § 832.7¶

    Current official source:

    https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=PEN&sectionNum=832.7.

    The current statute, including amendments effective January 1, 2026, begins from confidentiality for peace/custodial officer personnel records but creates explicit public-disclosure categories in subdivision (b).

    Critically, § 832.7(b)(1) applies notwithstanding the ordinary personnel confidentiality rule, Government Code § 7923.600, or other law for the enumerated categories.

    Current public categories include records relating to:

    Force/firearm¶

    • officer discharge of a firearm at a person;
    • officer force resulting in death or great bodily injury;
    • a sustained complaint finding of unreasonable/excessive force; and
    • a sustained finding that an officer failed to intervene against clearly unreasonable/excessive force.

    Sexual assault¶

    • sustained findings of statutorily defined sexual assault involving a member of the public.

    Dishonesty¶

    • sustained findings of dishonesty directly related to crime reporting/investigation/prosecution or officer-misconduct reporting/investigation, including false statements/reports, evidence destruction/falsification/concealment, and perjury.

    Prejudice/discrimination¶

    • sustained findings of specified prejudicial/discriminatory conduct based on protected characteristics.

    Unlawful arrest/search¶

    • sustained findings that an officer made an unlawful arrest or conducted an unlawful search.

    Prohibited agreements¶

    • the current statute also includes the specified prohibited agreement category tied to Penal Code § 13510.9.

    Broad incident record package¶

    For records within the public categories, the statute reaches far more than a one-page disciplinary outcome. Depending on the category, the disclosure package can include:

    • investigative reports;
    • photographs;
    • audio/video;
    • interview transcripts/recordings;
    • autopsy reports;
    • materials presented to prosecutors or disciplinary/charging bodies;
    • findings/recommended findings; and
    • records reflecting discipline/corrective action.

    The exact current statutory list controls.

    Sustained means sustained¶

    Penal Code § 832.8 defines "sustained" as a final determination after investigation and opportunity for applicable administrative appeal that the conduct violated law or policy.

    Current source:

    https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=PEN&sectionNum=832.8.

    Where § 832.7 requires a sustained finding, a mere complaint/allegation is not enough.

    The firearm-discharge and force-causing-death/GBI categories are different: their public status does not depend on a sustained misconduct finding.

    13. § 832.7 redaction and delay are structured, not a free-form secrecy license¶

    Current § 832.7 authorizes specified redactions/delays, including protection of:

    • personal data;
    • anonymity of whistleblowers, complainants, victims, and specified witnesses;
    • legally protected medical/financial/private information; and
    • specified safety interests.

    It also contains investigation-delay rules and current production timing rules for the public categories.

    The statute does not authorize an agency to black out everything merely because personnel information appears somewhere in the file.

    Field rule¶

    Ask the agency to identify the statutory paragraph supporting each withholding/redaction and release reasonably segregable material.

    For a live request, check the current 2026 text for the precise delay/timing branch because the Legislature has amended § 832.7 repeatedly.

    The current statute also provides that ordinary copy charges do not include agency search/edit/redaction labor for these records.

    14. Copley Press is historical context, not the final current rule¶

    Copley Press, Inc. v. Superior Court, 39 Cal.4th 1272 (2006)¶

    Status: California Supreme Court; controlling as to the then-existing confidentiality statute but materially altered by later legislation for enumerated categories.

    Copley broadly applied the former peace-officer personnel confidentiality scheme to disciplinary appeal records.

    Source:

    https://law.justia.com/cases/california/supreme-court/4th/39/1272.html

    The Legislature later enacted SB 1421/SB 16 and subsequent amendments creating current § 832.7(b)'s affirmative public categories.

    Do not write:

    "Copley says peace-officer discipline is confidential, so none of it is public."

    The correct current sequence is:

    Does § 832.7(b) place this record/incident in an affirmative public category? → If yes, apply the current disclosure/redaction/delay provisions → If no, analyze the remaining confidentiality/CPRA rules.

    15. Becerra / First Amendment Coalition — later § 832.7 is a genuine override¶

    Becerra v. Superior Court, 44 Cal.App.5th 897 (2020)¶

    Status: published California Court of Appeal.

    Becerra held that qualifying § 832.7(b) records do not become unavailable merely because the custodian agency holds records concerning officers employed by another agency. The public-record mandate focuses on responsive qualifying records in the state/local agency's possession.

    Source:

    https://law.justia.com/cases/california/court-of-appeal/2023/a165888.html

    (The 2023 published follow-on opinion summarizes and applies the 2020 Becerra holding; citation: 44 Cal.App.5th 897.)

    First Amendment Coalition v. Superior Court, 98 Cal.App.5th 884 (2023)¶

    Status: published California Court of Appeal.

    The court described § 832.7(b) as changing the former confidentiality rule. Its "notwithstanding" clause can override conflicting state-law confidentiality provisions; the opinion specifically addressed Government Code § 11183.

    It does not mean that every nonconflicting exemption or constitutional privacy interest disappears.

    Source:

    https://law.justia.com/cases/california/court-of-appeal/2023/a165888.html

    Project rule¶

    Treat § 832.7(b) as an affirmative statutory disclosure command with its own redaction/delay scheme—not as a suggestion layered beneath the older blanket confidentiality rule.

    16. Officer names — Long Beach¶

    Long Beach Police Officers Association v. City of Long Beach, 59 Cal.4th 59 (2014)¶

    Status: California Supreme Court.

    The court held that names of officers involved in on-duty shootings were generally disclosable on the record before it because the agencies failed to make a particularized showing sufficient to overcome the substantial public interest in officer identity.

    Source:

    https://law.justia.com/cases/california/supreme-court/2014/s200872.html

    Current Penal Code § 832.7(i) expressly states that the chapter does not limit public access provided by Long Beach.

    Field rule¶

    Do not assume officer names are automatically secret merely because the record concerns police activity. Safety/privacy can matter, but the agency must apply the actual governing rule rather than a categorical officer-name exemption.

    17. Redaction costs — National Lawyers Guild v. City of Hayward¶

    National Lawyers Guild v. City of Hayward, 9 Cal.5th 488 (2020)¶

    Status: California Supreme Court.

    The City attempted to charge a requester for staff time spent editing/redacting exempt material from police body-camera video.

    The Supreme Court held ordinary redaction work was not chargeable to the requester as "data extraction" under the electronic-record cost provision.

    Source:

    https://law.justia.com/cases/california/supreme-court/2020/s252445.html

    Current electronic-record/copy provisions:

    • § 7922.530 — copies/direct duplication;
    • § 7922.570 — electronic-form availability;
    • § 7922.575 — specified compilation/extraction/programming cost branch.

    Official sources:

    https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=GOV&sectionNum=7922.530

    https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=GOV&sectionNum=7922.570

    https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=GOV&sectionNum=7922.575

    Field rule¶

    Redaction is not automatically a requester-billable "extraction" cost merely because the record is electronic video.

    19. Post-encounter request workflow¶

    Step 1 — send parallel requests to likely custodians¶

    For a public-facility police response, consider separate requests to:

    • responding law-enforcement agency;
    • facility/department;
    • city/county/state host agency;
    • contracted security entity if legally subject to the request or records are held by the public agency.

    Step 2 — request the actual evidence¶

    Request categories narrowly enough to identify the incident:

    • incident/report number if known;
    • date/time/location;
    • involved officer/employee names if known;
    • 911;
    • CAD/event chronology;
    • dispatch/radio;
    • BWC/dash/video;
    • photographs;
    • CCTV;
    • exclusion notices;
    • complaints;
    • employee recordings;
    • relevant communications.

    Step 3 — invoke mandatory information separately¶

    If police claim § 7923.600:

    Please also provide all information required to be made public under Government Code §§ 7923.610 and 7923.615, as applicable.

    That avoids treating the record exemption as an information blackout.

    Step 4 — identify critical-incident video if applicable¶

    If the event depicts an officer firearm discharge at a person or force resulting in death/GBI:

    Please process responsive audio/video under Government Code § 7923.625 and identify any statutory basis and timeline for delayed disclosure/redaction.

    Step 5 — identify § 832.7 categories if applicable¶

    If records concern a qualifying officer incident or sustained finding, cite the specific current § 832.7(b)(1) category.

    Do not demand all officer personnel files.

    Step 6 — request segregable material and legal bases¶

    For every withholding/redaction:

    • ask for the current statutory basis;
    • request reasonably segregable nonexempt material;
    • request the required written denial;
    • preserve dates/timing.

    Step 7 — request retention schedules separately¶

    Disclosure and retention remain different.

    Request:

    • BWC policy;
    • CCTV retention schedule;
    • CAD/audio retention schedule;
    • deletion/destruction logs if the evidence is missing;
    • any preservation notice or litigation hold actually issued.

    20. Sample focused request language¶

    Pursuant to the California Public Records Act, I request records concerning the incident at [location] on [date] at approximately [time], including the incident/event report, 911 audio, CAD/dispatch event record, radio traffic, body-worn and dash-camera recordings, photographs, facility CCTV obtained or retained by your agency, and any exclusion/trespass notice relating to the incident.

    If you withhold an investigatory record under Government Code § 7923.600, please nevertheless disclose all information required by §§ 7923.610 and 7923.615 and identify the legal basis for each withholding or redaction.

    For any audio/video depicting a critical incident within Government Code § 7923.625(e), please process the recording under § 7923.625 and identify any claimed statutory delay, redaction, or withholding provision.

    For records subject to Penal Code § 832.7(b), please process them under the current public-disclosure, redaction, and delay provisions of that section.

    Please provide reasonably segregable nonexempt portions and responsive electronic records in the electronic form in which they are held when required by the CPRA.

    Adapt the request. Do not cite a specialized branch that plainly does not fit the incident.

    21. Common bad field rules¶

    Bad slogan Better rule
    "Police reports are exempt." Investigatory records can be broadly exempt, but §§7923.610/.615 mandate specified arrest/complaint/response information and later statutes can require records.
    "The investigation is closed, so the whole file becomes public." Williams says the ordinary investigatory-file exemption does not automatically expire when the investigation ends.
    "Everything police collect is an investigatory record." Haynie/ACLU/Castañares distinguish targeted law-violation investigations from routine/public-safety/assistance collection.
    "911 calls are automatically public." Request the audio, but preserve that §7923.615 mandates specified information and does not necessarily compel the underlying recording.
    "All bodycam is public after 45 days." §7923.625's special timeline applies only to defined critical-incident recordings. Other BWC footage follows other law.
    "BWC is exempt because it is evidence." Critical-incident §7923.625 and qualifying §832.7 records can override ordinary investigatory withholding.
    "§832.18 makes bodycam public." §832.18 is principally a BWC policy/retention statute; disclosure is a separate CPRA question.
    "Officer personnel records are confidential." Start with confidentiality, then apply current §832.7(b)'s affirmative public categories and procedures.
    "Every complaint about an officer is public." Many §832.7 categories require a sustained finding; firearm discharge and death/GBI force categories operate differently.
    "Copley means disciplinary records are always secret." Later SB1421/SB16/current §832.7 create explicit public categories notwithstanding older confidentiality rules.
    "Police can charge me for hours of BWC redaction." National Lawyers Guild rejects ordinary redaction labor as chargeable electronic data-extraction cost.
    "The same CCTV copy has the same result everywhere." Custodian, purpose, compilation into an investigation, privacy/security, and specific overrides can change the analysis.
    "I requested it, so it must be preserved forever." Disclosure and retention remain separate under City of Gilroy and the applicable retention law/policy.
