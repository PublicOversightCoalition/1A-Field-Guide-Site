    California Authority Sweep Ledger¶

    Purpose: Make the California Field Guide's completeness work reproducible and auditable.

    This ledger records source ranges that have actually been reviewed, what was retained, what was treated as specialized or outside the standing scenario, and what still remains unswept. A completed slice does not mean the entire California code has been completed.

    Classification labels¶

    • RETAINED — materially relevant to the standing peaceful/public-property audit scenario or to a recurring facility chapter.
    • CONTEXT — does not independently create the principal field rule but materially explains authority, definitions, procedure, or limits.
    • SPECIALIZED — relevant only to a distinct facility/event type and should be handled in that chapter.
    • N/A-STANDING-SCOPE — reviewed but ordinarily requires conduct excluded by the project's standing assumptions or concerns a setting outside the project's ordinary public-facility scope.
    • REVIEW-LATER — text identified, but case law or related provisions must be reviewed before final classification.

    For handbook/index status terminology, PRIMARY-TEXT-VERIFIED means current primary text was checked and an initial entry exists. It does not mean the controlling case-law layer is complete. DOCTRINE-REVIEWED is reserved for narrow propositions whose controlling doctrine/material case law have also been reviewed. DOCTRINE-REVIEWED-IN-PART means a narrow part of a broader statutory family has reached that stage while other applications remain unresolved.

    The canonical factual baseline for N/A-STANDING-SCOPE and related classifications is maintained in ../california/standing-scenario.md. It expressly excludes unstated fighting, unreasonable noise, obstruction/intimidation, independent § 148 conduct, unlawful-assembly predicates, restricted-area entry, and other unrelated offenses so a sweep does not smuggle hypothetical misconduct into the issue under review.

    Every retained authority should ultimately have a direct primary-source link, narrow proposition, important limitations, binding level where applicable, and last_verified date.

    Sweep 002 — Trespass and property-access family¶

    Review date: September 17, 2026
    Detailed ledger: SWEEP-002-TRESPASS-PROPERTY.md

    Primary-text scope completed: Penal Code §§ 601-603, including all subdivisions of § 602 and every current § 602.1-602.13 provision.

    The sweep produced the complete § 602 matrix and a practical removal decision tree in docs/california/trespass-removal.md.

    Final controlling-case review incorporated before merge¶

    The final PR #6 review promoted narrow propositions—not entire statutes—to doctrine-reviewed status:

    • In re Catalano, 29 Cal.3d 1 (1981) and Edgerly v. City & County of San Francisco, 599 F.3d 946 (9th Cir. 2010) — § 602(m) occupation requires nontransient, continuous possession; transient/minutes-long presence did not establish that element in Edgerly.
    • Parrish v. Municipal Court, 258 Cal.App.2d 497 (1968) — predecessor of § 602(q) is a public-building closing-hours rule; apparent-lawful-business inquiry is objective.
    • Dubner v. City & County of San Francisco, 266 F.3d 959 (9th Cir. 2001) — refusal alone does not supply § 602.1 intentional interference/obstruction facts.
    • Hamburg v. Wal-Mart Stores, Inc., 116 Cal.App.4th 497 (2004) — rule/policy enforcement and satisfaction of § 602.1 criminal elements remain separate; private-property facts limit its government-facility use.
    • People v. Hsiung (2026) — § 602(o) First Amendment discussion is in an expressly unpublished portion and is not relied on as precedent.

    The final refinement also distinguishes specific-area public access, forum classification, security screening, visitor sign-in/ID policy, and criminal trespass authority. Section 602(y) is not treated as a generic visitor-log/ID statute.

    Sweep 003 — California Public Records Act / evidence preservation¶

    Review date: September 17, 2026
    Detailed ledger: SWEEP-003-CPRA-EVIDENCE-PRESERVATION.md
    Handbook chapter: docs/california/public-records-evidence-preservation.md

    Scope: targeted operational/preservation review. This is not yet a complete exemption-by-exemption sweep of all Government Code Division 10.

    Core statutory layer reviewed¶

    • Gov. Code §§ 7920.530 and 7920.545 — public-record and writing definitions;
    • §§ 7921.000, 7921.010, 7921.300 — access, anti-evasion/lawful-destruction context, and purpose-neutral access;
    • § 7922.000 — justification for withholding;
    • §§ 7922.500-7922.605 — core request, inspection, copying, determination, denial, electronic-record, and duty-to-assist provisions;
    • §§ 7922.630-7922.640 — agency regulations/guidelines context;
    • §§ 7922.680, 7922.700-7922.725 — internet/enterprise-system provisions identified as contextual and queued for deeper relevance review;
    • § 7923.000 — enforcement/declaratory relief;
    • representative retention/destruction frameworks: Gov. Code §§ 12270-12279, 12275, 34090, 34090.6, 26202, 6200-6201.

    Controlling doctrine¶

    City of San Jose v. Superior Court, 2 Cal.5th 608 (2017)¶

    Retained propositions:

    • public-business writings do not cease being CPRA records merely because they are sent, received, or stored using personal accounts/devices;
    • public-record status depends on substantive relationship to public business, with content/context/purpose/audience/scope-of-employment factors;
    • primarily personal material with only incidental public-business references generally is not transformed into a public record;
    • agencies must make reasonable efforts to locate responsive material;
    • for likely personal-account/device records, agencies should communicate the request to employees and may reasonably rely on them to search their personal files/accounts/devices for responsive public-business records.

    Auditor consequence: a personal-phone recording deliberately made on duty to document an official encounter is a strong public-record candidate, but the guide does not use the word always without the substantive-public-business analysis.

    City of Gilroy v. Superior Court, 19 Cal.5th 38 (2026)¶

    Retained propositions:

    • the CPRA itself does not impose a three-year preservation duty on all responsive records withheld as exempt;
    • the CPRA is not a general retention statute;
    • other retention/preservation law can independently apply;
    • declaratory relief can remain available for an ongoing rights/obligations dispute likely to affect future requests/conduct;
    • footnote 20 limits the no-CPRA-preservation holding to normal implementation/operation of routine retention practices and expressly leaves intentional destruction reflecting possible deliberate misconduct unresolved.

    Auditor consequence: a preservation notice is useful evidence, but a pending CPRA request or statement of intent to request a record is not represented as an automatic CPRA litigation hold.

    Key Sweep 003 field distinctions¶

    1. Public record ≠ automatically disclosable. Exemptions/redactions remain separate.
    2. Personal device ≠ private-record safe harbor. City of San Jose controls the categorical question.
    3. Search ≠ disclosure. An agency may need to search a personal device/account for qualifying records even if some responsive material is ultimately withheld.
    4. Disclosure ≠ retention. Gilroy separates CPRA access from retention law.
    5. Preservation notice ≠ magic legal hold. It still documents existence, knowledge, notice, and potentially intent.
    6. Routine lawful disposition ≠ deliberate destruction. The latter is expressly outside Gilroy footnote 20's resolved scenario.
    7. No universal retention period. State executive agencies, cities, counties, special districts, law enforcement, courts, and other bodies can have different statutes/schedules.
    8. Request the schedule. If evidence disappears, the governing retention/destruction policy becomes part of the factual/legal record.
    9. Do not declare a felony on scene. Government Code §§ 6200-6201 have their own record/custody/willfulness scope and must not be equated automatically with the CPRA definition.

    Practical output¶

    The new chapter includes an auditor workflow for employee records me back:

    • establish on camera that a recording exists and its work-related context;
    • give a narrow preservation notice;
    • promptly submit a CPRA request including qualifying personal-device/account records;
    • invoke the City of San Jose search principle without demanding access to unrelated private content;
    • if withheld, demand the statutory withholding basis and segregable portions;
    • if missing/deleted, request retention schedules/policies, disposition/deletion records, metadata/audit logs if maintained, and communications about preservation/deletion;
    • analyze any destruction under the actual retention, litigation-preservation, and destruction law rather than asserting an automatic CPRA violation.

    Sweep 004 — Police Encounters: Fourth Amendment + Self-Incrimination¶

    Review date: September 21, 2026
    Detailed ledger: SWEEP-004-POLICE-ENCOUNTERS.md
    Handbook chapter: docs/california/police-encounters.md

    Scope: targeted operational/doctrinal review of the encounter sequence that commonly follows protected recording or other First Amendment activity. This is not a complete Fourth Amendment, Fifth Amendment, criminal-procedure, or remedies sweep.

    Core encounter layer reviewed¶

    • consensual police contact versus Fourth Amendment seizure;
    • Terry investigative detention and reasonable suspicion;
    • the requirement that the stop be justified at inception;
    • detention scope/duration baseline under Sharpe and traffic-specific Rodriguez;
    • protective frisk as a separate armed-and-dangerous inquiry;
    • probable-cause arrest and Devenpeck's objective-offense rule;
    • California identification distinctions: carry-ID versus request versus statutory/case-law duty versus false identification;
    • California Vehicle Code § 2806.5 pedestrian-stop notice relevant to facility audits, distinct from full reasonable-suspicion disclosure; the statute's traffic-stop branch is not developed in the current project scope;
    • Fourth Amendment detention versus Fifth Amendment/Miranda custody;
    • clear invocation of the privilege/right to remain silent under Berghuis and the plurality-limited Salinas discussion;
    • physical device seizure versus digital-content search; Riley warrant baseline;
    • explicit unresolved treatment of compelled unlocking/passcodes/biometrics.

    Principal authorities incorporated¶

    Federal controlling authorities include Terry v. Ohio, Berkemer v. McCarty, United States v. Sharpe, Hiibel v. Sixth Judicial District Court, Devenpeck v. Alford, Berghuis v. Thompkins, Salinas v. Texas (plurality-limited proposition), Riley v. California, and traffic-specific Rodriguez v. United States.

    California/Ninth Circuit anchors include:

    • People v. Tacardon, 14 Cal.5th 235 (2022) — totality-of-circumstances consensual-contact/seizure analysis; directed police attention does not itself establish a detention;
    • People v. Flores, 15 Cal.5th 1032 (2024) — particularized/objective reasonable suspicion required; high-crime-area context and the conduct there were insufficient in totality;
    • United States v. Landeros, 913 F.3d 862 (9th Cir. 2019) — passenger refusal to identify, without reasonable suspicion of an offense, did not supply reasonable suspicion and did not justify prolonging the traffic stop;
    • People v. Valle, A169080 (Cal. Ct. App. Sept. 18, 2024) — published California authority recognizing Vehicle Code § 2806.5's stop-reason notice requirement while keeping the federal objective Fourth Amendment justification inquiry separate.

    California identification findings¶

    Primary text reviewed:

    • Penal Code § 148.9 — false/fictitious identity during qualifying lawful detention/arrest;
    • Penal Code § 853.5 — identity/thumbprint procedures in specified infraction-arrest citation context;
    • Vehicle Code § 2806.5 — for current project purposes, a pedestrian stop requires the reason to be stated before covered questioning, subject to the statute's imminent-threat exception;
    • Vehicle Code § 12951 — driver-specific license possession/presentation rule;
    • Vehicle Code § 40302 — specified custody/magistrate rules following nonfelony Vehicle Code arrest.

    Bounded research conclusion: this targeted sweep has not identified a current general California pedestrian carry-ID statute or general statutory stop-and-identify law equivalent to the Nevada statute upheld in Hiibel. That does not mean identification or production of possessed identification can never be required. Older published California identification-production/search cases, including People v. Long, People v. Loudermilk, and People v. Garcia, still require a dedicated reconciliation, and specialized statutory contexts remain independently controlling.

    Historical context: Kolender v. Lawson, 461 U.S. 352 (1983), invalidated California's former requirement that a detained person provide credible and reliable identification because the scheme was unconstitutionally vague. The history is useful, but the guide does not turn Kolender into a blanket rule against all modern California identification requirements.

    Key Sweep 004 field distinctions¶

    1. Police contact ≠ detention. A consensual encounter requires no reasonable suspicion.
    2. Detention ≠ arrest. Terry permits a limited seizure based on reasonable suspicion.
    3. Detention ≠ frisk. A frisk requires its own armed-and-dangerous safety justification.
    4. Detention ≠ Miranda custody. A person can be seized for Fourth Amendment purposes without yet being in Miranda custody.
    5. Reasonable suspicion ≠ probable cause. Arrest ordinarily requires the higher threshold.
    6. Recording alone ≠ obstruction/RS/PC. Penal Code § 148(g) expressly supplies this bridge within its scope.
    7. Asking for ID ≠ proof of a general carry-ID or stop-and-identify law. Identify the actual California statute, context, and applicable case law before treating the request as compulsory.
    8. No general duty found ≠ permission to lie. Penal Code § 148.9 separately prohibits qualifying false/fictitious identification.
    9. Hiibel ≠ national stop-and-ID mandate. It upheld Nevada's statute during a valid Terry stop.
    10. Reason for stop ≠ full RAS recital. For pedestrian stops within this project's current scope, Vehicle Code § 2806.5 requires the reason for the stop before covered questioning, subject to its exception; reasonable suspicion itself must still exist when the seizure begins.
    11. Officer's announced offense ≠ entire arrest analysis. Devenpeck makes probable cause objective.
    12. Phone seizure ≠ digital search. Riley protects digital contents beyond ordinary search-incident-to-arrest authority.
    13. No Miranda warning ≠ illegal arrest. Arrest/seizure legality and custodial-interrogation safeguards are separate questions.
    14. Silence is not the safest invocation method. The guide recommends clear invocation rather than relying on an inference from silence.

    Remaining Sweep 004 research¶

    Important unresolved areas include:

    • handcuffs, drawn weapons, prone positioning, transport, and when a Terry stop becomes an arrest;
    • anonymous/911/security/employee reports and informant reliability;
    • collective knowledge and mistakes of law/fact;
    • older California identification-production/search cases, including Long, Loudermilk, Garcia, and later applications;
    • plain-feel/container doctrine and consent searches;
    • full Miranda custody/interrogation/public-safety/waiver/reinitiation doctrine;
    • physical seizure/retention of phones and cameras;
    • warrant scope/particularity for devices;
    • compelled unlocking/passcodes/biometrics;
    • independent California Constitution article I §§ 13 and 15 doctrine;
    • excessive force, suppression, § 1983, qualified immunity, Bane Act, and municipal liability.

    Those issues remain explicitly queued rather than converted into field slogans.

    Sweep 005 — Public-forum doctrine¶

    Review date: September 17, 2026
    Detailed ledger: SWEEP-005-PUBLIC-FORUM-DOCTRINE.md
    Handbook chapter: docs/california/public-forum-doctrine.md

    Scope: core federal forum architecture, Ninth Circuit government-building/application doctrine, California Speech Clause overlay, and an area-level registry methodology. This is not a facility-by-facility classification sweep.

    Core federal layer reviewed¶

    • Perry Education Association v. Perry Local Educators' Association — traditional/designated/nonpublic architecture;
    • Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Defense & Educational Fund — define the forum by the access sought; intentional opening; nonpublic reasonableness/viewpoint neutrality;
    • United States v. Grace and United States v. Kokinda — ordinary perimeter sidewalk versus special-purpose walkway distinctions;
    • Ward v. Rock Against Racism — public-forum content-neutral time/place/manner test;
    • International Society for Krishna Consciousness v. Lee — airport-terminal/separated-area context;
    • Rosenberger — viewpoint-discrimination principle;
    • Christian Legal Society / Wright / Hopper — limited-forum doctrine and terminology;
    • Minnesota Voters Alliance v. Mansky — a nonpublic forum still requires a reasonable, workable speech rule.

    Ninth Circuit California-facing layer¶

    Sammartano v. First Judicial District Court, 303 F.3d 959 (9th Cir. 2002)¶

    Retained propositions:

    • the judicial/municipal complex before the court was a nonpublic forum even though people entered it to conduct public business;
    • location, purpose, and governmental intent matter;
    • nonpublic-forum restrictions still must be reasonable in light of forum purpose and viewpoint neutral;
    • the government did not obtain a free pass merely from the nonpublic classification: the evidentiary connection between the restriction and asserted safety/order needs still mattered.

    Norse v. City of Santa Cruz, 629 F.3d 966 (9th Cir. 2010) (en banc)¶

    Retained propositions for the meeting crossover:

    • a city-council meeting opened to public participation is a limited public forum;
    • reasonable content/topic and time rules may apply, but viewpoint discrimination is not permitted;
    • the Ninth Circuit rejected a theory that First Amendment protection disappears after the public-comment period;
    • meeting removal doctrine remains tied to actual disruption rather than merely disfavored expression.

    The Brown Act statutory layer remains separately governed by public-meetings.md.

    Camenzind v. California Exposition & State Fair, 84 F.4th 1102 (9th Cir. 2023)¶

    Retained propositions:

    • analyze physically/policy-distinct areas separately;
    • the enclosed/ticketed private-event portion was nonpublic on the facts;
    • the exterior area was a public forum under the California Speech Clause;
    • California's forum analysis differs from the federal one;
    • the panel acknowledged it was predicting unresolved California law for the precise state-constitutional issue, and a partial dissent disagreed with the majority's state-law analysis for the interior.

    California Speech Clause¶

    • California Constitution art. I, § 2(a) is separately analyzed.
    • San Leandro Teachers Association v. Governing Board, 46 Cal.4th 822 (2009), classified internal school mailboxes as nonpublic under federal doctrine and separately rejected a broader California Speech Clause claim on the narrow facts. It also supplies an important warning against treating the older basic incompatibility formulation as the current California forum-classification rule.
    • International Society for Krishna Consciousness of California, Inc. v. City of Los Angeles, 48 Cal.4th 446 (2010), expressly declined to decide whether LAX was a public forum under the California Speech Clause; the challenged immediate-funds solicitation rule was valid even on an assumed-public-forum analysis.
    • Pruneyard, Ralphs Grocery, and Park Management are retained as context showing that California's Speech Clause can extend beyond federal public-forum doctrine and that location/access analysis is fact-sensitive. They are not converted into a universal rule that every public-facing property is a public forum.
    • Camenzind confirms that the old basic incompatibility classification approach used by some older Ninth Circuit cases rests on a doubtful foundation after San Leandro; the guide therefore does not use it as the current state-law classification test.

    Key Sweep 005 field distinctions¶

    1. Public ownership ≠ public forum.
    2. Open to the public ≠ public forum.
    3. Classify the specific area/channel, not merely the building.
    4. Traditional/designated public forum ≠ limited/nonpublic forum. The governing restriction standard changes.
    5. Nonpublic ≠ no First Amendment rights. Reasonableness and viewpoint neutrality remain.
    6. Limited forum ≠ unrestricted forum. Government can confine speech to the forum's lawful purpose but cannot use the limit as a pretext for viewpoint discrimination.
    7. Federal classification ≠ automatic California classification.
    8. Forum status ≠ recording permission/prohibition. Separate recording, privacy, security, statutory, and facility rules may matter.
    9. Forum status ≠ criminal trespass. Constitutional restriction, exclusion authority, detention, and Penal Code elements remain separate questions.
    10. Unresolved is a legitimate registry result. Do not infer forum status from ownership, a public counter, or a sign.

    Registry consequence¶

    Future facility records should contain, at minimum: facility, specific area, access status, federal forum classification, California Speech Clause classification, expressive activity analyzed, governing rule/policy, authority, confidence, and last-verified date.

    The registry should use UNRESOLVED whenever authority is inadequate.

    Remaining facility work¶

    Still separately queued: police/sheriff lobbies, administrative-service facilities beyond the Concord DMV pilot, federal post-office/federal-premises facility work, public-library branches and systems beyond the initial Contra Costa pilot sites, courthouses and courtrooms, colleges/universities, K-12, transit property, public health facilities, digital forums, employee workspaces, and long-term exclusion orders.

    Sweep 006 — Contra Costa County Public Libraries¶

    Review date: September 21, 2026
    Detailed ledger: SWEEP-006-CONTRA-COSTA-LIBRARIES.md
    Handbook chapter: docs/california/public-libraries.md

    Scope: targeted system-level facility-category sweep of the Contra Costa County Library. This is not a statewide survey of every California library system and does not assign a Registry status to the system or any branch.

    Library/forum doctrine retained¶

    Prigmore v. City of Redding, 211 Cal.App.4th 1322 (2012)¶

    Retained propositions:

    • published California Court of Appeal authority involving the outdoor grounds of a municipal public library;
    • forum defined by the access sought rather than the whole library campus;
    • on the record, the public open space at the entry, entry/exit-door area, and adjacent parking lot were a traditional public forum;
    • classification depended on actual use, unrestricted access, physical/congregation characteristics, surrounding public spaces, and library function;
    • interior and exterior were expressly distinguished;
    • the older California "basic incompatibility" formulation was not used as the forum-classification test;
    • most challenged outdoor speech-zone/reservation restrictions were likely invalid at the preliminary-injunction stage, while the parking-lot vehicle-leafleting ban was upheld on its safety rationale.

    Scope limit: do not convert the Redding property-specific holding into a universal rule that every library plaza, approach, or parking lot is automatically a traditional public forum. Footnote 11's broad interior observation is not treated as a categorical interior holding because the cited Faith Center forum was specifically the Antioch meeting room.

    Faith Center Church Evangelistic Ministries v. Glover, 480 F.3d 891 (9th Cir. 2007)¶

    Retained propositions:

    • the forum before the Ninth Circuit was the Antioch Library meeting room, not the entire library system or building;

    Sweep 018 — Law-Enforcement Records and Specialized Evidence Disclosure¶

    Research date: September 29, 2026
    Detailed ledger: SWEEP-018-LAW-ENFORCEMENT-RECORDS-DISCLOSURE.md
    Qualification package: GAP-08

    Key retained results:

    • current Government Code §7923.600 is the recodified law-enforcement investigatory-record exemption; §7923.630 preserves the construction/relationship of former §6254(f);
    • Williams preserves a broad investigatory-file exemption that does not automatically expire when an investigation ends, while mandatory statutory information still must be disclosed;

    • ACLU Foundation and Castañares preserve the distinction between targeted suspected-law-violation investigation and bulk/routine/noncriminal-assistance collection;

    • §§7923.610 and 7923.615 require specified arrestee and complaint/request/response information even where the complete underlying investigatory record remains exempt;
    • §7923.625 specifically governs audio/video depicting defined critical incidents (officer firearm discharge at a person or officer force causing death/GBI), with its own active-investigation delay, reassessment, privacy/redaction, and subject-access structure;
    • Sacramento Television Stations treats §7923.625 as a specific legislative limit on the older investigatory-record regime for qualifying recordings and requires the actual statutory delay showing;
    • not every BWC recording is a critical-incident recording; ordinary detention/trespass footage can remain under other CPRA rules;
    • current Penal Code §832.7(b), as amended effective 2026, expressly makes enumerated officer records public notwithstanding ordinary personnel confidentiality and Government Code §7923.600, subject to its current redaction/delay rules;
    • current public §832.7 categories include firearm discharge, death/GBI force, specified sustained excessive-force/failure-to-intervene, sexual-assault, dishonesty, prejudice/discrimination, unlawful-arrest/search findings, and the statutory prohibited-agreement category;
    • §832.8's definition of a sustained finding matters where the public category depends on a sustained finding;
    • Copley Press remains historical confidentiality context, but later SB1421/SB16/current §832.7 create explicit public categories;
    • Becerra and First Amendment Coalition confirm the later §832.7 disclosure command and the need to harmonize rather than blindly apply older secrecy rules;

    • Penal Code §832.18 principally addresses BWC policy/retention rather than public disclosure;
    • National Lawyers Guild v. City of Hayward prevents shifting ordinary BWC redaction labor to requesters as electronic "data extraction";
    • disclosure remains distinct from retention under City of Gilroy and record-specific retention statutes/schedules.
