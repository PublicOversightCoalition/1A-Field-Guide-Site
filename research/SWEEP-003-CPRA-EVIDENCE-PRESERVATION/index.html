    Sweep 003 — California Public Records Act / Evidence Preservation¶

    Date reviewed: September 17, 2026
    Status: Historical core operational CPRA/preservation sweep; later Sweep 018 completes the ordinary law-enforcement evidence-disclosure branch while specialized exemptions/retention remain separate.

    Defined scope¶

    Sweep 003 is a targeted doctrinal and operational sweep, not yet a section-by-section completeness claim for all of Government Code Division 10.

    The completed scope covers:

    1. the CPRA definition of public record and writing;
    2. core access/withholding principles;
    3. the request, inspection, copying, electronic-record, timeline, denial, and assistance provisions needed for an auditor to obtain incident evidence;
    4. personal-account/device doctrine under City of San Jose v. Superior Court, 2 Cal.5th 608 (2017);
    5. preservation/retention doctrine under City of Gilroy v. Superior Court, 19 Cal.5th 38 (2026);
    6. representative state/city/county retention frameworks needed to establish that retention is agency- and record-specific;
    7. Government Code §§ 6200-6201 as potential destruction authorities, with their applicability deliberately kept separate from the CPRA's broader disclosure definition;
    8. a practical workflow for work-related recordings made by public employees on personal devices.

    This sweep does not yet claim complete review of:

    • every exemption in Division 10 Parts 5 and 6;
    • every law-enforcement records rule;
    • every retention statute for every type of state/local agency;
    • all spoliation/discovery sanctions;
    • all CPRA fee/attorney-fee litigation doctrine.

    Core statutory authorities reviewed¶

    Definitions and access¶

    Gov. Code § 7920.545 RETAINED Writing includes photographs, pictures, sounds, electronic transmissions, and other recorded information regardless of storage method.

    Enforcement¶

    Authority Classification Initial proposition
    § 7923.000 RETAINED / DOCTRINE-REVIEWED-IN-PART Injunctive/declaratory relief and mandate; City of Gilroy confirms declaratory relief can remain available for recurring disputed practices even after existing records have been produced/destroyed.

    Controlling-case findings¶

    City of San Jose v. Superior Court, 2 Cal.5th 608 (2017)¶

    Binding level: California Supreme Court.

    Holdings/propositions retained¶

    1. A public employee's writings about public business do not fall outside CPRA merely because they are sent, received, or stored in a personal account.
    2. Storage location/device ownership is not dispositive of public-record status.
    3. At minimum, the writing must relate in some substantive way to the conduct of the public's business.
    4. Relevant factors include content, context/purpose, audience, and whether the employee acted or purported to act within the scope of employment.
    5. Primarily personal communications with only incidental public-business references generally are not public records.
    6. Agencies must make reasonable efforts to locate responsive records; searches need only be reasonably calculated to locate responsive documents and need not be extraordinarily intrusive.
    7. When responsive public records may be held in employee personal accounts/devices, an agency should communicate the request to the employees and may reasonably rely on them to search their personal files/accounts/devices for responsive public-business material.

    Auditor-specific consequence¶

    An employee-created personal-phone recording made on duty specifically to document an official encounter is a strong public-record candidate, because the medium is a CPRA writing and the content/context/purpose/scope factors point toward public business. The guide does not state that every recording by every public employee is automatically a public record.

    City of Gilroy v. Superior Court, 19 Cal.5th 38 (2026)¶

    Binding level: California Supreme Court.
    Filed: January 15, 2026.

    Holdings/propositions retained¶

    1. The CPRA itself does not impose a duty to preserve for three years all responsive records withheld as exempt.
    2. CPRA contains no general records-retention requirement; retention/preservation duties arise from other law where applicable.
    3. Government Code § 7921.010(c)'s recognition of destruction pursuant to law reinforces the distinction between disclosure and retention.
    4. Declaratory relief under § 7923.000 remains available at least where it would resolve an ongoing rights/obligations dispute likely to affect future requests or future conduct.
    5. The Court recognized separate litigation-preservation/spoliation law when litigation is reasonably foreseeable rather than inferring that duty from CPRA.

    Footnote 20 limitation¶

    The Court expressly limited its no-CPRA-preservation holding to destruction connected with the normal adoption, implementation, and operation of routine records-retention practices/policies. It stated that it had no occasion to address intentional destruction in other contexts that may reflect deliberate misconduct.

    Auditor-specific consequence¶

    A requester must not claim that saying I will request that video or filing a CPRA request automatically freezes the record under CPRA. A preservation notice is still valuable evidence of existence, knowledge, notice, and intent, but the legal preservation duty must be located in the applicable retention law, schedule, litigation-preservation doctrine, or another statute.

    Retention/destruction authorities reviewed¶

    State executive agencies¶

    The California State Archives identifies the State Records Management Act, Government Code §§ 12270-12279, and approved records-retention schedules as the state executive-agency framework. State Administrative Manual Chapter 1600 requires agency retention-schedule programs. State guidance notes that an unscheduled state record is not simply disposable at will and quotes § 12275's disposition restriction.

    Classification: RETAINED / CONTEXT.

    Cities¶

    Gov. Code § 34090¶

    City-record destruction mechanism subject to legislative-body resolution, city-attorney consent, and statutory limitations/exceptions.

    Classification: RETAINED / SPECIALIZED BY AGENCY TYPE.

    Gov. Code § 34090.6¶

    Specialized rules for specified routine video monitoring and telephone/radio communications, with stated periods and conditions. It is not treated as a universal retention rule for ad hoc employee phone video.

    Classification: SPECIALIZED.

    Counties — Gov. Code § 26202¶

    County record-destruction authority under specified conditions; must be read with more specific law and the county's adopted schedule.

    Classification: RETAINED / SPECIALIZED BY AGENCY TYPE.

    Gov. Code §§ 6200-6201¶

    Criminal provisions concerning specified records/documents and willful removal, destruction, alteration, etc.

    Classification: RETAINED / DOCTRINE-REVIEWED-IN-PART.

    Published California authority includes People v. Sperl, 54 Cal.App.3d 640 (1976), which applied § 6200 to official county radio logs and rejected the claim that such a record was freely disposable merely because no statute expressly required its creation or retention. Related published authority includes People v. Pearson and Loder v. Municipal Court.

    Important limitation: The guide does not equate the CPRA's broad public-record definition with the elements/scope of §§ 6200-6201. Those criminal provisions still have their own covered-record, custody, authorization, willfulness, and—under § 6200—officer having custody requirements. Their application to ad hoc personal-device recordings remains unresolved.

    Key Sweep 003 discoveries¶

    1. Personal device is not a safe harbor. City of San Jose rejects categorical exclusion based on a personal account/device.
    2. Content/context control public-record status. Employment alone does not make every private item public.
    3. Search and disclosure are different. The agency may have a duty to search for a personal-device public record even though an exemption may ultimately permit withholding/redaction.
    4. Disclosure and retention are different. The CPRA is not a universal retention statute.
    5. A pending request is not an automatic CPRA litigation hold. Gilroy squarely rejects the proposed three-year CPRA preservation rule.
    6. Gilroy does not bless deliberate destruction. Its express footnote 20 limitation leaves intentional/destructive-misconduct contexts unresolved by that holding.
    7. Retention periods are not universal. State executive, city, county, special-district, law-enforcement, court, school, and other records can fall under different statutes and schedules.
    8. Request the retention schedule. If a record disappears, the applicable schedule/policy becomes evidence and part of the legal analysis.
    9. A preservation notice remains useful even without automatic legal effect. It identifies the record and can establish knowledge/notice before later disposition.
    10. The safest field statement is narrow: I intend to request that work-related recording; please preserve the original. Do not announce a felony or automatic CPRA violation based solely on later deletion.

    Later research and remaining specialized queue¶

    Sweep 003 intentionally left law-enforcement investigative disclosure, BWC disclosure, and Penal Code §832.7 to a later package. Sweep 018 (September 29, 2026) now completes the ordinary incident/dispatch/BWC/CCTV/peace-officer-record branch.

    Remaining specialized work includes:

    • exemption families in CPRA Division 10 beyond the ordinary field branch;
    • juvenile, medical, coroner, victim-specific, database/surveillance, and other specialized confidentiality statutes;
    • complete state/city/county/special-district retention families relevant to particular facilities;
    • complete personal-device and technical-custody application of Gov. Code §§6200-6201;
    • current litigation-hold/spoliation rules and sanctions where incidents foreseeably become litigation;
    • attorney-fee/enforcement procedure; and
    • local retention schedules and CPRA guidelines where those policies materially affect evidence preservation.
